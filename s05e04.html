Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 5, Episode 4

Rumpole and the Tap End

Featherstone's controversial remarks and ruling in a domestic violence case cause women's-rights fallout and Lord Chancellor concern.

Wednesday, 14 December 1988 · ITV (Thames Television)

The episode opens on a violent domestic row at the Timsons' house: Tony Timson is arrested that same night and charged with the attempted murder of his wife April, whom he allegedly tried to drown while the two of them were sharing a bath. In the cells, Rumpole hears Tony's actual account: the couple routinely bathe together, with Tony traditionally seated at the uncomfortable "tap end" so he can rinse April's hair, and the fight broke out only after April, needling him about an invitation to a party where her old flame Peter "Peanuts" Malloy would be present, made a cutting comparison between Tony and Peanuts on the subject of virility, a detail Tony is mortified to repeat in front of Rumpole's pupil, Liz Probert. Rumpole explains to Liz, pointedly, that the Timson and Malloy families have feuded for generations, going back to disputes over stolen sheep, and resolves to seek a quick, pragmatic resolution rather than fight a week-long attempted murder trial.

In the judge's room before the case opens, Rumpole finds a sympathetic audience in Mr. Justice Guthrie Featherstone, an old chambers colleague anxious to be free by lunchtime for a royal garden party at Buckingham Palace. Rumpole persuades both Featherstone and prosecuting counsel Charles Hearthstoke (a modernising young barrister with a taste for computerized case-prep, and briefly, Liz Probert's romantic interest) to let Tony plead down to common assault, on the footing that his wife's wounding remark about his "comparison" with Peanuts Malloy amounted to real, if purely domestic, provocation. In court, Featherstone obligingly muses aloud, on the record, that April habitually made Tony sit at the tap end while she washed her hair in "the more placid waters" at the other end, and binds Tony over to keep the peace for a year in the sum of 50 pounds, a resolution Liz Probert erupts at outside court, denouncing it as "a famous victory for men" and storming off to organise a national protest through her branch of the women's movement.

The judge's remarks, reported out of context in the Evening Standard under the headline "Tap-end judge's amazing decision," detonate into a full-blown media scandal: Featherstone is accused of suggesting that a wife's choice of bathing position excuses her husband's attempted murder of her, feminist groups demand his removal from the bench, and his wife Marigold is mortified by a newspaper caller asking which end of the bath she herself prefers. At home, Hilda (newly outraged on behalf of "the women of England") announces that she intends to start reading for the Bar through the Open University, to make sure women "get a bit of justice in future," alarming both Rumpole and clerk Henry, who is horrified at the prospect of one day clerking for Mrs. Hilda Rumpole. Meanwhile, in chambers, Sam Ballard QC and Claude Erskine Brown are quietly delighted by Featherstone's disgrace, scenting a possible High Court vacancy that an old contact named Keith in the Lord Chancellor's office has hinted might go to "someone from the same stable," meaning Ballard himself.

Summoned before the Lord Chancellor to answer for what is diagnosed, half in jest, as a judicial case of "Judge-itis" (pomposity and ill-advised remarks from the bench), Featherstone is nearly eased into early retirement over his handling of the Timson case, but is granted a reprieve on the understanding that he may yet have a chance to "make his peace with the women of England" by righting matters in some future case. That chance arrives sooner than expected: Tony Timson, released after his first trial, is arrested again, this time for an armed robbery at a local off-licence, in which the proprietor, Mrs. Ruby Churchill, was coshed and left bleeding while an accomplice emptied the till, and Tony himself was found half-stunned nearby with the cosh and some of the stolen cash. Detective Inspector Brush testifies that Tony said "you got me this time then" on arrest, a remark Rumpole suggests the inspector has conveniently trimmed of its original, far less incriminating wording, "I suppose you think you got me this time then."

Rumpole's cross-examination establishes a web of motive and opportunity around the case: Tony's neighbour Chrissie had tipped him off that April was due to meet Peanuts Malloy at the off-licence at half past nine that night, prompting Tony to go round and confront them, walking straight into an ambush. The police forensic witness, Miss Sweeting, concedes under pressure that the bloodstains found on the shop counter are equally consistent with Tony having been struck on the head by an assailant as with him having slipped and hit his head while fleeing, undermining the prosecution's confident narrative. Rumpole also presses Inspector Brush into confirming that Peanuts Malloy and his brother Greg, well known to police as violent operators, have a history of using weapons exactly like the cosh in question, planting in the jury's mind the idea that Tony, cleared once already on a technicality everyone found embarrassing, may now have been deliberately framed by Peanuts as both revenge and a way of clearing the field for his own pursuit of April.

Away from court, Rumpole discovers a piece of leverage he is careful never to use improperly but is happy to let Featherstone know he possesses: the judge has been quietly sending gifts, including a pot plant with pink blossoms, to the attractive court shorthand writer, Miss Lorraine Frinton, who "corrected" the misplaced full stop in his original Timson judgment that had caused all the press trouble in the first place. Rumpole uses this knowledge not for blackmail but simply to remind Featherstone, under pressure from the Lord Chancellor's office to convict Tony hard this time and restore his reputation with the women of England, that a judge who accepts small courtesies himself is poorly placed to lecture anyone else about the proprieties, and that real courage means summing up according to the evidence regardless of which way the political wind is blowing.

Decisively, Rumpole calls April Timson herself (legally a "non-compellable" witness against her own husband, a point freshly drilled into Hilda by her Open University criminal law lectures, which Rumpole overhears and promptly repurposes) and April volunteers the truth: she invented the original attempted-murder accusation out of anger after the bathroom row, Tony never laid a dangerous hand on her, and after his first trial, Peanuts Malloy told her he intended to see Tony "get a bit of justice" by having him sent away properly this time, using a cosh identical to the one he habitually kept in his car, a weapon April knows for a fact Tony would never own or know how to use. Rumpole's closing speech to the jury draws a vivid, sympathetic picture of a gentle, hen-pecked man unjustly cast as both a wife-drowning chauvinist and a violent armed robber, and the jury acquits Tony of the robbery charge as well.

Mr. Justice Featherstone, having found the nerve to resist the pressure to convict regardless of the evidence, is rewarded rather than punished: the press, ever fickle, now runs a headline declaring "Bathtub judge proved right," praising his wisdom and integrity for refusing to bow to pressure groups, and Featherstone, delighted, speculates to Marigold over the soup about a possible further promotion to the Court of Appeal.

The episode closes at the Rumpoles' house, where Hilda, somewhat deflated now that April's own confession has undercut the feminist cause she had taken up, abruptly abandons her plan to read for the Bar after all, declaring she has "better things to do": namely keeping Rumpole in order and making sure he does the washing-up properly, and declares herself quietly scandalized, on reflection, by the whole idea of married couples sharing a bath in the first place. Rumpole, settling back into his chair, promises faithfully that he will never ask her to join him in one, bringing the episode's domestic comedy to rest exactly where it began, in the gentle, permanent standoff between Horace Rumpole and She Who Must Be Obeyed.
