Rumpole of the Bailey

S06E03: Rumpole and the Right to Silence

A radical professor accused of murder refuses to disclose his alibi; Ballard and Erskine-Brown learn domestic lessons on the right to silence.

Monday, 11 November 1991 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 11 November 1991 (Series 6), directed by Julian Amyes, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).

The episode opens at a graduation ceremony at Gunster University, a modern concrete campus Rumpole finds entirely without dreaming spires, where Hilda's niece (or similarly close relation) Audrey Wiston is collecting a first-class degree in English. Mingling afterward, Rumpole falls into conversation with two of the English faculty, the old-fashioned classicist Professor Martin Wayfield and the fashionably left-wing Professor Clive Climpton, about the university's creeping commercialization under its money-minded vice-chancellor, Hayden Charles, and its wealthy chancellor, the frozen-food magnate Sir Dennis Tolson. Rumpole draws a parallel to his own profession's troubles, lamenting that the same business-efficiency logic now threatens trial by jury and the right to silence -- the accused's right to make the prosecution prove its case without having to answer any questions. He is introduced in passing to Hayden Charles and to Charles's glamorous wife, Mercy, a former model.

Back in chambers, a parallel theme of marital secrecy opens up through Sam Ballard QC, who corners Rumpole late one evening, carrying a mysterious tartan zip bag he refuses to explain, to ask Rumpole's opinion on whether a husband is entitled to keep some things to himself. His wife Marguerite -- known to everyone in chambers as "Matey" from her days as the Old Bailey's matron -- has noticed Ballard slipping off with the bag and not bringing it home, and Rumpole, firmly defending the right to marital privacy, advises Ballard that a little well-kept secret is no bad thing. Ballard, in return, asks the Rumpoles to have Matey to dinner, since she is lonely and in need of company; Rumpole agrees, mostly out of curiosity about what is actually in the bag.

That dinner invitation is overtaken by news that reaches the Rumpoles' house the same evening: Audrey Wiston arrives in distress to report that Professor Climpton has been arrested, and that Hayden Charles has died after falling through a banister and down a marble staircase at his own house. Audrey tells Rumpole that Climpton specifically wants him as counsel because he believes Rumpole "understands about keeping silent." Visiting Climpton in custody, Rumpole lays out, essentially as a dare, his own reconstruction of what the prosecution will argue: that Charles discovered his wife Mercy's affair with Climpton, confronted him, that a scuffle developed on the stairs, and that Climpton, the stronger man, took Charles by the throat and pushed him to his death before fleeing into the night. Climpton refuses to confirm or deny any of it, or to say where he actually was that evening, invoking his right to silence over Rumpole's objections that the jury may draw their own conclusions from an unexplained alibi.

The housekeeper Mrs O'Leary's statement, which Liz Probert reads aloud to Climpton, becomes the Crown's central evidence: she heard a man's voice on the stairs around ten o'clock shouting something about "licking the chancellor's boots" -- a phrase she is certain was Climpton's, because she had heard him use that very insult about Hayden Charles earlier that same afternoon over sandwiches at the graduation reception. Rumpole also learns, visiting Mercy Charles at the house to reconstruct the fatal fall (to her visible amusement at how long his "inspection of the scene of the crime" is taking), that she is willing to simply tell Rumpole that Climpton was with her that evening if that is the alibi he wants -- an offer Rumpole is uneasy about accepting at face value, since it would be a convenient lie rather than necessarily the truth.

A separate thread of comic misdirection runs through Claude Erskine Brown's chambers, whose wife Phillida has returned from a corruption case in Hong Kong to find two programmes for Tristan and Isolde on the kitchen table -- evidence that Erskine Brown took someone other than her to the opera. The someone was in fact Liz Probert, with whom he had gone, entirely innocently by his own account, to discuss the future of chambers during the interval; rather than admit this, he has told Phillida he took the elderly, Wagner-loving Uncle Tom instead, and now needs Rumpole to coach Uncle Tom into backing up the story. Rumpole refuses to coach anyone into perjury and instead recommends Erskine Brown simply claim his own right to silence, "like everyone else seems to be doing."

At a dinner at the Rumpoles' house, Matey Ballard, pressing Hilda on the supposed virtue of total marital openness, mentions in passing that her late uncle, once an estate agent in Gunster, was frozen out of local business because he refused to join a secretive, Freemasonry-like body called the Ancient Order of Oslers, complete with leather aprons, gilded horseshoes, and a peculiar handshake. The detail lodges with Rumpole, who promptly decides to travel up to Gunster himself, with Liz Probert as his junior, to dig into the connection between the Oslers and the university establishment. At the Gunster library, Professor Wayfield -- happy to gossip now that students are out of earshot -- confirms the Oslers' existence and influence over local appointments, recounts catching a former university registrar sneaking into one of their secret dinners, and muses, in fluent and pointed Latin, on how little a woman's promises to her lover are worth and on the irony of Climpton's supposedly scandalous affair with the fashionable Mercy Charles.

At trial before the bluntly common-sense Mr Justice Ollivant -- who insists on calling the dead man's fall simply an "incident" until murder is proved, over Rumpole's objection that the prosecution's own choice of language is doing some of its work for it -- Rumpole cross-examines Mrs O'Leary and draws out that she had already heard Climpton use the "licking the chancellor's boots" line hours earlier at the tea party, meaning her later identification of the voice on the stairs may be nothing more than assuming the familiar insult belonged to the man she already associated with it. A student witness, Christopher Perkins, adds that he saw Climpton leaving his rooms in a hurry around 9:15pm carrying a zipper hold-all, ostensibly on his way to play squash -- testimony the prosecution plainly intends to use to place Climpton near the scene at the crucial time. Rumpole, working urgently behind the scenes, has Audrey Wiston retrieve something from Climpton's rooms to use at trial, while Climpton himself continues to refuse any explanation of his movements that night.

The case turns on a dramatic and unwilling alibi: Rumpole calls Sir Dennis Tolson himself to the stand, provoking Climpton into an outburst of protest in the dock -- because to confirm his alibi, Climpton must let it be known that he too is a secret member of the Ancient Order of Oslers. Under Rumpole's questioning, and only after an appeal to the oath's limits that the judge insists on enforcing, Tolson confirms that the Oslers met at the Gunster Arms Hotel from 9:30pm that night, that Climpton arrived about ten minutes early at 9:20 -- while Hayden Charles was demonstrably still alive -- and that Climpton remained with the group, initiating new members, until the gathering broke up around midnight, well after the housekeeper discovered the body. The alibi is airtight, and the jury duly acquits Climpton, but the victory costs him dearly: his secret membership in a body of ambitious local businessmen and property developers, the very establishment he postures against, is now public knowledge, and Liz Probert notes acidly that he has lost his standing with the left-wing students who once compared him to Nelson Mandela.

Outside court, Rumpole privately puts a different theory to Professor Wayfield: that the voice on the stairs was not quoting Climpton's English insult at all but crying out the Latin tag "O tempora, O mores" -- Cicero's lament for a degenerate age -- which Mrs O'Leary's ear, untrained in Latin, garbled into "temporary" and "more is," and which only a classics don enraged at the university's abandonment of his subject would have shouted at the man killing it off. Wayfield neither confirms nor denies it, remarking only that Rumpole argued Climpton's case very well, and the two part with a half-serious invitation to dinner that Rumpole, mindful of Gunster's secrets, politely declines to follow up in person.

Back in chambers, the subplots resolve in quick succession: Erskine Brown reports that Uncle Tom enthusiastically, if obliviously, confirmed the false opera story to Phillida, even humming a love song from the wrong interval -- but that telling Phillida the truth, that it was Rumpole's idea all along and that the real purpose of the evening was chambers business with Liz, has unexpectedly begun to work in his favour, leaving Phillida "thinking it over" rather than storming off for good. Ballard, for his part, is persuaded by Rumpole that "the time for secrets is past" and admits to Marguerite that his mysterious bag has been for aerobics classes undertaken purely to please her, news Matey receives with evident delight once Hilda relays it by telephone.

The episode closes at the Rumpoles' house, with Hilda teasing her husband about following Ballard's example and taking up gymnastics himself, and Rumpole translating, with satisfaction, the Latin tag Professor Wayfield had written on his card -- Horace's own line about seeking truth "in the groves of academe" -- a wry closing echo of a case built, from first scene to last, on people's reluctance to say plainly what they actually knew.
