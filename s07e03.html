Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 7, Episode 3

Rumpole and the Eternal Triangle

Rumpole becomes infatuated with a violinist and ends up defending her husband on a charge of murdering her lover.

Aired Thursday, 12 November 1992 · ITV (Thames Television)

Rumpole, Hilda, and Claude Erskine-Brown attend a concert by the Castorini Trio (violinist and leader Desmond Castorini, cellist Elizabeth Castorini, and pianist Tom Randall), where Rumpole, more taken with a half-remembered line of poetry than with the Schubert trio itself, is approached at the bar afterward by Elizabeth. She recalls watching him years earlier, from the public gallery, successfully defend a college friend of hers named Billy (Billy Halliday) in a criminal trial, and flatters him so effusively that she presses her phone number on him before being called away by Tom Randall. Back at Rumpole's chambers, a separate thread begins when Claude Erskine-Brown overhears clerk Henry delivering an extravagantly lascivious speech to the new typist, Dot Clapton (all shining hair, "deep pools of your eyes," and bodies "mingling") and, scandalized, reports a suspected case of sexual harassment to head of chambers Sam Ballard, who is instantly energized by the moral emergency.

Flattered by Elizabeth's attention, Rumpole quietly begins remaking himself: a haircut and a bottle of "McKizmo" cologne from the barber, and, over lunch with Elizabeth at a vegetarian restaurant she has chosen, a sudden conversion to not eating meat and drinking only water, all transparently for her benefit. Elizabeth, flirtatious and world-weary, confides in Rumpole about her marriage: her husband Desmond has become consumed by an irrational, obsessive jealousy of Tom Randall, to the point of keeping his late father's old loaded service revolver (a family heirloom from Desmond's Italian-and-Irish father, who kept it under his pillow against enemies of his own) as a kind of talisman behind the piano in the trio's rehearsal room. She describes the trio's dynamic as having curdled from "an eternal triangle" of professional partnership into something closer to a trap, and asks whether she and Rumpole might meet again, simply to talk; at home, Hilda immediately notices Rumpole's new fragrance, haircut, and hat, and teases him, not entirely without suspicion, about having "civilised" himself just for her sake at the concert.

The chambers harassment plot thickens when Erskine-Brown tries, clumsily and repeatedly, to get Dot to make a formal statement about Henry's supposed advances, cornering her over her lunchtime sandwiches and suggesting she come into his own room to talk more privately, an invitation Dot pointedly declines. Meanwhile, in the trio's rehearsal building, Tom Randall is found shot dead; Desmond Castorini, who discovered the body and got blood on his cuff while checking for a pulse, is arrested and charged with his murder by Detective Inspector Baker. Elizabeth comes to Rumpole's chambers in evident distress, asking him to defend her husband and insisting the case against him is "absolute nonsense"; Rumpole, already instructed through solicitor Mr Bernard, agrees to take it on, though he warns Elizabeth, once she is listed as a prosecution witness, that they should not meet again before the trial.

In conference, Desmond tells Rumpole he never suspected Elizabeth and Tom of anything and had no warning that Tom wanted to see him that evening; Rumpole bluntly lays out how damaging the obvious theory looks: a jealous husband shooting his wife's suspected lover, and presses Desmond on the practical details: the message pad noting the six o'clock meeting, the stuck lift forcing him up the stairs, the locked-but-not-locked door, the body on the floor. Bernard's background digging turns up that Elizabeth's money (which bankrolled the trio and let Tom give up other work to join it) came from a boutique she ran at college called "Dreams of Youth," and that Elizabeth had once attended, as a spectator, the drug-dealing trial of a musicians' agent named Hoffman, at which her friend Billy was acquitted and at which a then-bearded fellow student, Tom Cogsmill (later known, clean-shaven, as Tom Randall), gave evidence for the prosecution without ever implicating Elizabeth herself.

Chambers farce continues in parallel: Ballard confronts Erskine-Brown self-righteously about "flagrant immorality" infecting the building, convinced an unnamed harasser is loose, until he reveals that the formal complaint Dot Clapton actually filed names Erskine-Brown himself, not Henry, over the private-room invitation and talk of "urges." A mortified Erskine-Brown protests he only meant to investigate Henry's behaviour on Dot's behalf; Ballard, unmoved, advises him to "make a clean breast of it" to his wife. Separately, Liz Probert is overheard on the phone seemingly discussing a disputed pregnancy and child support with someone called Dave, alarming Ballard into suspecting a chambers paternity scandal, before it emerges she was simply handling a client's divorce case, Singleton v. Singleton.

At the Old Bailey, before the bluff, Yorkshire-chauvinist Mr Justice Ollivant (fond of folksy "common sense" pronouncements and visibly impatient with Rumpole), Detective Inspector Baker confirms the revolver was found carelessly behind the piano with no fingerprints on it, which Rumpole uses to suggest it makes little sense for a guilty man to leave his own traceable weapon at the scene while somehow also wiping it clean. A fellow musician in the building, French-horn player Michael Matheson, who knew Tom and Elizabeth from college and admits he was "a bit in love with her" himself, testifies to overhearing Elizabeth and Tom arguing by the lift shortly before the murder: Elizabeth saying she "can't go on like this," Tom replying that he would "have to tell him" before everyone else knew, and confirms the blood on Desmond's cuff and the jammed lift. Under Rumpole's cross-examination, Matheson also confirms Elizabeth once ran the "Dreams of Youth" boutique, over Mr Justice Ollivant's repeated impatience with where this line of questioning is heading.

Elizabeth Castorini takes the stand for the prosecution and testifies that Desmond was irrationally jealous and that she had always feared "something like this would happen," given that he owned a gun. Rumpole's cross-examination turns the case around: he establishes, over the judge's objections that this is "ancient history," that Elizabeth's old college drug-trial connections, and Tom's silence about her involvement at the time, gave Tom lasting leverage over her; that Tom's bank statements show regular payments from her; and that the overheard "I'll have to tell him" more plausibly referred to Tom threatening to tell Desmond where Elizabeth's money had really come from, a blackmail motive, rather than a romantic confession. Mr Justice Ollivant formally warns Elizabeth she need not answer self-incriminating questions, but she insists on answering anyway.

Rumpole then reconstructs an alternative sequence of events: that Elizabeth, not Desmond, had the opportunity between a lunchtime concert and a six o'clock drink with the trio's agent to take her husband's gun, arrange to meet Tom by car-phone (a call Rumpole suggests the itemized phone bill will show), shoot him, leave the weapon in plain sight to incriminate Desmond, and slip away up the stairs, across the roof, and down a fire escape while jamming the lift open behind her, all while Desmond was establishing an alibi with his sister visiting from Dublin. Elizabeth denies every suggestion, but Rumpole presses her on why she volunteered as a witness at all when she could not be compelled to testify against her own husband, concluding pointedly that she came "to make sure that your husband was convicted of a crime that you committed," and that she had hoped, in briefing him through Desmond, to secure a barrister who would not turn on her: "I am sorry to have disappointed you, Mrs Castorini." The trial's verdict is left unstated as the court scenes end.

Back in chambers, the harassment farce resolves happily for Erskine-Brown: Rumpole reveals that the overwrought dialogue he overheard Henry rehearsing with Dot was not a seduction but lines from a play written for the Bexleyheath amateur dramatics group by a local playwright, Mildred Hane, in which both Henry and Dot have parts, deflating Ballard's entire "moral decay" investigation along with the Probert paternity scare. At home that evening, Rumpole, asking Hilda for a chop after all, confesses, half in Keatsian allusion, quoting "La Belle Dame sans Merci," that he had briefly been enchanted by "that Mrs Castorini," and Hilda, unsurprised and a little smug, produces two chops she'd been holding back, teasing him that whatever else he is, he was "never a fairy's child," which, she notes dryly, is rather in his favour.
