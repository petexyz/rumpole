Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 5, Episode 1

Rumpole and the Bubble Reputation

A tabloid hires Rumpole to defend it in a libel suit brought by a puritanical novelist it accused of promiscuity.

Wednesday, 23 November 1988 · ITV (Thames Television)

In particular dialogue blocks frequently run two speakers' lines together under a single tag, so a handful of attributions below are inferred from context rather than a confident speaker reading. This episode was originally broadcast 23 November 1988 (Series 5), directed by Mike Vardy, written by John Mortimer.

The episode opens in the Rumpoles' bedroom, where Hilda is absorbed in the latest historical romance by the prim, hugely popular novelist Amelia Nettleship, reading aloud a purple passage about the virtuous Lucy Lockhampton while Rumpole begs for the light to be put out. A parallel scene shows Nettleship herself, at home with her assistant Hetty, horrified to learn that the Daily Beacon has run a piece suggesting that for all her novels' insistence on keeping one's legs crossed until the ring is on one's finger, "Amelia Nettleship" has a torrid private life of her own, including a married lover seen leaving her house in the small hours; Nettleship instructs Hetty to get onto the lawyers at once. At Pommeroy's Wine Bar, Claude Erskine Brown complains to Rumpole that his own practice has sunk to an affray case over a nightclub brawl with Coca-Cola bottles at the Kitten Agogo Club, and Rumpole, with a large claret in hand, advises him to inspect the locus in quo (the scene of the crime) in person. The Beacon's deputy editor Ted Sleeman, an old schoolfriend of Erskine Brown's, overhears them and introduces Rumpole to his editor, Maurice Machin, who flatters Rumpole shamelessly and offers him the brief defending the paper and Machin personally against Amelia Nettleship's looming libel action. The Beacon's own reporter on the story, one Stella January, had since left the paper and vanished, leaving no one to give evidence of how the story was actually obtained.

Rumpole, mindful that a long-running civil case at Fleet Street rates could finally provide for his old age, takes the brief with relish, over Erskine Brown's protests that Rumpole knows nothing whatever about libel. At the solicitors' offices of the newspaper's proprietors, Melbury Publications, leading counsel Robin Pepiot QC and his junior Dick Garsington want to settle quietly and apologize, since any attempt to justify the article's claims about Nettleship risks driving the damages into the hundreds of thousands; Rumpole, representing Machin separately, refuses point-blank to cave and insists the defense of truth must be tried, over solicitor Mr. Coxham's dismay that there is no actual evidence Nettleship did any of these things. Back in chambers, clerk Henry is delighted to report Rumpole's refresher fee of 500 pounds a day, and Rumpole, scenting a long and lucrative case, resolves to "stretch it out until the trump of doom." Meanwhile Erskine Brown's dutiful visit to the Kitten Agogo Club to inspect the scene for his affray case backfires badly: the Beacon photographs him there and runs a piece implying he is one of its sleazy regular patrons, under the headline about London's "square mile of sin." Head of Chambers Sam Ballard QC, scandalized, hauls Erskine Brown in and all but orders him to sue the Beacon for libel to protect the chambers' good name, blithely unaware that the newspaper publishing the story about Erskine Brown is the very client Rumpole is defending.

Erskine Brown's humiliation deepens when his wife Phillida, hearing of the story, tells him they had better live apart for a while "for the sake of the children," and he turns up, bags packed, to be taken in at the Rumpoles' house. Hilda, feeling sorry for him, installs him in the spare room with a stock of muesli, organic honey, and goat's milk, and subjects Rumpole to nightly recitals of Wagner's Meistersinger on the gramophone while Rumpole is left eating cold cereal and losing his chair. Rumpole, feeling obscurely responsible for having sent Erskine Brown to the club in the first place, enlists the private investigator Fig Newton ("Ferdinand Isaac Gerald Newton," known to the trade as Fig) to keep a discreet watch on Amelia Nettleship's house at night, hoping to find out just who her mysterious nocturnal visitor really is. He also leans on Ted Sleeman, who as features editor ought to know something about the vanished Stella January, to dig up the original copy of the offending article from the Beacon's computer system.

At trial before Mr. Justice Teasdale (an unmarried, cat-owning former Tory parliamentary candidate who turns out, to Rumpole's private amusement, to be a devoted fan of Nettleship's novels), leading counsel Peregrine Lancia QC opens for Nettleship, praising her "rattling good yarns" in terms so fawning that Rumpole gets the judge to agree the jury should be left to form its own opinion of the author rather than take counsel's word for it. Pepiot, for Melbury Publications, makes clear from the outset that the proprietors are not attempting to justify the allegations and are conceding the case on damages alone, leaving Rumpole, for Machin, as the one voice still prepared to fight. Erskine Brown, who wins his own libel claim against the Beacon in a separate, much quicker settlement, gets a grovelling front-page apology and a substantial tax-free sum, and promptly patches things up with Phillida over dinner, moving out of the Rumpoles' spare room just as abruptly as he moved in.

On the witness stand, Nettleship swears under oath that there is "not one word of truth" in the Beacon's article, and plays the wronged, courageous authoress to the hilt, winning sympathy from the bench. Rumpole's cross-examination takes a different tack entirely: rather than attacking her morals, he picks apart the historical accuracy and prose style of her novel Lord Stingo's Fancy, demonstrating with relish that her heroine Sophia would have had to be in her thirties, not her dewy teens, by the time of the second coronation scene she describes, since the entire Cromwellian Commonwealth falls between the two historical events the book glosses over, and that the passage is littered with split infinitives and at least one glaring tautology. The judge, visibly uncomfortable and clearly a fan himself, presses Rumpole on the relevance of all this, but Rumpole insists it goes to the witness's basic credibility as a self-styled "historical novelist," before finally turning, late in the day's cross-examination, to the matter of her unnamed married lover and the visitor seen leaving her house at half past five one rainy morning, a line of questioning Nettleship furiously denies but cannot fully deflect.

That evening, Fig Newton reports back to Rumpole: he failed to get the licence number or a clear look at the driver of the car that visited Nettleship's house, but after she had gone inside and put out the lights, he recovered an item dropped on the drive near the car: evidently a copy of the Beacon's first edition, the one that would only have reached an editor's desk around ten o'clock the previous evening, long before it reached newsstands. Confronting Ted Sleeman afterward, Rumpole also learns that the original "Stella January" article, despite supposedly being filed by a reporter who has since vanished, was logged into the paper's computer system from the editor's own word processor. Putting the two facts together (the rare early edition at Nettleship's door and the editor's authorship of the piece attacking her), Rumpole goes that night to confront Maurice Machin directly at the Beacon's offices, laying out the whole scheme: Machin, whose circulation and job are both in trouble, has been secretly involved with Nettleship himself; the two of them cooked up the scandalous, anonymously bylined article together, knowing it would provoke a hugely damaging libel suit against the deep-pocketed proprietors, with Nettleship and Machin planning to split a tax-free settlement once the case was resolved, and they chose Rumpole, "an old Bailey hack" with no instinct for a quiet civil settlement, precisely because his pugnacious cross-examination would inflame rather than calm the damages.

Rumpole, furious at having been used as the unwitting engine of a conspiracy to pervert the course of justice, resigns on the spot as Machin's counsel and tells him plainly that as a private citizen he is now minded to report an attempted crime, but, ever the practical negotiator even in outrage, offers Machin a way out: Nettleship must withdraw her action entirely, leaving her and Machin to bear the costs (including Fig Newton's fee, and compensation for Fig's cold caught on the nighttime stakeout), and the Beacon must still print a full and abject apology to Claude Erskine Brown together with a further payment of damages to him. The next morning in chambers, a jubilant, vindicated Erskine Brown reads out his own front-page apology to Henry and Diane, blithely unaware for a moment that Rumpole's case has just collapsed entirely: Nettleship has dropped her suit, Rumpole's own client Machin is left to pay the costs of the whole abortive two weeks, and the promised windfall "provision for my old age" evaporates as quickly as it appeared. Rumpole, more amused than bitter at the irony, asks Henry to find him "a nice gentle breaking and entering" instead (something, he says, that shows human nature in a better light than the civil courts) while at home that night Hilda, oblivious to the scandal she helped create by harbouring Erskine Brown, has already moved on to a new chapter of Amelia Nettleship's next novel, prompting Rumpole's dry closing observation that the authoress is, after all, "no better than she should be," and neither, by his own lights, is anyone else.
