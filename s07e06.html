Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 7, Episode 6

Rumpole on Trial

Rumpole is hauled before a bar disciplinary tribunal for allegedly coaching a client mid-testimony and insulting the judge, but the true target of his overheard outburst turns out to be someone else entirely.

Thursday, 3 December 1992 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 3 December 1992 (Series 7), directed by John Gorrie, written by John Mortimer, the final episode of the original 1978-1992 series. A recurring mis-hearing renders "Nick" as "Boris" in a couple of places and "landlady" as "lander"/"Mansplorter"; read as intended given context, not corrected in the quoted dialogue below.

At his dentist's chair in Harley Street, Rumpole mentions to Mr. Lionel Learing, between drillings, that he's mid-trial at the Old Bailey defending a Mr. Tong on a manslaughter charge (his wife died after a fall during a domestic quarrel) and that he's appearing before Mr. Justice Ollivant, a blustery judge who prides himself on blunt "Yorkshire common sense." Learing fits him with a temporary filling meant to see him through the day. In the robing room, Rumpole collides with a flustered Liz Probert and needles Claude Erskine-Brown (newly in silk and visibly puffed up about it) over a pair of mixed-up bands.

In court, the trial opens badly: Tong, a devoutly religious former Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries clerk, refuses to swear a straightforward oath, objecting on scriptural grounds (quoting Ezekiel) that he won't "drag down" his God to a sinful courtroom. Ollivant, baffled and irritated, repeatedly leans on Rumpole to "control his client" and just as repeatedly lets slip prejudicial asides (calling the case "manslaughter" before the jury has decided, assuming Rumpole's version of events is the one going to the jury), which Rumpole needles him for ("a little premature adjudication?"). Meanwhile, away from court, Hilda pays a call on Marigold Featherstone to lobby, through Guthrie's influence, for Rumpole to be made a sitting judge, not for the honor, but because she thinks a desk job would cure his exhaustion, temper, and toothache.

Over the lunch adjournment (during which Ollivant has expressly ordered that no one speak to the defendant Tong), Rumpole, alone in a corridor and in real pain from his tooth, loudly vents at someone unseen: "You're driving me mad... you boring old fart... just get it out... put me out of my misery." When court resumes, Erskine-Brown reports to the judge that he personally overheard Rumpole speaking to Tong during the break, repeating the "boring old fart" line as having been said to the defendant. Rumpole, cagey, neither confirms nor denies it, observing drily that he isn't the one on trial. Tong is acquitted regardless, but the damage is done: that evening, at the Inn's dining hall, Ollivant complains to the Master Treasurer and a visiting Bishop about Rumpole's theatrical shabbiness and insolence, and reveals that Rumpole's conduct (the alleged breach of the no-contact order, plus his rudeness on the bench) has been referred to a bar disciplinary tribunal. "Rumpole on trial," the Bishop muses, half-amused.

At home, Rumpole and Hilda talk past each other: she's heard a rumor from Marigold and assumes his news is that he's being made a judge; he has to correct her, he's not becoming a judge, he's facing disbarment. Thinking his career may be over, he quotes Byron to her, half to himself: "For the sword outwears its sheath, / And the soul wears out the breast, / And the heart must pause to breathe, / And love itself have rest." Hilda vows to stand by him "through thick and thin, no matter what the cost", a declaration that visibly unsettles Rumpole more than the tribunal does.

In chambers, Hilda takes matters into her own hands, first pressing the family's instructing solicitor Mr. Bernard for reassurance, then confronting Head of Chambers Sam Ballard and all but ordering him, as a matter of professional duty, to take on Rumpole's defense. Separately, Fred Timson (head of the recurring Timson crime family) tries to help by digging up a fabricated alibi (two Timson relatives who supposedly saw nothing), but it collapses the moment Ballard asks a basic follow-up question about which judge was sitting that day. Ballard does take the brief, but his strategy is simply to get Rumpole to apologize; under pressure from both Ballard and a guilt-tripping Hilda ("How can I bear to be the wife of a disbarred bouncer"), Rumpole reluctantly agrees to apologize to Ollivant.

At the tribunal, Ballard's examination of Ollivant goes as planned until the chairman presses on what exactly was said; Rumpole, rather than apologizing cleanly, offers a backhanded non-apology, calling Ollivant "a more sensitive plant" than most judges he's faced, which the tribunal, rattled, lets pass as sufficient contrition for the moment, adjourning the question of a "proper, unilateral apology" to the next session. Away from the hearing, Liz Probert (defying Rumpole's own instruction not to fight the case) reconstructs the corridor scene with Erskine-Brown on site, walking him through exactly where he was standing and what he could and couldn't have seen, and realizes his testimony rests entirely on assumption rather than direct observation.

Probert gets her chance to prove it: conducting her first real cross-examination in the case, she calls Rumpole's own dentist, Lionel Learing, as a surprise witness. Learing testifies that Rumpole phoned him in pain at lunchtime that very day, and that the "boring old fart... get it out... put me out of my misery" outburst Erskine-Brown overheard was addressed to him, about the troublesome tooth, not to Tong, and not about the judge. With that, and with Erskine-Brown's own evidence confirming he never actually saw Rumpole with Tong during the break, the central charge collapses; only the offensive-remarks-to-Ollivant matter remains, and Rumpole's earlier backhanded apology is allowed to stand. He leaves, as the party later puts it, "without a stain on his character."

The episode closes with a chambers party honoring two real status changes: Phillida Erskine-Brown's elevation to the bench as a judge, and Claude's own silk. Subplots resolve in passing, clerk Henry's infatuation with typist Dot Clapton collapses when his wife Eileen, far from threatened, cheerfully confirms Dot isn't interested ("something a bit more muscular"), and Eileen herself refuses to go back to her diet for anyone's sake. Ollivant, mollified, admits he rather enjoyed making Rumpole squirm. Rumpole corners Claude to explain, half-apologetically, half-smugly, that his outburst really was about the dentist, then, pressed on what's gotten into him, quotes the Byron lines again, but changes the ending: "But not quite yet. Perhaps not quite yet." He closes the party with a characteristic, unrepentant toast (to Fred Timson and "the criminals of England," without whom, he points out, none of the judges, barristers, or policemen in the room would have any work at all), leaving Hilda sighing "Rumpole!" as the episode, and the original series, ends.
