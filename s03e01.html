Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 3, Episode 1

Rumpole and the Genuine Article

An art dealer is accused of selling a forged painting; Rumpole shows it's genuine and the defendant tried to frame himself.

Tuesday, 11 October 1983 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 11 October 1983 (Series 3), directed by Robert Knights, written by John Mortimer.

The episode opens at a London auction house, where a painting attributed to the late society portraitist Septimus Craig, RA (a nude titled "Nancy at Dieppe"), sells for thirty-eight thousand pounds to a Mrs De Moyn. The moment the hammer falls, Detective Inspector Tebbit steps in to arrest the man who consigned it, the flamboyant painter Harold Brittling, on a charge of obtaining money by deception through the forgery of the picture. Brittling's first instinct, even as he is led away, is to send his young companion Pauline to track down Horace Rumpole in chambers, not any solicitor she happens to find, but Rumpole specifically, on the strength of his having once got a horse-loving acquaintance of Brittling's off a charge at Cambridge Assizes.

Meanwhile, chambers is consumed by a separate piece of gossip: Guthrie Featherstone MP, head of chambers, is apparently about to be appointed a judge, and is agonized that word is leaking out before the Lord Chancellor's office makes it official, fatal, he insists, to the appointment, citing the cautionary tale of one Morton Colfax, who lost a judgeship the same way. Rumpole needles him about it in his room and again in the clerks' room with Henry, who primly declines to confirm or deny anything while all but confirming it. Henry's question hangs over the episode's title: whether Featherstone, suited and golf-club-connected as he is, is actually "the genuine article" as a judge, as opposed to merely looking the part.

Brittling duly arrives in Rumpole's room in person, bursting in with Pauline and a bottle of champagne, utterly unrepentant and oddly eager to celebrate rather than to mount a defense. He claims absurd, inflated credentials (Slade School gold medalist, hung at the Royal Academy) and insists the painting is "the genuine article," complete with an impeccable provenance running through Craig's niece in Swansea, Miss Price, who put it into the auction on his behalf for a small commission because the (fictitious) real seller wanted anonymity. Along the way Brittling claims, bizarrely, to recognize Hilda from his bohemian Soho drinking days, fluster that Hilda both denies and seems faintly flattered by. When Rumpole presses him on whether the picture is a forgery, Brittling toys with him, winking and talking about his own "unusual aptitude" for painting female models from life, behavior Rumpole finds "most unsettling" in a client who seems to be hinting he is guilty.

Hilda's own subplot runs alongside: at Harrods she bumps into Marigold Featherstone, who is shopping for an outfit for a Palace investiture and lets slip, before anxiously trying to retract it, that Guthrie is about to get "a handle," a knighthood that comes automatically with a judicial appointment. Hilda relays this to Rumpole at home, having had it independently confirmed by Phillida Erskine Brown in the supermarket, and the news reaches Featherstone's own ears soon after at Pomeroy's wine bar, where Claude Erskine Brown raises a telling toast to him. Featherstone, convinced the secret's leak will cost him the post exactly as it cost Colfax, grows increasingly rattled, and pointedly does not believe Rumpole's denials of having spread the story himself.

The trial opens at the Old Bailey with, as it turns out, Featherstone already sitting as the judge, the appointment evidently having gone through regardless. The prosecution, led by Erskine Brown, calls the art expert Mr Gandolfini, whose hedging testimony about the picture's "doubtful" authenticity Rumpole picks apart by fixing on the purely aesthetic question of whether the painting is beautiful, regardless of who painted it, building to a Keatsian flourish ("beauty is truth, truth beauty... that is all ye know on earth and all ye need to know") that visibly irritates both Erskine Brown and the judge. Mrs De Moyn testifies that she was tipped off by an anonymous telephone caller that the picture was not a genuine Craig but was, confusingly, "better than a Craig" and so still a bargain; she can only recall that the caller's name had something like "White" in it. Miss Price then gives sympathetic, guileless evidence about selling the picture for her modest ten-percent cut, genuinely unaware anything was amiss, leaving Rumpole with nothing to cross-examine: "nothing more like banging your head against a brick wall than cross-examining a witness who's telling nothing but the truth."

In the judge's private room, Featherstone frets about court scheduling because of his looming Palace ceremony, and separately rebukes Rumpole for a bad habit of keeping his hands in his pockets while addressing the court, a petty, newly judicial note of authority that visibly amuses Rumpole. Brittling then takes the stand himself and is a disaster as a witness: boastful, evasive about where the painting actually came from, and ultimately unable to resist the suggestion that he himself painted it, not to escape the forgery charge but, perversely, to claim the credit, musing aloud that "if I did paint this picture, I must be a genius, mustn't I?" His bail is revoked on the spot. Afterward, a shaken Pauline walks with Rumpole to a pub and admits she fears Brittling actually wants to lose the case and go to prison rather than be acquitted as a non-entity.

That night, at the Monmouth pub in Soho, Rumpole tracks down the witness who can resolve everything: Nancy Brittling, Harold's estranged wife, the "Nancy" of the painting's title, whom Brittling himself had tried to prevent from being called. Rumpole returns home late to find Hilda has locked him out over suspicions about Pauline, forcing a conversation through the door and a night on the sofa. A separate phone call to Claude Erskine Brown that night, apologized for at length, given that the Erskine Browns' child is only four and Rumpole still assumes a baby, asks him to have the police put a specific name, "Blanco Basnett," to Mrs De Moyn, on the theory that "Blanco" simply means "white."

The next day, Nancy Brittling's evidence demolishes the forgery charge from an unexpected angle. She confirms she modeled nude for Septimus Craig at a Dieppe hotel during an affair that began at Rottingdean (where Harold had first introduced her to Craig, and was consumed with jealousy over it), that she watched Craig paint and sign the picture, and that she herself scrawled "Nancy" upside down near the edge of the carpet in the painting as a private joke between lovers. Craig gave her the picture; after she and Harold separated, Harold kept it, and years later, driven by lifelong resentment of Craig's genius next to his own merely competent talent, tried to pass it off as his own forged masterpiece rather than let it stand as proof of Craig's superiority. In his closing speech, Rumpole turns this into the defense itself: Brittling is "a fake criminal," guilty of nothing but corrosive envy of a better artist, because the painting is genuine and he never actually forged anything; he only wished he had the nerve to. The jury acquits; a furious Brittling, robbed of the one outcome he wanted, calls Rumpole "a bastard" who has "joined the Connoisseurs."

Afterward, Featherstone, now comfortably installed as the trial judge, congratulates Rumpole on the "win" and claims smug credit for his own supposed knack for spotting an honest witness. He then thanks Rumpole, rather pointedly, for having telephoned the Lord Chancellor's office to deny that Rumpole himself had been the source of the leak about the judgeship: Rumpole's dry, unconfirming reply ("Can't you tell, Guthrie? I thought you had the infallible judicial eye") leaves open exactly who did talk, and to whom.

The episode ends at home that evening, Rumpole arriving with champagne rather than his usual conciliatory flowers, parrying Hilda's pointed remarks about "Pauline of the Soft Eyes" from the pub the night before. He toasts the case's resolution with the same Keats line he'd deployed in court, then immediately undercuts it: beauty, he reflects, isn't the whole truth after all: at the Old Bailey, "we need to know a damn sight more than that."
