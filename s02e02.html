Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 2, Episode 2

Rumpole and the Case of Identity

Dave Anstey is charged with liquor-store robbery and GBH; a mistaken-identity defence; Anstey was framed by his employer.

Tuesday, 5 June 1979 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 5 June 1979 (Series 2), directed by Derek Bennett, written by John Mortimer.

A police appeal for witnesses opens the episode: a tall man in a distinctive red-and-yellow tartan cap and red driving gloves attacked the manager of a Walthamstow off-licence. At a minicab office, Dave Anstey clocks off for the evening, affectionately seen off by his employer, Freddie Allbright ("Allbright will see you right," by his own motto, the biggest minicab operator in London), who sends his love to Dave's wife, Betty. After an identity parade, Rumpole visits Dave in Brixton Prison, where Dave insists he's "in the clear": surely no one planning a stabbing would wear anything as memorable as his tartan cap, unless he wanted to be recognized, or wanted someone else to be. Dave's alibi seems airtight: he was out with Freddie, who has been like a father to him and lent him money for his house deposit, eating a curry and admiring an evening bag Freddie had bought his own wife for her birthday, on the evening of the stabbing. Rumpole, unimpressed, warns that "cast-iron alibis" are exactly the kind that sink fastest.

Back in chambers, a parallel comic plot is building around Guthrie Featherstone, QC, MP and Head of Chambers. Erskine Brown, working late, discovers Featherstone's room locked with the light on and hears what sounds like giggling inside; Rumpole dismisses it as mice. Featherstone has been pleading all-night Parliamentary sittings (ostensibly over cod fisheries in Scottish waters) to explain his increasingly erratic hours, while a new typist in chambers, Angela, a politically minded young woman in a combat shirt who refuses on principle to type landlord's claims against tenants, has become a running irritant to clerk Henry and to Erskine Brown. Erskine Brown, newly engaged to Phillida Trant, spots a parliamentary report showing the House rose at 10:30 the night Featherstone claimed to be sitting all night, and starts to suspect his alibi is collapsing just like a defendant's.

At home, Hilda has joined the Bar Choral Society at the urging of Marigold Featherstone, and reports, to Rumpole's feigned shock at the gossip, that the Featherstone marriage is "cracking up," supposedly because Rumpole keeps Guthrie out late at Pomeroy's. Rumpole privately registers that this is news to him. In court, the Anstey trial proper gets underway before a jocular judge who needles Rumpole about the age of the electric razor when Rumpole points out his clean-shaven client doesn't match the victim Paddy's description of a man with long sideburns. Rumpole picks apart Paddy's identification piece by piece, a matter of seconds' glimpse, a vague "brown" hair colour, no real certainty, while Erskine Brown and Phillida watch from the gallery, with Phillida predicting correctly that a hard time from the bench is exactly what Rumpole thrives on.

In the court corridor, Rumpole meets Freddie Allbright in person, all bluff cheer, introducing himself as Dave's governor and offering his alibi "ready and waiting." Dave's wife, Betty, is visibly anxious about appearing as a witness. Erskine Brown, asked by Rumpole to find out who owns the off-licence, discovers it belongs to a company called Allbright Motors Limited, Freddie's own company, a detail Rumpole notes with interest. Back in Brixton, Rumpole warns Dave that a collapsed alibi poisons a jury's view of everything else a defendant says, and reveals that Freddie's company owns the very premises where Paddy was attacked; Dave, genuinely startled, insists he never knew. Rumpole also presses, apparently idly, on where Betty got the expensive fur coat she's been seen wearing (paid for, Dave insists, from her own savings) while Dave begs Rumpole to call Freddie as an alibi witness regardless.

Meanwhile the Featherstone subplot escalates in classic farce fashion: in the chambers library, Featherstone flirts clumsily with Phillida Trant, inviting her to lunch and comparing her to a librarian in an old James Stewart film; that night, at a disco called Fridays favoured by "beautiful people," Erskine Brown and Phillida spot Featherstone himself dancing enthusiastically with Angela in a loud multicoloured shirt. The next day, Marigold Featherstone corners Rumpole in the chambers corridor, having seen her husband arm-in-arm with a girl from the top of a bus, and asks Rumpole to represent her in a divorce; Rumpole, citing professional conflict and arguing (in an echo of his own cross-examination technique) that eyewitness identification from a moving bus is notoriously unreliable, declines, but Marigold, undeterred, says that if it does come to divorce she wants it to be "as embarrassing as possible."

Rumpole springs his real trap in court: he has Betty Anstey brought into the courtroom still wearing her fur coat, then calls Freddie Allbright as a witness and, exploiting the rule that a witness who contradicts his own signed statement may be treated as hostile, dismantles Freddie's alibi date by date, forcing him to concede that the "evening bag" story actually pins the curry outing to the night after the stabbing, not the night of it, and that Dave had specifically asked him to say March the 4th. Rumpole then puts it to Freddie directly, and devastatingly, that he is Betty Anstey's lover, that he has been paying for her fur coat and "tiding her over" while her husband sits in custody, and that he sent someone wearing Dave's own distinctive cap to the off-licence to discipline the light-fingered Paddy, after which Paddy was pressured into identifying Dave himself as the attacker so that Freddie could have his rival safely put away. The judge bristles at Rumpole's language ("fitted up" is "hardly a legal term") but cannot stop the substance landing. The jury acquits Dave Anstey by a majority verdict after four hours ("a damn close-run thing," as Rumpole puts it at Pomeroy's afterward), with Erskine Brown full of admiration and grievance on Rumpole's behalf that he was passed over for Head of Chambers in favour of Featherstone.

The two plots converge: the same unreliable-identification logic that freed Dave Anstey gets turned on Guthrie Featherstone's scandal. Erskine Brown, now certain Featherstone is "having it off" with the typist, half-seriously proposes a chambers coup (forcing Featherstone to resign as Head of Chambers in Rumpole's favour, or splitting off to new chambers in Lincoln's Inn) but Rumpole needles him for mistaking identity yet again: no one could seriously believe staid Guthrie Featherstone would wear a loud shirt with tigers on it. Alone with Featherstone that night, Rumpole gets the real confession: a chance detour behind a golf green with Mr. Justice Vosper, watching a young couple kissing in the rough, left Featherstone newly aware of an entire world of feeling he'd missed, and he's since been slipping off nightly with Angela: locking his chambers door, lying to Marigold, hiding a borrowed dancing blouse in his room. Rumpole, half-mocking and half in earnest, argues Featherstone simply isn't built for that kind of escape: his whole life is "mapped out," cradle to the High Court bench, and accuses him of being, in effect, the only anarchist chambers can't afford to lose.

The crisis resolves itself off-page: Angela quits chambers "as a matter of conscience," and by the next scene Guthrie and Marigold are visibly reconciled, singing together in the Bar Choral Society and inviting Rumpole to the carol service (an invitation he deflects with transparent excuses about pressure of work). At the chambers Christmas party, Featherstone, declining to make a real speech, obliquely references "somebody peeling off in the typing pool," and Rumpole, seizing the moment, delivers a mock-grand toast affirming Guthrie Featherstone, QC, MP as a man "fashioned by nature to be head of chambers," urging the assembled "old Bailey hacks" into the new year under his leadership. The episode closes with a church scene, the chambers' wives and members singing "O Come, All Ye Faithful" at the carol service Rumpole has been dodging all episode, the Featherstone marriage and the chambers' uneasy peace both outwardly restored.
