Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 1, Episode 5

Rumpole and the Learned Friends

Rumpole accuses a dishonest policeman of framing his safecracker client, and gets into legal trouble when he cannot back the claim.

Monday, 1 May 1978 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 1 May 1978 (Series 1), directed by Graham Evans, written by John Mortimer.

The episode opens on Horace Rumpole laid up with flu, grimly joking to himself about dying rather than being merely ill: "there's a lot of it about," he's been told. A night scene at Dartford police station follows: Detective Inspector Dickerson leans on a professional safebreaker, Charlie Wheeler, over a post-office job, steering him toward a confession and, tellingly, offering to shake hands on the understanding. Back at the Rumpole house the next morning, Rumpole recites Keats to himself ("to cease upon the midnight with no pain") while Hilda relays Dr. Hanson's verdict that he has nothing worse than influenza. The moment clerk Henry rings about a new safe-blowing brief, Rumpole's talk of dying evaporates and he is out of bed and dressed, flu notwithstanding.

At chambers, Erskine Brown grumbles about the chronic accommodation problem (his pupil has to share a desk, George Frobisher must share a room with the heavily-conferencing Hoskins) and drops a hint that Rumpole, "a good age," might not be with them forever. Miss Trant has a hopeless-looking thief to defend; Rumpole delivers his stock sermon that "never plead guilty" should be written in letters a foot high on every wall in chambers. Henry informs Rumpole that he has been briefed on Wheeler's case with a two-counsel certificate, meaning Rumpole will be led rather than leading, to his visible annoyance (he recalls doing the Penge Bungalow Murders without a leader). The leader turns out to be Guthrie Featherstone QC, MP, who dismisses the case as "dead as a doornail": Wheeler's fingerprints are on a lump of gelignite found beside the blown safe at a Dartford sub-post office.

At Brixton Prison, Featherstone lays out a defeatist strategy (plead guilty, make no attack on the police), warning Wheeler (via solicitor Mr. Bernard) that the trial judge has a reputation for "bullying" any barrister who questions police honesty. Wheeler is frightened and biddable. Rumpole, needled, privately doubts the Crown's case on instinct alone: an old professional like Wheeler, he argues, would never blow a safe without gloves, and therefore would never leave prints on the explosive itself. Featherstone insists there is no "straw" to make "bricks" with; Rumpole is unconvinced that the case is as dead as his leader thinks.

At Pomeroy's Wine Bar, Rumpole hears from George Frobisher that ailing, elderly "Uncle Tom" is poorly, and is approached by the journalist Philbeam, who gets confused over who is actually leading whom on the Wheeler case. Philbeam then supplies the real gift of the scene: an unpublished tip from a minor Dartford villain named Harry Harris, who told him that Detective Inspector Dickerson ("Dirty Dickerson") once handed Harris a stolen cigarette case, let him get his fingerprints all over it, and then demanded three hundred pounds not to prosecute. Rumpole, delighted, asks Philbeam to track Harris down as the straw he needs to build a defense out of Dickerson's own methods.

In court the fingerprint evidence goes unchallenged: the expert's identification of Wheeler's prints on the gelignite stands unchallenged when Featherstone, leading, asks "no questions" of the police witness, to Rumpole's frustration at having no say as junior counsel. That evening at the Featherstone house, Mrs. Featherstone bullies her feverish husband (temperature up around 102) into bed, worried more about a Foreign Office dinner and his speech on devolution than about Wheeler. Featherstone telephones instructions down the line to Rumpole to carry on exactly as he began (no attack on the police), then retires for the week. Left alone, Rumpole gleefully confides to himself that he is the one who gave his learned leader the flu in the first place, freeing himself to run the defense his own way.

The next morning, with Philbeam still chasing Harry Harris through his sister's workplace and solicitor Bernard off at a funeral, Rumpole visits Wheeler alone at Brixton. He has Wheeler re-enact the two-a.m. handshake with Dickerson in the dark cell, recalling that the inspector's grip felt oddly clammy and nervous rather than firm, and floats the theory that Dickerson transferred the lump of gelignite into Wheeler's hand during that very handshake, manufacturing the fingerprint evidence on the spot. Wheeler, without Bernard present to take it down formally, gives Rumpole his informal go-ahead to attack Dickerson's honesty in court, over quoted scraps of Keats about "stout Cortez" staring at the Pacific.

Back in court, Rumpole, now effectively leading alone, Featherstone still absent with flu, cross-examines Dickerson directly. The judge repeatedly warns him off as irrelevant and accuses him of "crossing the Rubicon" by attacking a police officer's character without evidence, but Rumpole presses on: he gets Dickerson to deny any financial dealings with Harry Harris, forces the admission that Dickerson has never before shaken a prisoner's hand, and establishes that Dickerson showed no other officer the gelignite until he was already back at the station, meaning the jury must take his word alone that it was ever found at the scene at all. The court adjourns for lunch mid-cross-examination, with Miss Trant's earlier advice to Rumpole ("fight everything") looking vindicated.

The jury convicts Wheeler anyway. Worse, a frightened Wheeler disowns his own defense in open court, telling the judge he never wanted his barrister asking those questions and had told him to keep quiet. The judge, incensed, hands down a long sentence (stated first as twelve years, then raised on the spot to fourteen specifically because of what he calls Rumpole's vicious and apparently unauthorized attack on Detective Inspector Dickerson's integrity) and announces he will report Rumpole to the governing Senate of the Inns of Court for proceeding without instructions.

In the court corridor afterward, a now-recovered Featherstone pressures Rumpole to tell the Senate hearing that the attack on Dickerson was Rumpole's own escapade, carried out in the heat of the moment and now regretted: useful cover for Featherstone, since there is no written record of Wheeler's instructions (Bernard was at the funeral, clerk Joyce was elsewhere on another case). Rumpole flatly refuses to apologize, musing about chucking in the bar altogether for a smallholding behind Gloucester Road tube station, growing artichokes, leeks, parsnips, marrows, and eventually asparagus. At home that evening, a worried Hilda invites George Frobisher to dinner for the first time in eight years specifically to talk sense into her husband; George presses Rumpole on whether he genuinely believes Wheeler was innocent, and Rumpole admits he probably wasn't, but insists that was never the point, only that the police must not lie to, deceive, or con a defendant in a dark cell. Both Hilda and George call him "obstinate," noting he won't back down even facing suspension or disbarment.

That night in chambers, Rumpole and Miss Trant talk vegetables and retirement, and he drifts into the same unresolved question that closed the pilot episode: who is he, really, after a career spent being other people (safeblowers, fraudsmen, "a few rather gentle murderers") with remarkably little time left over to be Rumpole himself. Elsewhere, in a nighttime scene at a Chinese restaurant, Dickerson meets Harry Harris to collect his overdue "instalment," and Harris, having heard that Dickerson "fitted Charlie up nicely," pointedly needles him about Wheeler's friendly handshake, confirming for the audience that Rumpole's theory was correct: Dickerson routinely manufactures fingerprint evidence to blackmail small-time crooks, and threatens to do the same to Harris if he doesn't keep paying.

At a chambers meeting called to settle the accommodation dispute, Erskine Brown proposes simply taking over Rumpole's room, on the assumption that Rumpole means to retire and grow vegetables. Rumpole arrives late with news that changes everything: Dickerson has been suspended pending a full inquiry, and Wheeler, on hearing it, has conveniently "remembered" giving Rumpole his instructions after all, so an appeal is going forward on fresh evidence. Rumpole declines Featherstone's offer to lead the appeal together, preferring to go it alone, and the smallholding is indefinitely postponed, to Erskine Brown's visible disappointment. That night at home, braced for the Senate's verdict, Rumpole learns from Hilda that he has been let off, in fact all but commended for exposing one of the few genuinely rotten apples in an otherwise sound Metropolitan Police. Relief comes wrapped in grumbling; Hilda admits she'd have found having him underfoot all day unbearable, and the pair toast a future that, Rumpole notes, shows every sign of looking exactly like the past.
