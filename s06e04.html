Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 6, Episode 4

Rumpole at Sea

On a Mediterranean cruise Rumpole meets his old nemesis Judge Graves, and a fellow passenger disappears unexplained.

Monday, 18 November 1991 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 18 November 1991 (Series 6), directed by Michael Simpson, written by John Mortimer.

The episode opens on one of Rumpole's worst days at the Bailey: a bail application for a young Timson, already refused by the magistrates and by one High Court judge, is refused again, this time by the coldly unsympathetic Mr Justice Graves, whom Rumpole privately and repeatedly renames "Mr Injustice Graves." Rumpole's argument that the presumption of innocence should apply to Timsons and judges alike earns him only a reprimand and a threat that Graves will report his "personal and improper" advocacy to the Bar authorities. Shaken and fed up, Rumpole comes home to Hilda, who -- seizing on his rare vulnerability -- announces that what they both need is a second honeymoon, funded by the last of Aunt Tedder's legacy: a Mediterranean cruise, flying out to join the ship at Venice. Rumpole, who swears that "no power on earth" will get him aboard a cruise ship, is nonetheless aboard one within the next scene.

The newlywed-style idyll curdles almost immediately when Rumpole spots, to his horror, that his fellow passenger is none other than Mr Justice Graves, there for a rest cure from a certain barrister he does not name. Rumpole's first instinct is to get off the ship entirely, inventing a sudden allergy to sea travel and begging the ship's officers to put him ashore -- to no avail, since the next port of call is three days off. He spends the opening evenings skulking in the cabin while Hilda dines, and effectively tells the other passengers that she is travelling "entirely on her own," rather than admit her husband is hiding from a judge. Those fellow passengers include the bestselling and self-important mystery novelist Howard Swainton and his devoted personal assistant Linda Nelson, and a honeymooning couple, the newly ordained Reverend Bill Britwell -- a former insurance man who came late to the Church -- and his wife Mavis.

Rumpole's concealment collapses on deck, when Graves corners Hilda and, not realizing who she is married to, praises Rumpole's courtroom appearances as "quite unforgettable" and insists there is no advocate at the criminal bar "in the least like" him. Hilda, delighted, lets slip that her supposedly shy and retiring husband is standing right behind them, and Rumpole is forced to "break cover" and accept Graves's invitation to a drink at the Old Salt's Bar -- a forced, increasingly absurd shipboard camaraderie between barrister and judge that neither man particularly wants but that the confines of the voyage make unavoidable.

A second, more serious-seeming mystery develops around the Britwells: Mavis Britwell is reported unwell and confined to her cabin, missing cocktail parties and dinners, while her husband fends off everyone's sympathetic inquiries. Hilda, visiting out of kindness, is startled to find Mavis apparently dressed and lying on top of the bedclothes rather than genuinely laid up -- a detail she can't help repeating to the company. Howard Swainton, professionally delighted by any whiff of real-life intrigue, seizes on the oddity as inspiration for his new novel-in-progress, provisionally titled Absence of Body, and begins, half in jest and half in earnest, to float the theory that the Reverend Bill might have done away with his wife -- pointing out, with relish, that a body is not legally necessary for a murder conviction, as a historical case of a steward who pushed a female passenger through a porthole demonstrates.

The theory gains unwelcome traction when Hilda reports that Bill Britwell has been seen throwing two framed photographs overboard -- one his own wedding photograph, the other of an unknown young girl who seemed oddly familiar to Hilda -- an act of destruction so strange on a honeymoon that even Rumpole, while publicly scoffing at the whole exercise as "the product of a mind addled with detective stories," cannot quite ignore it. Graves and Swainton, improvising a kind of impromptu shipboard tribunal in the bar each evening, debate whether Britwell destroyed the photographs to erase the evidence of the people in them, while Rumpole insists on treating the Reverend as innocent until proven otherwise -- without, however, declining to join a staged "stakeout" in the cabin corridor that catches Britwell visibly lying to Graves about his wife being quietly in bed.

Matters escalate further when Mavis Britwell appears to disappear outright, with nobody able to confirm whether she is still aboard; the captain, prompted by Graves, sends a cautious signal to the police ashore, and Graves eagerly volunteers to be present if and when the Reverend is confronted. Swainton uses his scheduled onboard lecture on "how I think up my plots" to recount the real historical murder case behind his new book and to pointedly watch Bill Britwell's reaction as he describes it -- an unsubtle echo, as Graves himself notes, of the "play within a play" trick from Hamlet -- but the demonstration proves less revealing than intended.

The resolution comes through Rumpole's memory rather than anyone's deduction. At the ship's fancy-dress ball, Rumpole recognizes the lounge singer, Gloria de la Hay, as a faded music-hall performer he remembers from decades earlier, one half of a novelty double act called "Betty D and Buttercup." Putting two and two together, he draws Mavis Britwell -- who has in fact simply been hiding out with her old friend Gloria rather than meeting any sinister end -- out onto the deck and quietly confronts her: she was "Buttercup," the young showgirl in the discarded photograph, and years before had killed her first husband, a brutal drunk-act comedian called Happy Harry Harmon, in self-defence with a pair of scissors, and had served five years for manslaughter in a case prosecuted, as it happens, by a very young and already icily efficient Gerald Graves. Bill Britwell had thrown the photographs overboard not to erase his wife but to protect her hard-won respectable new life as a vicar's spouse from being connected to that old scandal.

Rumpole reassures Mavis that lawyers and judges almost never remember the faces of people they once sent to prison, and that she has nothing to fear from Graves's presence on the voyage; gently, he brings her back into the ballroom to general relief, deflating the entire "murder" theory at a stroke. He then turns, with evident satisfaction, to point out to Graves that in publicly speculating that the Reverend Bill had killed his wife, and in reporting that speculation to the captain -- who duly wired it on to the authorities ashore -- the judge has exposed himself to a very plausible action for defamation, and offers, tongue firmly in cheek, to represent him should it come to that.

The episode closes with the Rumpoles' second honeymoon finally allowed to resume on its own terms: Rumpole, having routed the judge not with legal argument this time but with an old barrister's memory for faces and cases, proposes splitting a bottle of wine and smashing a few plates with Hilda, declaring, with some relief, that there is absolutely no danger of his own wife ever going missing.
