Rumpole of the Bailey

S02E06: Rumpole and the Age for Retirement

Elderly Percy Timson is accused of receiving stolen property; his family set up the crime to force his retirement -- and pressure Rumpole to retire too.

Tuesday, 3 July 1979 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 3 July 1979 (Series 2), directed by Donald McWhinnie, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23). A fragment of a nursery rhyme ("teddy bears have their picnic") bleeding into the very last line of dialogue appears to be stray background radio/music picked up by the transcription rather than spoken dialogue, and is treated as such below.

The episode opens with a new, zealous Detective Inspector Brush raiding the garage of veteran fence Percy Timson and finding a stolen Italian Renaissance painting -- a work by the fourteenth-century master Taddeo di Bartolo, nicknamed "Il Zoppo" (the lame one) -- worth perhaps half a million pounds. In an unrelated case already underway, Rumpole loses a trial defending art thief Melvin Glassworth, a fastidious, self-pitying client horrified at the prospect of prison hygiene, who tells Rumpole bluntly that it's time he retired. Outside court, an American law professor from the University of Baltimore, Julius Kramer, introduces himself to Rumpole with starstruck admiration, mentions that he knows Rumpole's son Nick (who teaches sociology at the same university), and hints at an invitation to come lecture in America -- a seed that will grow across the episode into a full-blown retirement subplot.

At home, Hilda is delighted by a letter from Professor Kramer formally inviting Rumpole (and "his good lady") to Baltimore to lecture during the autumn semester, and by a phone call from Nick himself, who is flying over to visit and who, it becomes clear, has engineered the whole invitation as a pretext to lure his parents to America permanently. Hilda has already quietly put their flat on the market, reasoning that once Rumpole sees Nick's new house with its swimming pool and barbecue, he'll simply stay; she visits Phillida Trant (now married to Erskine Brown and recently delivered of a baby boy, Tristan, over whom Erskine Brown is comically, excessively proud) to share the retirement plan in confidence, insisting Rumpole is "desperately tired" and that everyone retires eventually. Phillida, who knows Rumpole rather better, predicts privately that he's not tired at all -- he's just started having fun again.

Rumpole takes on Percy Timson's defence, the latest episode in the Timson family's decades-long reliance on him, with Nick tagging along to watch his father work (echoing childhood trips to the Old Bailey with his own grandfather). Percy's signed "confession" turns out to have been extracted by Detective Inspector Brush through a bluff -- falsely telling Percy that his wife Noreen had already been arrested and would be charged unless he cooperated -- which Rumpole exposes devastatingly in cross-examination, forcing Brush to admit he had no actual intention of charging Noreen and had simply abandoned the threat once the confession was signed. The judge, unmoved, reminds Rumpole that regardless of how the confession was obtained, the painting was still found in Percy's own garage, leaving the defence with real work still to do.

Seeking expert insight, Rumpole visits Melvin Glassworth in prison -- ostensibly to discuss possible grounds of appeal Rumpole has spotted in the judge's summing-up, but really to pick Glassworth's professional brain as a genuine art thief. Glassworth confirms that a painting as famous as a di Bartolo would be essentially impossible to sell, and that any sensible thief caught with such a thing would simply "dump it" somewhere anonymous and untraceable -- a municipal rubbish tip, for instance, with perhaps an anonymous tip to the insurance company afterward. This throwaway piece of criminal expertise becomes the key to the whole case once Rumpole learns, from a sympathetic family member, Fred Timson, that the wider Timson clan has grown fed up with old Percy: he's been careless lately, undercutting prices, gossiping in pubs, and got young Jim Timson six months in prison through loose talk. The family, Fred admits, decided among themselves that Percy was simply "past it" as a fence and needed to be eased into retirement.

Confronting the family directly at the Timsons' house, Rumpole pieces together the plot: Cyril Timson, the one clean-record member of the family who works as a crane driver on the municipal rubbish tip, found the di Bartolo (apparently dumped there by whoever actually stole it) and planted it in Uncle Percy's garage using a spare key, then anonymously tipped off the police -- engineering Percy's arrest as a backhanded, risk-free way of forcing the "menace" of an aging fence out of the business entirely. Rumpole lays the scheme out point by point to a visibly uncomfortable Fred and Cyril, accusing the family of using Percy's own ignorance of fine art against him and threatening that if the case goes to trial as planned, Percy will be forced into the witness box to describe, under oath, the family's entire history of hot carpets, stolen scampi, and fenced cutlery -- implicating everyone, not just Percy. He leaves them to "think quick," since Percy is due to give evidence the next day, without the transcript showing how, or whether, the family resolves it before trial.

A parallel subplot threads through chambers: word spreads, via Hilda's visit to Phillida and thence to Marigold Featherstone and the wider chambers grapevine, that Rumpole is retiring to America, and Guthrie Featherstone -- genuinely moved, or at least performing being moved -- begins organizing a leaving dinner at his club, the Sheridan, complete with a presentation gift (after debating a loving cup versus a clock, they settle on the clock). At the club beforehand, Judge Frobisher runs into a fellow member, an MP and QC, whose son Simon has just finished pupillage and is angling for a seat in chambers -- and Erskine Brown, overhearing, mentions that there may soon be a vacancy, since Rumpole is apparently retiring. Detective Inspector Brush, encountered repeatedly through the episode, needles Rumpole about his age and imminent retirement at every turn, inventing a mocking fictional villa ("Rumpole's Retreat") that Rumpole deflects by naming it, instead, "La Morte" -- a property he expects to take possession of only when he's dead.

At the Sheridan Club dinner, with Hilda, Nick, Judge Frobisher, the Erskine Browns (baby Tristan parked next door for the evening), Uncle Tom, and the Featherstones all assembled, Guthrie delivers a slightly maudlin toast comparing Rumpole to "a valued antique" chambers will only truly miss once he's gone, and presents the engraved clock as a token of the chambers' affection. Rumpole, visibly moved and quoting Tennyson's "Ulysses" throughout the episode (lines about an "aged wife," unequal laws dealt out to "a savage race," and the resolve "not to yield"), admits he has indeed been tempted lately, amid the police's relentless attacks on the profession and his own creeping sense of being stuck in a rut, to "jack it all in" and retire to grass.

But the gift itself reverses the decision rather than confirming it: Rumpole announces, to general chambers bewilderment, that the dinner, the clock, and everyone's evident affection have changed his mind entirely -- he will not be retiring after all, and the clock will instead ensure he is never late to court again, at the Old Bailey, the London Sessions, Luton Crown Court, or even before the Uxbridge magistrates. The episode ends on Guthrie Featherstone's baffled closing line, unable to understand why Horace Rumpole is "hanging on to our clock" rather than sailing off into the sunset his son and wife had so carefully arranged for him -- Nick's whole scheme to relocate his parents to Baltimore left unresolved and, by every sign in the final scene, quietly abandoned.
