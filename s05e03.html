Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 5, Episode 3

Rumpole and the Age of Miracles

Hilda's nephew, a canon accused of adultery, is defended in an ecclesiastical court.

Aired Wednesday, 7 December 1988 · ITV (Thames Television)

The episode opens in a cathedral, where a sceptical, plain-spoken clergyman preaches that the Church should not need "conjuring tricks" to prove God's goodness, a sermon that turns out to belong to Canon Timothy Donkin, a distant nephew of Rumpole's whom he has not seen in years. Back in chambers, Rumpole discovers that Head of Chambers Sam Ballard QC has been handed a new and absurdly inflated sense of his own importance: Ballard has been appointed Worshipful Chancellor of the Diocese of Launchester, a lay judicial post in the ecclesiastical courts open only to communicant members of the Church of England, and he insists this is no occasion for Rumpole's usual frivolity, however tempting the jokes about unfrocking priests and burning heretics over the weekend may be. That evening, Timothy (known to the family as Timmy) turns up unannounced at the Rumpoles' house to ask his "Uncle Horace" for legal help: six of his parishioners in the cathedral city of Launchester have lodged a formal complaint with the bishop accusing him of "old-fashioned adultery," on the strength of a chambermaid's evidence that she saw him admit a woman to his room at the St. Edithna Hotel, where he habitually takes a room to write his sermons away from his own noisy household and his two sons, Martin and Erasmus. Hilda, intrigued and faintly scandalized, recalls that the Donkin family has always had "bad blood" in it, pointedly mentioning a disgraced relative named Wendy who once went to prison, a detail she insists nobody ever talks about, even as she brings it up herself.

Rumpole travels down to Launchester to take the case, discovering along the way that Claude Erskine Brown has been engaged, at Ballard's suggestion, to prosecute on behalf of the Archdeacon, and that Ballard himself, as the newly minted Chancellor, will sit as judge, with lay assessors from the diocese standing in for a jury. At the St. Edithna Hotel (named, a tour guide explains, for an early Christian martyr falsely accused of immorality and stoned to death on the very spot where the hotel now stands, and said to "walk" whenever a great injustice is done nearby), Rumpole meets his instructing solicitor Frank Marlin, who identifies the six accusers bringing the ecclesiastical prosecution: a bank manager, a retired admiral, the formidable Mrs. Elphick (chairman of the local bench), a lay-preaching chemist, and the estate agent Peter Lambert and his wife Cynthia. Marlin notes, intriguingly, that all six have a shared financial interest in a controversial new supermarket development Lambert is planning on cathedral-adjacent land, a detail Rumpole files away. That evening, over an incongruously solemn dinner and drinks at which Rumpole, Ballard (now "the Chancellor"), and Erskine Brown all agree repeatedly not to discuss the pending case while plainly doing exactly that, Rumpole plants the legend of St. Edithna in Ballard's impressionable, newly devout mind, needling him about miracles, injustice, and the ghost said to walk the hotel corridors when wrongs go unpunished.

When Rumpole visits Timothy at home to press him about the chambermaid's evidence, the canon simply refuses to discuss what she actually saw, declaring that God will be his judge, a stance Rumpole bluntly points out will not help him much in front of Chancellor Ballard, who is eager for his first real case. Timothy mentions, almost in passing, that his historical research into the cathedral's archives (conducted, inconveniently for his defence, in a hotel bedroom rather than the chattering cathedral library) has turned up something interesting: evidence of a gift of land to the cathedral from the Crown in 1672. At the ecclesiastical court the next morning, Erskine Brown opens the prosecution by calling hotel manager Thomas Campion, who confirms that Canon Donkin's usual room, number 39, has twin beds two feet apart that could in theory be pushed together, though Rumpole's cross-examination establishes that Donkin never requested anything larger and that the hotel, having been renovated, in fact has no single rooms left to offer at all. The more damaging witness is chambermaid Rita O'Keefe, who testifies that on her afternoon off she watched a thin, red-haired woman in a grey suit let into room 39 through the emergency staircase, waited three-quarters of an hour outside, and afterward found lipstick-stained cigarette ends in the ashtray.

Rumpole's cross-examination of Miss O'Keefe quickly exposes the catch: she was not simply loitering out of curiosity on her day off, but was paid ten pounds by a gentleman to keep watch on the canon specifically, and when pressed to identify him, she points directly at Peter Lambert, sitting in the public seats. This throws the ecclesiastical court into disarray; Erskine Brown, visibly blindsided by his own witness's revelation, asks for an adjournment to take instructions, and Rumpole confronts Lambert in the corridor. He lays out the real motive behind the prosecution: Lambert's planned supermarket development sits on the very parcel of cathedral land that Canon Donkin's archival research suggests was granted to the cathedral by Charles II in 1672 and has never legitimately left church ownership since, meaning that if Donkin's history is published, Lambert and his fellow accusers (several of whom, Rumpole establishes, have also invested in the development) stand to lose their investment entirely. Unfrocking the troublesome, historically-minded canon on a trumped-up morals charge, Rumpole suggests, is simply the cheapest way to silence him before he can finish his research.

That night, pursuing a separate line of enquiry through a contact at the police criminal records computer, Rumpole learns the true identity of the mystery woman: she is Timothy's own sister, Wendy Donkin, convicted of fraud and false pretenses in 1986, released on licence, and now wanted for ten further offences involving stolen cheques and credit cards: the very family skeleton Hilda had vaguely alluded to back in London. Confronting Timothy in his hotel room, Rumpole deduces that Wendy, desperate for money and avoiding arrest, had arranged to meet her brother secretly via the emergency staircase, and that Timothy gave her his word he would never reveal to anyone that he had seen her. When Rumpole urges him to put this complete, exculpatory explanation before the court the next morning, Timothy flatly refuses: having given his promise to Wendy, he will not break it to save his own reputation, however much this leaves him looking guilty, and he accepts the prospect of being unfrocked rather than expose his fugitive sister. Rumpole, exasperated at being handed a client with the inconvenient scruples of an actual saint, nonetheless resolves to win the case without Timothy's cooperation.

Meanwhile, Hilda arrives unannounced at the St. Edithna Hotel bar, having convinced herself, not unreasonably given Rumpole's history, that he has brought his pupil Liz Probert along for an improper weekend in a West Country hotel; finding Rumpole genuinely alone, she is somewhat deflated, admits she has come "to see you, Rumpole," and, it being too late to travel home that night, ends up staying over herself, with Rumpole dryly noting that the hotel's food, like his own jokes, is "not always in the very best of taste."

Back in the ecclesiastical court the following morning, with Timothy's real alibi still unspoken, Rumpole instead presses Lambert directly on the record about his financial stake in the disputed land and his admitted knowledge of Timothy's historical research, forcing him to concede, step by step, exactly why he and his fellow "guardians of public morality" wanted the canon disgraced and silenced. Chancellor Ballard, who has spent what he describes as "a sleepless night" wrestling with the case and openly admits to having prayed over it (invoking, by name, the spirit of St. Edithna and the need to avoid repeating her historical martyrdom), announces that, given the clear financial motive behind the prosecution and the real possibility that the chief witness invented her evidence for money, it would not be safe to let the case proceed any further on the evidence as it stands. He directs the assessors to acquit Timothy outright, all without ever learning the actual, far more mundane truth about Wendy Donkin that Rumpole has kept to himself throughout.

The episode closes back at the Rumpoles' house in London, where Hilda, a little abashed at having rushed down to Launchester under false suspicions, insists she was not in the least jealous and is relieved to find the household running as usual in her absence. Rumpole, well pleased with the outcome (an acquittal won without ever having to call the one witness who could have explained everything, and without his own client's cooperation), declares the case "a famous victory" and toasts "the age of miracles," noting dryly that it is "not past" after all, before raising his glass in a final, pointed joke to the blessed St. Edithna, "also known as She Who Must Be Obeyed."
