Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 1, Episode 3

Rumpole and the Honourable Member

1974 case: an MP on a rape charge; his self-incriminating testimony leads to conviction; Nick and Erica move to Baltimore.

Monday, 17 April 1978 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 17 April 1978 (Series 1), directed by Graham Evans, written by John Mortimer.

In chambers one morning, Horace Rumpole mutters a half-remembered line of verse about enforced chastity to himself before clerk Henry hands him a brief: a rape case, and, unusually, a privately paying one, not legal aid. Albert the chief clerk and Erskine Brown trade the morning's usual needling (Erskine Brown, devoted to "moderately distasteful" civil work, complains about typing errors and a backlog Albert can't clear), while Rumpole jokes about Albert's long-serving instructing-solicitor's clerk, Mr. Myers, who grows prize tomatoes and has evidently touted this brief Rumpole's way. Rumpole needles Erskine Brown for never having "done a rape" and collects his monthly chambers-expenses statement from Albert with the usual theatrical grumbling about shared costs. That night at home, Hilda greets a late-arriving Rumpole (he recites Shakespeare's Sonnet 57 at her half in earnest, half as a dodge) with news that their son Nick is coming to dinner tomorrow with his American fiancee. Between recipe instructions for Hilda's pork dish, the identity of Rumpole's new client slips out: a Labour backbench MP, married with two children, accused of rape, "an honourable member."

The next day in chambers, Rumpole takes instructions from the MP, Ken Aspin, and his wife Anna. Anna, fiercely ambitious for her husband's career and certain the whole thing is a misunderstanding, wants Rumpole to broker some "reasonable compromise" with the complainant, Bridget Evans; Rumpole tells her flatly that there is no halfway house in a rape charge, either Aspin did it or he didn't, and sends her off shopping so he can hear Ken's account alone. Aspin's story: working late at the constituency office, Bridget called him into the next room claiming the duplicating machine was jammed, then locked the door behind him. She accused him of betraying his socialist principles and, eventually, of being afraid to leave his wife Anna for her. In the heat of the row he "made love to her," believing, he says, that it was what she wanted. He also recalls a campaign poster of his own face, defaced on the office wall; Rumpole fixes at once on whether Aspin saw that vandalised poster *before* the encounter (which would suggest premeditated malice on Bridget's part) or only noticed it afterward, and drills him to be certain of the sequence.

That evening Nick arrives for dinner with his fiancee, the bright, idealistic American sociology student Erica ("Ricky"), fresh from Baltimore. Hilda steers the table talk toward which university job Nick should take, a lectureship at Warwick, convenient for the family, versus staying in America, while Rumpole deflects Erica's earnest questions about his "motivation" for defending the underprivileged with a flat "money," insisting he'd fall as silent as the tomb without a legal-aid cheque. Erica, charmed and curious, asks to come and watch him in court; Rumpole, half against his better judgment, invites her and Nick down to the Bailey the following Monday for lunch and "some fun."

Back in chambers, Rumpole goes digging for ammunition on Bridget Evans. A colleague, Janice, dismisses her as "a bloody Trot," a Trotskyite troublemaker and former agitator, and lets slip that Bridget had a long love affair with Ken Aspin's own parliamentary agent, Paul Eddington, which ended badly. Mr. Myers, working his solicitor's-clerk network, digs up still more: a teenage history of lovers, a legal abortion, a psychiatric certificate for "emotional instability," and a more recent overdose of sleeping tablets after the Eddington breakup, all material Rumpole intends to use. In a parallel subplot, Erskine Brown corners Rumpole with "proof" that chief clerk Albert has been skimming the petty cash: a marked five-pound note, deliberately crossed in red ink and planted, that Erskine Brown traced to Albert's bar tab at Pomeroy's. Rumpole deflects on the spot by claiming he himself had borrowed that very note from petty cash to buy Albert's round, a lie, as it turns out, that Erskine Brown later disproves with a cancelled cheque recovered from the wine bar's own landlord, Jack Pomeroy.

In Court Number One, Rumpole cross-examines Bridget Evans with the kind of ruthlessness he half-apologizes for even as he deploys it. He presses her on why she told no one for nearly twenty-four hours, then pivots to her private life: the years living with Paul Eddington from the age of not-quite-eighteen, the abortion, the psychiatric diagnosis, the sleeping-tablet overdose. He forces her to admit she met Ken Aspin shortly after that breakdown, and, piece by piece, gets her to concede that she, not Aspin, defaced the campaign poster of his face that hung on the office wall, though the question of exactly when she did it (before the encounter, suggesting premeditated spite, or after, in anger) is left hanging rather than cleanly resolved. The trial judge, addressed familiarly by Rumpole as "Sam" (Sam Parkin), tries once to shield the witness from the raking-over, with limited success; a prosecuting barrister dryly observes that Rumpole is deploying his old "Alhambra cinema technique."

That evening at Pomeroy's Wine Bar, Rumpole tries to celebrate the day's progress with Nick and Erica, and introduces them to head of chambers Guthrie Featherstone QC, MP, and to the elderly Uncle Tom. Erica, having just watched the cross-examination, is horrified rather than entertained: she accuses Rumpole of using a rape trial to publicly degrade a woman's sexual history for sport, calls his "doubt" tactics a "lousy argument" dressed up as fair play, and tells him flatly that if his client isn't innocent, he'd "better be." The row escalates into a genuine argument about consent, female equality, and the adversarial system itself; Erica storms off upset, with Nick trailing after her, and the warm family evening Rumpole had hoped for collapses.

Later, in a quiet, down-at-heel cafe, Rumpole coaches an exhausted Ken Aspin ahead of his own testimony, hammering home the two points that matter: that he saw the defaced poster as soon as he entered the room, and that he genuinely believed Bridget wanted what happened. Aspin, worn down, drifts instead into a rambling, almost confessional account of how devotedly Bridget had worked for the party, how hard his wife Anna had pushed his career, and admits, when Rumpole asks what he himself had wanted out of any of it, simply: "I wanted it to stop." The next day in court, that fatigue shows: under Rumpole's own gentle questioning Aspin can no longer confirm when he saw the poster, concedes he "made love to her" in anger, and admits he doesn't really know anymore what he believed she wanted: answers that visibly damage his own defence.

Back in chambers, the Albert affair comes to a head at a formal meeting convened by Featherstone after Erskine Brown exposes Rumpole's earlier lie about the marked fiver. Rumpole, cornered, admits outright that he lied to protect Albert, and launches into an impassioned defence of him: forty years' service, a man who "goes out and grubs for briefs" the gentlemanly barristers are too proud to chase themselves, guilty of nothing worse than small, almost affectionate pilfering that Rumpole likens to a mark of respect. Erskine Brown is unmoved and moves that the matter go to the police; Uncle Tom, while opposing any police involvement as ungentlemanly, nonetheless proposes immediate dismissal instead, and the chambers votes Albert out on the spot. Rumpole's bitter closing remark, an old anecdote about a condemned man whose own barrister said nothing in his defence, lands as a eulogy for a friend, not a colleague.

The rape trial concludes off-page: Rumpole mentions almost in passing, over a drink at Pomeroy's with a newly arrived Henry (Albert's replacement as chief clerk) and later with Nick, that "the honourable member got five years." Nick drops by to say a different kind of goodbye: he and Erica have decided, after all, to take the job in Baltimore rather than Warwick, partly because Erica's close-knit American family wants her nearer home. Rumpole, needled gently, warns Nick only half-jokingly about the dangers of American "hygiene" and "purity." The episode closes the next morning at the Rumpoles' breakfast table: a postcard arrives from Nick and Erica, and Hilda reveals the real reason behind Erica's hasty departure from England: she'd told Hilda by telephone that she found Rumpole's courtroom questioning of Bridget Evans "distasteful." Asked by Hilda what he intends to do with his day, Rumpole answers without much contrition that he supposes he'll "go on asking distasteful questions," closing the episode on that unresolved, faintly weary note rather than on any resolution of the MP's fate or the family rift it exposed.
