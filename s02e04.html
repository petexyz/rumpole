Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 2, Episode 4

Rumpole and the Fascist Beast

A retired captain is charged under the Race Relations Act; Rumpole has taken on a Punjabi pupil barrister.

Tuesday, 19 June 1979 · ITV (Thames Television)

The episode opens on a public hall rally where Captain Rex Parkin, in full oratorical flight for a far-right party, demands that "the African," "the Indian," and "the native-born Englishman" each be kept to their own kind, invokes an elderly widow in Brixton too frightened to leave her home, denounces the police as lackeys of a government that "crawls" to immigrant communities, and climaxes with a single shouted word: "Blood!" At the Rumpole house that evening, Hilda's enthusiasm over wartime-nostalgia rationing-style cooking (tinned mulligatawny soup from the local Indian shopkeeper, Chatterjee) sits in pointed contrast to the news bulletin Rumpole has just watched, and to Hilda's own casual complaints about the multicultural crowd outside the tube station, undercut by Rumpole's gentle reminder that her own "great-aunt Fran," married to a colonial railway official in the Punjab, would have felt entirely at home there. Hilda is also expecting Rumpole's new pupil to dinner, assuming, as it turns out, wrongly, that having a pupil at all is simply a nice, ordinary thing.

The pupil in question is Latif Khan, a keen, formally polite young barrister newly arrived in chambers, to visible bemusement from Uncle Tom and Erskine Brown, who mutter about pupillage-committee procedure and reminisce (not entirely comfortably) about a long-ago chambers connection to Gandhi. Rumpole installs Khan in his room just as his new client arrives: Captain Parkin himself, charged under Section 5B of the Race Relations Act 1976 for his speech, which carries a maximum of two years or a fine or both. Parkin, far from contesting the charge, wants Rumpole to ask the judge for the maximum sentence, modelling himself explicitly on Gandhi's demand for life imprisonment before a British magistrate as a act of martyrdom for the cause, a comparison Rumpole punctures by noting that Gandhi was "of foreign extraction." Rumpole refuses point-blank to ask any judge to imprison his own client, calling it "against my religion," and Parkin accordingly threatens to conduct his own defence.

That plan collapses almost immediately: at Parkin's bungalow that night, his own party colleagues, worried about optics ahead of an election and pointedly noting that Rumpole's "immigrant assistant" photographs well as proof the party isn't racist, make it clear the unnamed "leader" wants the case fought all the way, as something close to an order. Parkin, ever the dutiful soldier, falls immediately into line. Meanwhile in chambers, Guthrie Featherstone reveals he will prosecute the case himself with Phillida Trant as his junior, and privately warns Rumpole, citing a contact at the Lord Chancellor's office, that defending "this wretched fascist beast" could cost him any future hope of a seat on the circuit bench; Rumpole brushes this off with a pointed paraphrase of Voltaire on the right to say unpopular things, which he suggests Featherstone might like to read to the jury himself.

Dinner at the Rumpoles' brings out an uncomfortable irony: Khan, treated to roast beef and Hilda's nostalgic stories of the Raj, turns out to hold harsher, more punitive views than his pupil-master, volunteering that two Pakistani students in his lodgings he suspects of theft should be "sent inside double quick," and that prosecution work, not defence, is "the path to the bench nowadays." Hilda, delighted, enlists Khan as an ally in gently pushing Rumpole toward a judgeship Rumpole insists he will never, "in a million years," accept. In court, Judge Jameson, an old schoolfellow of Rumpole's from "Birkenshaw" (the pair trade school nicknames and reminiscences throughout, including Jameson's eagerness to finish the case quickly for a week's fishing), presides over a trial that turns, almost comically, on a single ambiguous phrase: did Parkin say "the answer is blood" (an incitement to violence, as Featherstone argues) or "the answer is in the blood" (merely an opinion about racial difference, as Rumpole argues), a distinction the arresting sergeant, amid the scuffle and shouting at the rally, admits he cannot be sure of.

Alongside the trial, a second, quieter plot develops: Phillida Trant, recalling her own rocky start in chambers as the first woman tenant, tries to extend solidarity to Khan as a fellow outsider navigating institutional prejudice, including a joke about being fobbed off over access to the lavatory key, but Khan rebuffs the overture firmly, telling her his father sent him to England specifically to avoid "alliances" and that he intends to keep his distance from any factional loyalties in chambers. Erskine Brown, for his part, spends the episode failing to find anyone willing to accompany him to Verdi's Un Ballo in Maschera at Covent Garden: Phillida won't skip trial preparation for it, the clerks' room secretary Diane has a Chinese dinner with her boyfriend instead, and Featherstone only agrees once he learns his wife Marigold is "frightfully keen on Verdi."

In his closing speech, Rumpole leans fully into the Voltairean defence of free speech for views he does not himself share, painting Parkin not as a dangerous agitator but as a faintly absurd, essentially harmless dreamer, a man who has never travelled further east than Beaulieu on a day trip, who flies the Union Jack over a suburban bungalow named "Mandalay" and lowers it ceremonially at sunset, and whose closest brush with the Raj is a weekly curry house dinner with his wife, Mavis. He invokes his own Scottish ancestry and a line of Robert Burns to argue that "the answer is in the blood" is a claim he himself would shrug off if said of him, and leaves the jury with the proposition that a genuinely free country has to tolerate its eccentrics and bigots alike, not merely the opinions it already agrees with. The jury acquits Parkin of incitement to racial hatred, with Judge Jameson issuing only a mild warning about being careful how one expresses views that "may be misunderstood."

The victory curdles immediately for Parkin himself: back at his own bungalow, his party colleagues denounce him for the "yellow streak" of letting his own barrister mock the party and its creed in open court and in the national press, and vote overwhelmingly, over his wife Mavis's oblivious offer of coffee and biscuits, to strip him of his chairmanship of the Southeast Area Party in favour of a more "dynamic" successor who declares the days of the British Raj formally over. Parkin, utterly defeated, tells Rumpole he will never forgive him for winning.

Back in chambers, a vacancy left by George Frobisher's elevation to the circuit bench prompts a tenancy debate between a Welsh solicitor-connected applicant, Owen Glendower-Jones, and Khan himself; Phillida bluntly calls out the chambers meeting's euphemisms about Khan being a "special case," drawing an explicit parallel to her own treatment as chambers' first woman tenant, while Uncle Tom, in his own doddering way, actually offers the most substantively positive assessment of Khan's work. Chambers votes Khan in, but when Rumpole delivers the happy news that night, Khan declines: he has been offered a place with Treasury Counsel, with a direct line to the Director of Public Prosecutions, and admits plainly that Rumpole's style of advocacy, laughing at the system even while using it, makes him uneasy, and that what frightens him about people like Parkin losing isn't Parkin himself but whoever might come after him. Khan leaves chambers for the prosecution side, exactly where he told Hilda at dinner he wanted to be all along.

The episode closes on the private, half-serious question Rumpole has been turning over all episode: should he actually accept elevation to the bench? He talks himself through both sides of it at home, the loneliness and moral weight of sentencing people, set against the ease, the pension, and the possibility of occasionally doing good by sentencing a defendant to "time already served," before going to the Lord Chancellor's office to tell an official named Keith that he's "thought it over most carefully" and is "not totally opposed." The scene plays as a judgeship interview until the punchline lands: Keith isn't offering Rumpole a judicial appointment at all, only the unpaid secretaryship of the Old Birkenshavians at the Bar Society, an alumni dinner club, Rumpole's own old school connection to Judge Jameson having apparently been the only qualification anyone actually had in mind.
