Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 3, Episode 4

Rumpole and the Female of the Species

Anthony Timson is accused of bank robbery; Phyllida takes silk and Rumpole becomes her junior.

Tuesday, 1 November 1983 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 1 November 1983 (Series 3), directed by Donald McWhinnie, written by John Mortimer.

The episode opens with Detective Inspector Brush calling on small-time villain Tony Timson at home, needling him about a handsome new stereo and the recent Pond Hill bank job before Tony is duly arrested. Back in chambers, Rumpole discovers the Timson brief has been pointedly marked not for him but for Phillida Erskine Brown (the former Phillida Trant) -- fallout, Henry explains, from Rumpole having called the instructing solicitor Bernard a "villainous viper" (quoting Shakespeare's Richard II at him) after Bernard advised a Rumpole client to plead guilty at Uxbridge Magistrates' Court while Rumpole himself was stuck on the Tube. At home, this becomes a running irritant with Hilda, who is needling Rumpole both about his thin workload and his botched attempt at putting up a shelf without plugging the wall first -- a small, pointed emblem, as the episode unfolds, of a man who trusts instinct over instructions and often gets it wrong.

Rumpole is also keeping a friendly eye on Fiona Allways, Phillida's young pupil, whom he coaches (not entirely seriously) on closing speeches even for a petty shoplifting case over six frozen chicken pieces. The question of whether Fiona will be kept on permanently comes to a head at a chambers meeting under the new head of chambers, Sam Ballard, where Ballard and Claude Erskine Brown -- newly and smugly revealed to have applied for silk himself -- argue against taking on another woman, citing vague doubts about her resilience and chambers' need for solicitor-attracting "well-established" men rather than, as Rumpole puts it, "a token woman." Rumpole needles them both for hypocrisy, recalling how recently it was Phillida herself fighting the same battle against entrenched chauvinism, and maneuvers a stalling tactic -- inventing, on the spot, a nonexistent chambers rule that entry questions are only ever decided "on the last Thursday in Hilary term" -- to buy Fiona more time before the vote goes against her.

Because of the Bernard rift, it's Phillida, not Rumpole, who first visits Tony Timson in Brixton to take his instructions on the robbery charge, with Rumpole eventually brought in only as her junior. Tony, visibly uncomfortable being represented by "a woman," voices old-fashioned unease that criminal defense is "man's work," while Phillida brushes off his chauvinism and gets down to the facts: an accomplice, Jerry Malloy, has turned Queen's evidence and named Tony as the getaway driver on the Pond Hill job, in exchange for a sharply reduced sentence, despite the long-standing mutual contempt between the Malloy and Timson families. Separately, at Pomeroy's wine bar, Erskine Brown complains to Rumpole that he barely sees his increasingly successful wife these days -- an opening Rumpole exploits for his own purposes, planting the mischievous idea in Erskine Brown's head that young Fiona Allways finds him personally alluring, calculating that male vanity, flattered, will soften his opposition to her admission.

Rumpole runs an identical manipulation on Sam Ballard, hinting by telephone that the supposedly devious and ambitious Phillida had deliberately goaded Ballard into exposing his own sexist attitudes at the chambers meeting for a damaging Bar Council report, and separately that she has "taken something of a shine" to him. The ploy backfires hilariously: a flattered, unmarried Ballard turns up in Phillida's room late one evening to assure her, at length and with excruciating awkwardness, that he finds women in wig and winged collar "surprisingly alluring" and invites her to lunch at a hotel -- a proposition Phillida reports to Rumpole in disgust as "the most disgusting suggestion." Meanwhile, in the corridors of the court, April Timson -- Tony's wife -- quietly raises her own concerns about the case with Rumpole, mentioning that their young son Vincent has been staying with her friend Chrissie, who turns out, tellingly, to be an estranged in-law of the very Malloy family now informing against Tony.

At trial before the brisk, almost machine-like Judge Dover (nicknamed "Bluebird"), Rumpole objects furiously when the judge warns the jury, before any evidence is heard, to be alert to approaches from "serious professional criminals" -- language Rumpole argues has already prejudicially "nobbled" the jury against a man merely accused, not convicted, and whose real criminal record is for minor burglary and receiving rather than armed robbery. His application for a fresh jury is refused. Jerry Malloy then testifies under prosecution questioning that Tony Timson drove the getaway car, naming him without hesitation; under Phillida's cross-examination, however, Malloy -- caught in the gutter after tripping over his own hold-all, sentenced to two years, and clearly buying leniency by informing -- is forced to concede that the Malloys and Timsons despise each other, that no Timson has ever before been trusted on a job as big as a bank raid, and that most of his own Malloy relatives involved in the robbery have conveniently vanished rather than be traced by police. Phillida presses hard on whether it was a Malloy family member, perhaps via Malloy's own wife's prison visits, who planted the stolen cash in the Timsons' house to frame Tony; Detective Inspector Brush, cross-examined next, admits the police arrived at the Timsons' with suspicious speed after Malloy's statement, though he denies any sign that Tony had been caught scrambling to hide anything.

In a wry, domestic cross-examination of Tony himself, Phillida and Rumpole establish, almost as a joke, that Tony never touches housework, cooking, or the washing machine -- all strictly April's domain -- which becomes quietly significant once it's clear the stolen money was found stashed near that same washing machine, in a hiding place Tony would never have thought to use or to check. Rumpole, piecing this together in the corridor, privately confronts April Timson directly: he suggests that she herself, not Tony, was the actual getaway driver that day, filling in for a sick Malloy relative, and that she hid her own cut of the money somewhere she knew her husband would never look -- and that the Malloys, having used a woman driver, could never admit to it without humiliating themselves, so blame fell on the family they already despised. April neither confirms nor denies it outright, daring Rumpole to prove it; he lets the matter drop with a pointed, self-aware remark about leaving "man's work" to the men to make a mess of, and hopes it was the first and last time.

Back in chambers, Erskine Brown is blindsided to find his wife's name, not his own, on the newly published list of appointed Queen's Counsel -- Phillida never told him she had even applied. At trial the next morning, Phillida startles Rumpole by announcing, mid-case, that she has taken silk and is now his leader rather than his equal; she delivers a closing speech for Tony Timson that pointedly echoes, almost word for word, the "this case is but a small part of your lives, but for my client it is the whole of his life" speech Rumpole had earlier taught Fiona Allways to use on a frozen-chicken shoplifter -- and the jury, with the Malloy evidence thoroughly undermined and no proof the money got there any way but the one the defense suggests, is left to return what the episode strongly implies is an acquittal.

Afterward, Phillida admits to Rumpole that she applied for silk in a fit of jealous anger after finding Claude and Fiona apparently cozy together at Pomeroy's -- a scene Rumpole had engineered by planting exactly that impression in Erskine Brown's head. Confronted, Rumpole owns up cheerfully to having manipulated both Ballard and Erskine Brown through invented flattery to tilt the odds in Fiona's favor, defending himself to an exasperated Phillida on the grounds that a woman shouldn't have to rely solely on her own talent to get ahead when the system is already stacked in men's favor -- "you have to rely and manipulate male vanity." Grudgingly amused rather than genuinely angry, Phillida agrees to vote for Fiona's permanent place in chambers, and accepts Rumpole's suggested cover story for Claude's wounded pride: that she was appointed silk only because the Lord Chancellor's office wanted more women QCs, not on pure merit -- a face-saving fiction Rumpole cheerfully concedes "is possible, isn't it?"

At home, Hilda reveals she has quietly, competently repaired the shelf Rumpole himself had botched -- "You get a man in, did you?" "Yes. Me." -- a small coda to the episode's recurring joke about who actually gets things done. The final chambers party doubles as a celebration of both triumphs: Ballard pompously congratulates chambers on its enlightened lack of prejudice against women while welcoming Phillida's promotion and Fiona's confirmed place in chambers, as Rumpole impishly needles a still-smarting Erskine Brown with the "quota" story about his wife's silk gown. Erskine Brown reveals Phillida has promised to cut back on work and that the couple is expecting a second child; Rumpole welcomes Fiona warmly to the set, undercutting his own feminist maneuvering slightly by advising her, half in earnest, never to wear trousers to court -- "a fellow looks so much better in a skirt," Fiona agrees, with a smile that leaves the joke pointed back at him. The episode closes the next morning with the whole of chambers, Ballard and Erskine Brown included, falling over themselves to greet the newly elevated "Mrs Erskine Brown" with exaggerated deference in the corridor.
