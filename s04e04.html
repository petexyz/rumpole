Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 4, Episode 4

Rumpole and the Judge's Elbow

A doctor is accused of running a disorderly massage parlour; Judge Featherstone's secret visit complicates the case.

Monday, 9 February 1987 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 9 February 1987 (Series 4), directed by Donald McWhinnie, written by John Mortimer.

On the squash court, Judge Guthrie Featherstone takes a painful knock to the elbow playing against his usher Norman, and the pain refuses to fade. Norman, solicitous, recommends a "very hygienically run" health centre near Tottenham Court Road and a particular masseuse, Elsie, assuring the judge the girls there are all "medically trained," a claim that does not survive much scrutiny once Featherstone actually visits, finds himself asked to "take his things off," and gets a perfectly innocent, if faintly absurd, massage for an elbow he insists came from tennis rather than squash. Back in chambers, the modernizing new tenant Charles Hearthstoke is busy with efficiency studies (threatening clerk Henry's traditional 10% commission with a privatization scheme, eyeing typist Diane for replacement by someone "more upmarket," and pushing dress-code reforms), all with the enthusiastic backing of Head of Chambers Sam Ballard QC, to Rumpole's and Uncle Tom's considerable disgust.

Rumpole takes on the defence of Dr. Morris Horwich, proprietor of the "True Line" chain of health centres (some thirty-five branches across Greater London, in fact), charged with running the establishments as disorderly houses and living off immoral earnings. Horwich's "doctorate," it emerges, is in theology, obtained entirely by mail-order correspondence course, and his defence, that his staff are all "spiritually trained" and he had no idea any of them engaged in "hanky-panky" with clients, strikes even Rumpole as thin. What makes the case deliciously dangerous, from Rumpole's point of view, is the client list: the prosecution's evidence includes the names of numerous respectable, married clients ("big wigs," he tells anyone who'll listen) foolish enough to have paid for their visits by credit card, leaving a paper trail with their names on it for all to see.

That detail reaches Judge Featherstone with real alarm once he realizes his own American Express card may be sitting somewhere in that same evidence, not, as it turns out, from Horwich's chain at all, but from the unrelated establishment Norman had recommended for his elbow. Panicked, he rings American Express trying to quietly swap his card payment for cash and erase the record, then tries to track down Norman, only to learn from Norman's replacement, Harold, that Norman has suddenly left the judicial service for a job "up north." A visit to the health centre itself turns up nothing: the lease is up, the business has vanished entirely, "even the sauna." At home, Featherstone's wife Marigold notices he's become strange and anxious, musing aloud about retiring to run a little bar in Ibiza and asking, with theatrical unsubtlety, whether she would stand by him "through thick and thin," a question Marigold, tellingly, declines to answer with any confidence.

Hoping to smooth things over, Featherstone invites Rumpole to an expensive lunch at the Sheridan Club, fishing delicately for what exactly is in the prosecution evidence Rumpole has seen as defence counsel, and dangling, seemingly out of nowhere, the suggestion that Rumpole might like to be appointed an assistant recorder, a paid judicial sideline worth 150 pounds a day, as a "tribute" to his tact and discretion. Rumpole, oblivious to the judge's real motive, finds the whole idea of judging rather than defending absurd, but the offer plants a seed that Hilda, once she hears of it from Marigold at Harrods, seizes on with great enthusiasm, even buying herself a hat in case she ever gets to sit beside her husband on the bench. Marigold, for her part, is growing visibly uneasy about Guthrie's behaviour, and finds a strange kinship with Hilda in neither of them being quite sure their devotion runs to "thick and thin."

When the trial opens, Featherstone very nearly recuses himself, citing an undefined "private matter" (the lunch with defence counsel), but both Rumpole and prosecuting counsel Mr. Lampit insist he carry on rather than waste public money on a delayed retrial, so he reluctantly proceeds, now presiding over a case he has every personal reason to want handled delicately. In the judge's chambers, Featherstone leans hard on both sides to keep the trial short and keep credit-card evidence out of it altogether "to cut down on public spending"; Rumpole, cagily, declines to rule it out, noting that credit-card use might actually help prove a client's innocence, since no one in his right mind pays by card for an illicit massage, a line that happens to serve Featherstone's interests just as well as it serves Rumpole's client.

In court, Rumpole's cross-examination of the undercover Detective Constable Martin, who posed as a client with a faked knee injury, produces one of the episode's comic set pieces: Martin admits he lied about his injury, propositioned the masseuse himself with a crude request for "a bit of the other," and was quoted twenty pounds for it: evidence that cuts both ways on who really initiated the impropriety. Judge Featherstone intervenes repeatedly and conspicuously on the defence's behalf, steering the jury toward the idea that plenty of perfectly respectable, married men might visit such places for entirely legitimate sporting injuries, prompting Liz Probert to ask Rumpole, pointedly, why the judge seems to be "batting so strenuously" for their side; Rumpole credits his own irresistible charm. Rumpole's own earlier anxiety about a damning credit-card slip evaporates during the formal reading of the charge, when the clerk of the court correctly names the business as "True Line," not "True Life" as Rumpole had misheard, confirming that whatever establishment the judge actually visited, it isn't the one on trial, and Featherstone, at least as far as this indictment goes, is in the clear.

With that threat removed, Featherstone's sympathy for the defence noticeably cools, and Rumpole's own cross-examination of Dr. Horwich turns harder: pressed on whether he truly had no idea respectable, injured clients were being propositioned in his own health centres, Horwich can only manage an unconvincing "I suppose so, my lord," a concession Rumpole notes aloud will have registered with the jury, remarking to himself that "the wind has changed direction." Horwich is convicted and sentenced to two years. Meanwhile, a quieter subplot has Hearthstoke courting Liz Probert, an odd match given his Conservative council candidacy in Battersea against her father's socialist politics in North London, bonding, somewhat touchingly, over being young and wanting to change their respective worlds, which culminates in an invitation to share Chinese food after a late night spent together sorting through the case's credit-card records.

It is precisely those records that undo Hearthstoke. Rumpole, still combing through the prosecution's credit-card evidence looking for another name entirely, keeps running across Hearthstoke's own: regular weekly visits to a different branch of the same seedy network, the Battersea Health Depot. Confronted, Hearthstoke insists his visits were "entirely innocent," but the threat of exposure to Ballard is evidently enough. At the chambers party that follows (financed, pointedly, by Claude Erskine Brown), Ballard announces that Hearthstoke, after barely three months in chambers, is leaving for a commercial set in Middle Temple, citing his supposed distaste for criminal work, to visible relief all around. The happier announcement comes next: it is Erskine Brown, not Rumpole, whom the Lord Chancellor has chosen to sit as an assistant recorder at Snaresbrook: Featherstone's earlier offer to Rumpole having evidently come to nothing once his own exposure risk had passed. Rumpole, far from disappointed, declares that his own prospective "sitting" has become as unnecessary as Featherstone's fantasy of retiring to Ibiza, and offers Erskine Brown just one piece of advice for his new judicial career, the episode's closing line and its real moral: never, ever pay by credit card.
