Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 4, Episode 1

Rumpole and the Old, Old Story

A man is accused of attempted murder after brake sabotage; Rumpole moves into chambers after a row with Hilda.

Monday, 19 January 1987 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 19 January 1987 (Series 4), directed by Roger Bamford, written by John Mortimer.

The episode opens with a police officer charging a man named Arnold Gleeson's business partner with attempted murder, then cuts to the Scales of Justice dinner at the Savoy, where Rumpole and Hilda are guests of Mr. Justice Hugh Gwent Evans, a humorless Welsh judge, and his wife Gwyneth. Over the claret, Rumpole trots out a decades-old, mildly indecent anecdote about a long-ago indecent-assault case in a Bethnal Green cinema, a story he has clearly told many times before. Hilda is mortified, and in the taxi home afterward she tells him, not for the first time, that repeating "that disgusting story" in front of the judge's wife makes him look senile. Stung, Rumpole does something uncharacteristic: rather than simply apologizing, he opens the cab door mid-journey and gets out, leaving Hilda to finish the ride to Casterbridge Road alone: the small, almost accidental rupture that sets the whole episode's domestic plot in motion.

The next day, back at work, Rumpole visits his new client, Hugo Lutterworth, in a prison interview room alongside instructing solicitor Mr. Driscoll. Lutterworth is charged with attempting to murder his garden-centre business partner, Captain Gleeson, by draining the brake fluid from Gleeson's estate car, causing it to crash. The case is complicated by the fact that Lutterworth has been having an affair with Gleeson's wife, Amanda, a fact Lutterworth desperately wants kept out of proceedings, to Rumpole's professional exasperation, since the prosecution is certain to raise it constantly. At the garden centre itself, examining the "scene of the crime" (a modest slope and a sharp junction at the gates), Rumpole learns from Driscoll that a large supermarket chain has long wanted to buy the site, that Lutterworth, a committed environmentalist, has always refused to sell, and that Gleeson, by contrast, would happily cash in. Rumpole buys a geranium on his way out, almost as an afterthought.

Back in chambers, Head of Chambers Sam Ballard QC convenes a meeting ostensibly about maintaining standards (a stray set of antique shaving tackle has turned up, unclaimed, in the downstairs lavatory), but really to announce a proposed smoking ban, aimed squarely at Rumpole's cheroots. Ballard also drops a pointed remark about Rumpole's practice apparently thriving, given the "circumstantial evidence." That night, working late specifically to catch whoever has been secretly sleeping in chambers, Ballard is interrupted by a visiting Mr. Justice Gwent Evans, who assumes from Ballard's cagey manner that some scandal (adultery, even) is afoot; Ballard chases a fleeing pajama-clad figure down the corridor but fails to catch him. The next morning Rumpole strolls in as if nothing has happened, but Ballard, piecing together a bedside Oxford Book of English Verse, an out-of-date Archbold, and a glimpsed pajama leg, confronts him directly: Rumpole, after leaving Hilda in the cab, has quietly been living in his own chambers room. Rumpole defends his cheroots by solemnly misquoting Magna Carta, but the real crisis comes when curry containers are found in his waste bin, proving, worse than mere lodging, that he's not even going home to eat. Ballard, invoking the precedent of a tenant once evicted for cooking spaghetti bolognese on an electric fire, threatens Rumpole's tenancy outright.

Erskine Brown, sympathetic, intervenes and, somewhat to his own later regret, invites Rumpole to stay temporarily with him and his wife, QC Phillida Erskine Brown, at their house. The arrangement produces a sharply funny breakfast scene: the Erskine Browns' priggish, health-obsessed children, Tristan and Isolde, lecture Rumpole on the dangers of smoking and the coming criminalization of cigarettes, to his weary disbelief that Claude and Phillida have "given birth to a couple of prosecuting counsel." Meanwhile Phillida, trying to play peacemaker, calls on Hilda at the Rumpole flat, gently suggesting that Horace is miserable and merely waiting for a sign that Hilda wants him back. Hilda, however, is thriving (enjoying a newly tidy flat, uninterrupted Archers listening, and an active social calendar with the Gwent Evanses) and tells Phillida in no uncertain terms that Rumpole can stay away as long as he likes. Phillida reports this back to Claude, who is dismayed to realize Rumpole may now be a permanent houseguest; Rumpole, overhearing enough to understand the real state of play, teases Erskine Brown with the news that Hilda doesn't want him back after all, before asking Claude's help finding "another billet."

In court, Ballard opens the Crown's case against Lutterworth before Mr. Justice Gwent Evans, framing it, in his own words, as "the old, old story": an attractive younger wife, an older husband who has lost his charm for her, and an affair with the husband's business partner that escalates into attempted murder. A garden-centre witness testifies to having seen Lutterworth kissing Amanda Gleeson in full view of the staff, prompting the judge's theatrical shock; Rumpole, cross-examining, gets the witness to concede that Lutterworth regularly serviced the garden centre's vehicles, including, quite possibly, at Gleeson's own request. Outside court, Hilda appears, invited to lunch by the judge's wife and moving in distinctly grander circles than her husband at the moment, and offhandedly remarks on something she noticed during the evidence: Mrs. Gleeson never took her eyes off Lutterworth in the witness box, watching him far too intently for mere romantic longing. The observation strikes Rumpole as a genuine flash of insight, and he immediately asks Driscoll to dig up the Gleeson-Lutterworth partnership agreement, a document Driscoll drew up years earlier and had dismissed as irrelevant to the case.

Rumpole also presses a police witness, Detective Sergeant Wilmer, on the forensic examination of the car's brake system: unidentified fingerprints were found on it that were never checked against anyone else's, simply because they didn't belong to Lutterworth: an investigative shortcut Rumpole flags pointedly for the jury's benefit. Back at the Erskine Browns', Rumpole tells Claude he intends to stay on a while longer, under the pretext of "studying the law of partnership in depth"; Claude, resigned, reveals that Phillida's visit left her under the impression Hilda was desperately unhappy and wanted Rumpole home: a report sharply at odds with what Hilda actually told Phillida. Rumpole, delighted at the misunderstanding and in no hurry to leave, cryptically tells Erskine Brown that all he needs to do to help is turn up to the chambers' forthcoming social evening: a lecture, advertised on the notice board, by the Suffragan Bishop of Sidcup on "The Christian Approach to the Rent Act," with sherry and sandwiches to follow, organized by Ballard himself.

The trial's turning point comes when Ballard, examining Captain Gleeson, asks whether, despite the apparent attempt on his life, he is prepared to give his marriage another chance; Gleeson says yes, then dramatically develops heart palpitations, drawing the judge's solicitous concern. Rumpole's cross-examination of Gleeson is where the case truly turns: he establishes that Gleeson never so much as lifted his own car's bonnet, always leaving mechanical matters to Lutterworth; that Gleeson had received a very lucrative offer from a supermarket chain, Arcadia Stores, to buy the garden-centre site; and that Lutterworth, the committed conservationist, had steadfastly refused to sell. Rumpole then has the jury's attention turn to paragraph 12 of the partnership agreement, which Gleeson is made to read aloud in court: if either partner is convicted of a criminal offence, his entire share of the partnership property and income reverts immediately to the other partner. The implication lands visibly: if Lutterworth is convicted of attempting to murder him, Gleeson inherits sole ownership of the site and can sell it to Arcadia for a fortune. Confronted with this motive, and with his wife's unmistakable, affectionate-turned-suspicious watchfulness over Lutterworth now explained as something closer to guilt, Gleeson collapses under the pressure and the court adjourns in disarray.

Shortly afterward, Driscoll brings Rumpole fresh fingerprint evidence: Captain Gleeson's own prints, found "in all the right places" on the brake system he'd always claimed never to touch, confirming that Gleeson sabotaged his own car to frame Lutterworth, both to be rid of his wife's lover and to trigger the partnership clause and the supermarket windfall. Rumpole, unsurprised, observes that incompetent crooks are always the ones who get caught. That night, Ballard, having overindulged in sherry at the Bishop of Sidcup's talk and missed his train home to Waltham Cross, is warned off driving by Erskine Brown, who notes that a breathalyzer conviction would end any hope of a judicial appointment; Ballard, stuck, ends up sleeping rough in his own chambers room, exactly as Rumpole had been doing. The next morning Rumpole brings him tea and gleefully turns the tables, threatening, in the same mock-officious terms Ballard had used on him, to report Ballard for breaching the terms of his own lease. Thoroughly chastened and caught in his own hypocrisy, Ballard quietly abandons both the smoking ban and his pursuit of Rumpole's living arrangements, settling for an unspoken mutual understanding.

In the robing room afterward, Rumpole gives full credit for the case's winning insight to Hilda: it was her remark about Mrs. Gleeson's watchfulness that cracked the case, he says, a nice piece of advocacy instinct for someone who, unlike him, never trained as a lawyer. That evening Rumpole turns up at the flat bearing the now rather wilted geranium from the garden centre, declaring that he still lives there; Hilda, having thoroughly enjoyed her independence, makes him work for it, feigning surprise that he still remembers the address. The two reconcile over a gin and tonic to celebrate her unofficial co-authorship of the courtroom victory, and Hilda caps the evening with a long-told family story of her own: how her father, C. H. Wystan, won his first great case defending a pickpocket for nothing, only for the grateful former client to try to pay him back with the stolen watch itself. It is, pointedly, every bit as old and familiar a story as the one Rumpole told at the Savoy that started the whole episode, and he has, of course, heard it many times before. The episode ends on Hilda's small, domestic punchline, sending Rumpole off to water the dying geranium in the kitchen.
