Rumpole of the Bailey · Special

Rumpole's Return

The 1980 TV special, not a true Series 2 episode: a bored, retired Rumpole living in Florida returns to London chambers after an enquiry from Phyllida draws him back.

Tuesday, 30 December 1980 · ITV (Thames Television)

The episode opens at Rumpole's old chambers, where a meeting convened by Head of Chambers Guthrie Featherstone marks "the first chambers meeting in the absence of a once familiar figure": Horace Rumpole has, apparently for good this time, retired from the bar and moved to Florida to live with his son Nick, a sociology professor at a university there. The reaction is decidedly mixed: Uncle Tom laments that chambers "just isn't chambers" without him, while Erskine Brown enjoys the absence of "the stink of cheap little cigars" and clients who roll up the waiting-room carpet and carry it off in a holdall. With the room empty, Featherstone has installed two new tenants to share it: Owen Glendower, a Welsh insurance specialist nicknamed "knock-for-knock Owen," and Ken Cracknell, a scruffy, civil-rights-minded young radical barrister who arrived in chambers as an unglamorous "squatter" on a motorbike and has just picked up his first solo murder brief.

In Florida, Rumpole is visibly restless in retirement, reading newspaper reports of a London stabbing (the Honourable Rory Cantor, a wealthy landowner's younger brother, killed in a tube station under mysterious circumstances) with far more interest than Hilda would like. Hilda, delighted by the climate and determined that this retirement stick, reminds him pointedly that it was Judge Bullingham, not she, who drove him out, after ten losses in a row left him "sick of the screams about race relations." At a poolside barbecue thrown by Nick, Rumpole is introduced to his son's university colleagues, including law professor Nathan Blofield (eager to book Rumpole for a faculty seminar, over Hilda's firm objection that he needs rest) and a statistician named Tiffany Jones, Nick's girlfriend, who turns out to share an odd, repetitive turn of phrase ("we shall meet and talk, friend and brother," "as sure as the seed grows in the sunlight") with a street proselytizer who had earlier handed Rumpole a sunburned flower downtown. Soon afterward, Tiffany disappears without a trace, taking nothing but her own work materials, leaving Nick bewildered and increasingly lonely.

Back in London, the stabbing case lands on young Ken Cracknell's desk: his client, a meek, fastidious tax official nicknamed "the Duchess" (surname Simpson), has confessed to police, was identified by witnesses, had the knife in his dustbin, and, most damningly, appears to have written a strange message in the victim's blood referencing something called "the children of the sun." Cracknell, thrilled at first to have a solo murder to make his name on, quickly realises the case is close to hopeless and, without telling his girlfriend-turned-wife-to-be Phillida Trant his real motive, writes to Rumpole in Florida describing the puzzle "in the blood," hoping to lure his old chambers rival back into a case specifically designed to fail and send him packing for good.

The bait works: Rumpole, bored stiff and increasingly estranged from Hilda's vision of a sunshine retirement, flies home on a budget airline, reclaims his old room over Featherstone's objections that chambers has no spare accommodation for a man who is supposed to be retired, and effectively squats in his own former office alongside, and at the expense of, Cracknell and Glendower. Hilda, left behind in Florida with Nick, is furious to learn by telegram (which she initially mistook for a tax notice) that her husband has gone back to practising law without her. While waiting to be formally briefed on the murder, Rumpole takes on an unrelated obscenity case in the northern town of Grimble, defending adult-bookshop proprietor Mr. Meacher against a self-righteous local alderman, Lancelot Pertwee, mounting an impassioned free-speech defence ("freedom is indivisible... I utterly deplore that rubbish, but I will defend to the death anyone's right to read it"), and loses badly, with Meacher sentenced to eighteen months, leaving Rumpole visibly shaken and his confidence dented just as Cracknell had hoped.

A subplot of marital comedy follows: Hilda, now returned to London and alarmed by Rumpole's gloom and his evenings spent reading seized pornographic magazines "for the case," has the family doctor make a concerned house call, leading to a thoroughly mortifying misunderstanding about Rumpole's supposed private tastes before the truth (that it was simply trial preparation) comes out. Cracknell, meanwhile, formally asks Rumpole to lead him in the Simpson murder after all, privately hoping that one more courtroom defeat, on a case this difficult, will finally send the aging barrister back across the Atlantic for good; Phillida Trant, unaware of Cracknell's real motive, admires his generosity in "getting the old man a case he needs."

In Florida, Nick's search for Tiffany leads him to a remote house belonging to a group calling itself "the Children of the Sun," led by a figure known only as "the master," who promises recruits miraculous power and a share in ruling the world once the group inherits the earth, in exchange for signing over their worldly possessions and, disturbingly, a pledge written in their own blood. Nick finds Tiffany there, now using her statistical skills to keep the group's books, cryptically mentioning a previous bookkeeper ("some guy from England") who was denounced as a traitor before vanishing; Tiffany refuses to leave, telling Nick, "I am home." Back in London, this same organisation's name resurfaces as the key to the murder case: the bloodstained note found with Cantor's body reads "Sunlight to the children of the sun... blood to the children of the dark," and it becomes clear that the Honourable Rory Cantor (trained at Sandhurst and in army commando techniques, engaged to an American convert he met while playing polo in Florida, and having given away his entire inheritance and family farm to the same organisation) was a fanatical disciple of this group, not an opportunistic predator as the "guardsman's defence" Cracknell had floated first assumed.

At the Old Bailey, before a predictably hostile Judge Bullingham, Rumpole dismantles the prosecution case piece by piece: eyewitness testimony that Cantor deliberately waited on the platform rather than board an available train, rather than Simpson stalking him; evidence that Simpson's apparent collapse and exhaustion on the train afterward is equally consistent with shock as with guilt; and, most decisively, a forensic demolition of the "miracle" note itself. Cross-examining the Crown's blood expert, Professor Ackerman, Rumpole establishes that blood-group antigens fade and become harder to classify accurately over time, and that a message written months earlier in Simpson's own blood (plausibly as an initiation oath for the same cult) could, by the time of testing, read out under laboratory analysis as a false match for the victim's different blood group. The "supernatural" bloody confession note, Rumpole argues, may have nothing to do with the night of the killing at all.

With the forensic "miracle" stripped away, Simpson finally tells Rumpole, and then the jury, the real story: recruited on a Florida holiday by the same friendly stranger-with-flowers tactic Rumpole himself encountered, drawn into the cult's accounts department, and horrified to discover the "master's" operation was a financial swindle preying on the lonely and gullible, Simpson was marked as a traitor once he confided his suspicions to a fellow member. Too frightened to go to the police, he fled, and Cantor, the group's trained and fanatically devoted enforcer, was sent to silence him permanently at Kentish Town station; in the ensuing struggle for the knife, it was Cantor, not Simpson, who ended up stabbed. Rumpole's closing argument reframes the entire case as "not a miracle" but ordinary human greed and violence dressed up in religious language, and persuades the jury to acquit.

The aftermath lands two separate blows against Ken Cracknell's hopes. Phillida Trant confronts him directly once she realises he deliberately manoeuvred Rumpole into leading a case Cracknell expected to lose, specifically to drive Rumpole back into permanent retirement and clear his own path in chambers, and tells him bluntly that Rumpole, whatever his age, is the real radical of the two of them, willing to say what he thinks and take cases Cracknell is too timid to risk alone, while reaffirming that she will never risk her marriage to Erskine Brown for him. At the chambers Christmas party, Featherstone announces both Rumpole's unlikely murder acquittal and Owen Glendower's elevation to the circuit bench, which reopens the very room question that triggered Rumpole's return in the first place. Pressed by Featherstone to "discuss with your family" whether he's truly staying this time, Hilda settles the matter herself: she will remain in England and look after Rumpole, since she's finally convinced his one true devotion is to the law (and, she jokes, to Judge Bullingham specifically) rather than to any retirement in the sun. Asked, one more time, how long he intends to stay, Rumpole answers with an old courtroom anecdote about an elderly prisoner given a sentence far longer than he could ever live to serve, who was told simply to "do as much of it as you can," and that, Rumpole says, is exactly his own plan, wishing the room a merry Christmas as the episode ends without ever pinning down a firm date for any future retirement.
