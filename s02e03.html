Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 2, Episode 3

Rumpole and the Show Folk

Actress Maggie Hartley is charged with murdering her husband; Rumpole wins an accidental-discharge acquittal, then learns she withheld money owed to him.

Tuesday, 12 June 1979 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 12 June 1979 (Series 2), directed by Peter Hammond, written by John Mortimer. Before the main titles, a brief, impressionistic cold open conveys the killing itself through fragments of overlapping sound and panicked speech: "It's a good bar," "£50," a cat called Puss being fed, repeated cries of "I killed him -- what could I do with him? Help me."

Before the titles, a confused, barely-transcribable cold open seems to depict the crime itself: raised voices, a cat called Puss being shooed and fed, a struggle, and a woman's voice crying out, over and over, "I killed him -- what could I do with him? Help me," the line that will turn out to be the spine of the whole case. The story proper begins with Rumpole arriving by train in the northern town of "Grimble" (a fictional stand-in, per the script's own joke about "all points north"), greeted warmly by his old clerk Albert Handyside, now working for a local firm of solicitors. Albert has brought Rumpole up to appear as junior counsel in the town's first murder in years: a "lady of the show folk," an actress charged with killing her actor-manager husband. Rumpole, needling Albert about provincial pretensions and reminiscing about having run the Penge Bungalow Murders single-handed without a leader, asks to go straight to the prison rather than the hotel, joking that he'll "feel more at home" there.

At the prison, Rumpole meets the defendant, stage name Maggie Hartley, legally Mrs. Freyre, alongside her leading counsel, the smooth and compromise-minded Jarvis Allen QC. Maggie claims shock has wiped her memory of events, but Allen is already steering toward a deal: he knows the prosecutor, Tommy Pierce, and believes the judge, Mr. Justice Skelton, might accept a plea to manslaughter rather than murder. Rumpole balks: their client was found holding the gun, true, but that's "a stupid place to have it if she was planning a murder," and starts probing the evidence more carefully, including statements from a company member, Alan Copland, and the existence of a much younger rival for her late husband's affections. Maggie herself focuses obsessively on how her one incriminating line to the stage manager, Mr. Croft ("I killed him. What could I do with him? Help me"), was actually delivered; she insists the "reading" of the line, its stress and intonation, changes its meaning entirely, and that only her advocate in court, not her barrister in a cell, can convey that to a jury.

Rumpole and Albert tour the Theatre Royal, the "locus in quo," meeting the company's harried manager, Mr. Derwent, who delivers a comic eulogy for the murdered man, GP Freyre (a hard-drinking actor famous for upstaging colleagues and chasing a new ingenue every season) while praising Maggie's "perfect truthfulness" and "absolute reality" as a performer, a compliment whose double meaning will resurface later. In the judge's chambers before trial, Jarvis Allen reveals, visibly stung, that Maggie has sacked him as leading counsel in favor of her junior, telling him candidly that she thinks Rumpole would be "better casting." Despite pressure from Allen and the judge to accept an adjournment so Maggie can find a proper QC, and despite circuit etiquette that frowns on a junior carrying on after his leader is dismissed, Rumpole declines any delay ("the show must go on") and proceeds to try a murder case entirely alone.

At trial, prosecutor Tommy Pierce leans hard into the theatrical setting, painting the "world of the show folk" as a glamorous surface over "deep tides of jealousy and passion," and builds his case on Mr. Croft's account: Croft, arriving after two shots, found Freyre dead and Maggie standing over him with the gun, speaking the fatal line. Rumpole's cross-examination of Croft is a careful geometric reconstruction of the dressing room: the position of the shattered cheval mirror at the far end of the room from the door, the two bullets (one in the mirror, one in Freyre's body), and the awkward physical logic of Maggie having had to fire in two different directions. He also presses, over the judge's and prosecution's objections, on the ambiguity of "What could I do with him? Help me," arguing the jury alone, not Croft, should decide what it meant, complete with a theatrically timed pause before his questions that Jarvis Allen later praises, to Rumpole's irritation, purely as stagecraft.

Further witnesses round out the picture: Alan Copland confirms Freyre owned a legally licensed revolver (bought after a stint playing a James-Bond-style secret agent, a joke the judge pointedly declines to understand), kept at a rifle club but recently brought back to the theatre at Freyre's own request, ostensibly against burglars, with ammunition Copland never told Maggie about, for fear it would make her "nervous" of what her drunken husband might do with it. Copland also testifies, unprompted, that Maggie never once retaliated during the couple's frequent public quarrels, including one where Freyre threw a glass of milk stout in her face in front of the whole company. The investigating inspector confirms that once a revolver's hammer has been drawn back for a first shot, far less pressure is needed to fire it a second time, a detail Rumpole will use to argue the fatal shot could have gone off accidentally in a struggle. Finally, under Rumpole's questioning, the young actress Christine Hope (who waited nightly at the stage door and was regularly walked home by Freyre until six in the morning "talking about the theatre") all but confirms she was his mistress, establishing the motive for the marriage's collapse, before the judge spares her from confirming it outright.

In the cells, Rumpole coaches Maggie for her own testimony, warning her against any hint of criticizing "the dear departed" and telling her simply to be herself, a request Maggie, bitterly amused, says is impossible, since what he actually wants is for her to perform an "ordinary North Country housewife," not an actress on trial for murder. She also balks at swearing on the Bible, admitting she's agnostic at best, though Rumpole persuades her that a Grimble jury will like it better if she does.

On the stand, Maggie gives her account: Freyre, drunk and furious, told her he was leaving the company and her for Christine Hope and meant "to get rid of her," then produced the revolver from his dressing table. Terrified (she says she had no idea he kept it there, let alone loaded), she watched him fumble with the hammer and fire a first shot that shattered the mirror; as he turned the gun on her and began to cock it again, she grabbed for his hand to push it away and, in the struggle, forced his finger back onto the trigger, firing the shot that killed him. She insists, repeatedly and with apparent anguish, that she never intended to kill him, and that her words to Croft meant only "how could I help him," not a confession. Rumpole stages a demonstration in court with the court usher standing in for the deceased, re-enacting the physical struggle to show the jury how an accidental discharge at close range was entirely possible, drawing a sharp rebuke from Pierce when Rumpole lets slip the word "accident" before the jury has reached any conclusion.

In closing, Rumpole turns the courtroom-as-theatre framing back on the jury themselves: this case has taken only a few hours of their lives, after which they'll go home, put the kettle on, and forget the "angry, drunken actor and his wretched infidelities," but for Maggie Hartley, the whole of her life hangs on their verdict, and the only real question is whether she goes down into darkness and disgrace or returns to the stage to "bring us all joy and laughter and entertainment once again." The verdict is not shown being read aloud; the scene cuts instead straight to the corridor afterward, where Albert's jubilant congratulations ("you did it, Mr. Rumpole... the Penge Bungalow job all over again, sir") and Rumpole's dry aside that he "wasn't defending an actress" after all make clear Maggie has been acquitted outright, not merely convicted of manslaughter. Mr. Derwent and even the deposed Jarvis Allen offer warm congratulations, and Rumpole is invited to that night's circuit dinner at the Midland Hotel.

The episode closes at that dinner with a mock-ceremonial "trial" of Rumpole himself by the assembled bar mess, charged with the offence of continuing to represent a client after his leader was sacked, and "fined" twelve bottles of claret for winning in Jarvis Allen's absence. Pressed for a mitigation speech, Rumpole turns unexpectedly serious, delivering an impassioned complaint about what the adversarial system does to defendants generally: forcing them into ties and hats and suitable solemnity, coaching them to call the judge "my lord" and say nothing that might upset a jury of lay preachers, and asking the mess bluntly whether any of them, in all their trials, ever actually glimpse the truth of a client's life, or merely dress it up in a hat of seriousness to please judge and jury alike. The mess shouts him down with "time's up" and launches instead into a rowdy chorus of "On the Road to Mandalay," the episode trailing off into song and half-intelligible merriment rather than any tidier resolution of the question Rumpole has just raised.
