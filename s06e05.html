Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 6, Episode 5

Rumpole and the Quacks

A doctor is accused of molesting a patient; Phyllida suspects Erskine-Brown of philandering.

Monday, 25 November 1991 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 25 November 1991 (Series 6), directed by Robert Tronson, written by John Mortimer.

The episode opens, before Rumpole is even introduced, on a GP's surgery where a young woman, Marietta Liptrot, complains of a sore throat to Dr Ghulam Rahmat, who asks her to lie on the couch and, oddly, to slip down her trousers -- a scene whose significance only becomes clear much later. Rumpole himself first appears nursing a spectacular hangover after a late night celebrating the elevation of a colleague, Hoskins, to the bench, theatrically declaring himself to be dying. Hilda insists on calling a doctor despite Rumpole's horror of "quacks" like the surgery's late, starvation-diet-prescribing Dr McClintock, and the practice sends round Dr Rahmat, who diagnoses a simple case of food poisoning (in fact a hangover), charms Rumpole thoroughly, and mentions in passing that he may one day need Rumpole's professional help with "no crime at all."

Rumpole's day brightens briefly at the magistrates' court, where he successfully defends the exuberant Mrs Bambi Etheridge on a dangerous-driving charge after the prosecutor, Mr Govitt, carelessly implies the case is really about her being "a lady driver," a slip the magistrate pounces on and Rumpole exploits without having to do much work himself. Bambi, delighted and smitten, repeatedly invites Rumpole to lunch and describes him as "a very cuddly kind of barrister," propositions he fends off with limited success, and which generate a string of awkward telephone calls to the Rumpole household that Hilda relays with amusement. Back in chambers, Guthrie Featherstone, as head of chambers, reprimands Rumpole for scrawling an irreverent comment on an official notice and introduces a new pupil to Claude Erskine Brown: Heather Whitaker, a pleasant woman who has come to the bar late in life.

Dr Rahmat's promised trouble duly arrives: he is facing a General Medical Council complaint of sexual assault, brought by Marietta Liptrot, who claims he groped her and propositioned her during that sore-throat examination and fled screaming into the waiting room. The solicitor, Mr Pinhorn, is skeptical of the client's chosen defence -- Rahmat keeps citing E.M. Forster's A Passage to India, in which an Indian doctor is falsely accused of rape by an Englishwoman in the Marabar Caves and ultimately acquitted when she withdraws her story -- as hopelessly literary for a professional tribunal. Rumpole, intrigued despite himself, presses Rahmat on his precise clinical reasons for the examination and begins quietly working the case, while noting that the complainant works, inconveniently, as a "children's nurse" for wealthy Kensington families -- a detail that does not sit well with the rest of what emerges about her.

A parallel domestic plot develops through Claude Erskine Brown's marriage: his wife Phillida, back from a long fraud trial in Hong Kong, grows suspicious of a "lonely hearts" advertisement she finds in a magazine in Claude's room in chambers -- a self-described barrister, bored with married humdrum, seeking opera-going, country walks, and "all the other pleasures of the flesh." Phillida, convinced Claude placed it, confronts Rumpole with the clipping, and it happens that Phillida is also prosecuting the General Medical Council's case against Dr Rahmat, so the two threads keep crossing in the corridors of chambers. Rumpole, borrowing the clipping to investigate, dials the contact number out of curiosity -- and finds himself talking to "Naughty Marietta," the very escort-agency alter ego of Marietta Liptrot, Rahmat's accuser -- a discovery Phillida overhears and badly misreads, assuming Rumpole himself is indulging in escort agencies.

Convinced the lonely-hearts advertisement must have been placed by Erskine Brown, Rumpole sets what he considers a foolproof trap: a reply advertisement arranging an assignation at the Temple Church at six o'clock, both parties to wear a white carnation. The ambush produces nothing but an awkward encounter with Erskine Brown's new pupil, Heather Whitaker, who happens to be wearing a white carnation for entirely unrelated reasons, leaving Rumpole none the wiser about who the real lonely heart actually is. Meanwhile Hilda, alarmed by a report from Marguerite Ballard that Rumpole has been behaving oddly -- specifically, telephoning escort agencies from chambers -- packs him off to see the surgery's senior partner, Dr Tim Cogger, for a check-up, on the theory that her husband may be suffering some sort of midlife crisis.

At the surgery, Rumpole learns from the receptionist, Miss Danquist, that the escort-agency magazine was actually found discarded in one of the doctors' own wastepaper baskets -- not in chambers at all -- which redirects his suspicion squarely onto Dr Cogger rather than Claude Erskine Brown. Pressing Rahmat afterward about his relationship with his senior partner, Rumpole learns the two men quarreled over Cogger's insistence on prescribing expensive new drugs supplied by the manufacturer Marchmain, whose representative, Peter Callaway, regularly lunches Cogger at the Savoy and showers the surgery's receptionist with chocolates; Rahmat had refused to go along with what he considered an unnecessary and costly prescribing habit, souring his relationship with Cogger considerably.

At the General Medical Council hearing, presided over by the pompous Sir Hector McCollough, Phillida opens the case for the Council, describing Marietta's account of the assault in lurid detail and calling Dr Cogger -- who treated Marietta afterward for nervous strain -- as her first witness. Rumpole's cross-examination of Cogger is devastating: he establishes that a sore throat can indeed be a symptom of glandular fever, the so-called "kissing disease," which would legitimately require exactly the kind of groin examination Rahmat performed, exposing Cogger's earlier dismissive testimony as either incompetent or dishonest. Rumpole then drives at Cogger's financial relationship with Marchmain and its representative Callaway, forces him to invoke his right not to answer questions that might incriminate him, and finally produces the escort-agency magazine itself -- found, as established, in a doctor's own wastebasket at the surgery, dated weeks before the alleged assault -- strongly suggesting that Cogger had prior knowledge of, and likely paid for, Marietta Liptrot's services in order to frame his inconveniently principled partner and clear the way for the lucrative Marchmain drugs.

The case collapses when Marietta Liptrot herself takes the stand: under gentle questioning from Rumpole, she admits that nothing improper occurred at all, that Dr Rahmat behaved throughout like "a proper gentleman," and Phillida, left with no case to continue, formally withdraws the charge. Sir Hector sharply rebukes Marietta for wasting everyone's time with a scandalous false allegation, blissfully unaware of the far murkier financial conspiracy Rumpole's cross-examination has actually exposed and left officially "unresolved" rather than pressed to a conclusion.

Afterward, Dr Rahmat confesses to Rumpole, with evident pride, that he had in fact solved the whole problem himself well before the hearing: rather than let the case be won by "ravishing poor old Tim Cogger" in cross-examination and souring surgery relations permanently, he had quietly taken Marietta out to dinner at an Indian restaurant on several occasions, persuaded her of the truth, and -- in the process -- fallen for her; the two are now engaged to be married, with Rumpole and Hilda, and even Mr Pinhorn, cordially invited to the wedding and reception (Dr Cogger invited too, pointedly, so that he might be made to propose the toast). Rumpole is equal parts delighted and professionally affronted that his client has effectively settled his own case without him.

The lonely-hearts mystery resolves at home that evening: Hilda, examining the clipping Rumpole still has not managed to decode, points out the small "(F)" after the word "Barrister" that Rumpole had overlooked in his rush to accuse Claude -- the advertisement was placed by a female barrister, almost certainly Heather Whitaker, Claude's new and evidently restless pupil, not by Erskine Brown at all. Chastened, Rumpole resolves to make amends to Claude with a lunch at the Savoy, while Bambi Etheridge rings yet again to report a further driving mishap; Rumpole, declining to take the case himself this time, happily recommends her instead to the unsuspecting Sam Ballard, assuring him that Bambi finds him "a rather cuddly kind of barrister" too.
