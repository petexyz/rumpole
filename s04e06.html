Rumpole of the Bailey

S04E06: Rumpole's Last Case

Rumpole, defending a Timson, places an exotic racing bet that could let him retire if it wins.

Wednesday, 25 February 1987 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 25 February 1987 (Series 4), directed by Rodney Bennett, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).

Cousins Cyril and Dennis Timson are caught tunnelling into the Pennywise Savings Bank when they hear a noise behind them in the tunnel, panic, and bolt into the strong-room corridor -- where a bank guard, Mr Huggins, is shot and wounded in the foot before both Timsons are arrested empty-handed, each privately ready to blame the other for carrying the gun. At home, Rumpole and Hilda bicker over a scorched old hearthrug (another casualty of his cigars) that Hilda insists on replacing, while Rumpole grumbles that legal aid rates barely cover the fare to Temple station, let alone a new rug -- a domestic money squeeze that will matter once a much larger sum of imaginary money enters the picture. In the cells, Phillida Erskine Brown QC, leading for Cyril, bluntly advises her client to tell the judge the plain truth that his cousin Dennis carried the gun; Rumpole, defending Dennis, counsels exactly the opposite, setting up a classic "cutthroat" defence between two members of the same family, even as both men privately protest that Timsons simply do not carry shooters or grass on each other.

A welcome distraction arrives when clerk Henry hands Rumpole a hundred pounds in winnings from a chambers sweepstake on the Derby; flush with "folding money" for once, Rumpole visits Dennis in custody and discovers his client is a serious, resourceful gambler, running an elaborate four-horse accumulator through a friendly, food-obsessed prison officer named Gerald, who places bets for inmates on the outside. Dennis confidently predicts winnings in the hundreds of thousands of pounds; amused and tempted, Rumpole hands Gerald his own hundred pounds to ride along on the same accumulator, starting with a horse called Pretty Balloon at Goodwood. Meanwhile the wider Timson clan -- patriarch Fred, and wives Doris and Maureen -- corner Rumpole in the court corridor, mortified that their rivals the Malloys have been taunting them in the street and in Tesco's, calling the Timsons "ferrets" now that word has spread the cousins might inform on each other.

At the Old Bailey, before Judge Bullingham, prosecuting counsel Charles Hearthstoke lays out the Crown's case -- a sophisticated tunnel job via an existing service passage, ending in Huggins being shot "albeit in the foot" -- while Bullingham is far more interested in ogling Phillida Erskine Brown than in the evidence, musing aloud about sending her chocolates and inviting her, alone, to lunch with the judges, to the silent mortification of her own junior and husband, Claude Erskine Brown, sitting forgotten behind her. Throughout the trial, updates on Rumpole's growing fortune arrive via Dennis, who relays tips smuggled in from the prison grapevine ("Pretty Balloon romped it... one up, three to go") in open court, each leg of the accumulator landing safely.

At Pomeroy's that evening, Charles Hearthstoke -- newly solicitous, and clearly interested in more than Phillida's advocacy -- plies her with gossip about Dennis Timson's record and compliments on her courtroom technique, while she admits, a little wistfully, that she is "not all that much married" these days. Rumpole arrives with Liz Probert in tow, deflates the celebratory champagne as cheap "methode champenoise," and, riding high on his gambling luck, startles the table by wondering aloud whether this might turn out to be "Rumpole's positively last case." At home, Hilda proudly unveils the new hearthrug -- not real Persian, but a decent pattern -- while Rumpole, intoxicated by visions of a tropical retirement funded by Dennis's accumulator, recites Tennyson's "The Lotos-Eaters" about sailing off to a land where it is always afternoon, vowing to throw his Havana cigars into the sea and never wear a winged collar again. Hilda's practical question -- whether he has actually still got the hundred pounds -- goes conspicuously unanswered.

Cross-examining Detective Inspector Bellman, Rumpole draws out that no Timson fingerprints were found inside the strong room or on the safe itself, since both men wore gloves -- and, more pointedly, that the police never bothered checking whether any of the other prints found at the scene belonged to other known criminals. Privately, Rumpole asks Bellman for "a little favour": in exchange for a solemn (and characteristically insincere) promise never again to embarrass his officers in court, he asks Bellman to let it be known, truthfully or not, that fresh fingerprint evidence has turned something up -- ostensibly "in the interests of justice," though Bellman clearly suspects there's more to it than that.

In court, Phillida draws from Cyril Timson exactly the testimony she wants -- that he never carried the gun, had no idea Dennis was armed, and would have told him to leave "that thing" at home, since using a firearm simply isn't how the Timsons do business -- and Rumpole, unusually, accepts every word of it as true rather than attacking it. Instead he presses Cyril on a different detail: when the two men heard a noise in the tunnel and ran, they fled forward into the corridor rather than back the way they'd come -- implying the noise, and the gun, came from someone following them in from behind, not from either Timson. Shortly afterward, on an ordinary London street, a detective constable arrests a man named Peter Malloy, of the rival Malloy clan, over a fingerprint.

Finally free, on his self-declared last case, to say exactly what he thinks, Rumpole launches into a blistering opening speech to the jury about "the Bullingham Factor" -- the presumption, as pernicious as the presumption of innocence is supposed to be golden, that everyone in the dock is guilty and every defence barrister merely a legal-aid parasite wasting the court's time, all enforced by a judge who effectively briefs himself for the prosecution. Before Bullingham can retaliate, Hearthstoke interrupts with news from Bellman: further inquiries mean the Crown can no longer allege that either Timson carried or used the gun that wounded Huggins -- charges over that will be brought against "another firm" entirely -- leaving only a straightforward burglary count, to which Dennis, for the first time in his life, pleads guilty. Afterward, Rumpole explains to Dennis what Doris's "ferrets" remark had told him: the Malloys had tracked the Timsons' tunnel, planning to let their rivals do the digging and then rob them of the proceeds at gunpoint; in the chaos, a Malloy gun wounded Huggins and the Malloys made off with the actual contents of the safe while Cyril and Dennis, caught empty-handed, blamed each other. Bellman's bluffed fingerprint tip was enough to make the real culprits turn informer on their own family -- a reversal the Timsons will enjoy for years. Dennis, sentenced to four years, takes it in good humour and tells Rumpole he'll ring him up if he's ever passing through "Lotusland."

In the judge's room afterward, Phillida smooths over Rumpole's contempt of court by assuring Bullingham, quite inventively, that the second half of his interrupted speech was actually going to praise him as one of the fairest, most compassionate judges ever to sit at the Old Bailey -- a flattery so effective that Bullingham, now thoroughly smitten, drops any thought of reporting Rumpole and instead asks Phillida, apropos of nothing, whether she prefers hard centres in a box of chocolates. Back in chambers, Henry begs Rumpole to reconsider retiring, fearing Head of Chambers Ballard will hand his vacated room -- and Henry's whole clerking commission -- straight back to the departed Charles Hearthstoke. Rumpole obliges with one last piece of mischief: he tells Hearthstoke, who has clearly been pursuing Phillida, that returning to chambers would mean more or less adopting the Erskine Browns' children and moving them all into his Battersea bachelor flat, a prospect that sends the ambitious Hearthstoke into a swift and total retreat from both the room and, implicitly, Phillida.

That evening at Pomeroy's, just as Rumpole is about to order a bottle of Dom Perignon to celebrate his apparent windfall, Claude Erskine Brown relays a garbled, clearly tipsy phone message left by Gerald -- calling from the airport to thank Mr Rumpole for his excellent tips before boarding a flight to a warmer climate. Rumpole immediately understands he has been comprehensively swindled: Gerald has absconded with the whole accumulator winnings, Rumpole's hundred pounds included, leaving nothing behind -- "not even a betting slip." He downgrades the order to cheap plonk and urges Erskine Brown, with sudden and genuine feeling, to go and take his own wife to the opera while he still can. At home, Rumpole confesses the loss to Hilda, who is relieved at least that the money for the real hearthrug was kept separate; both admit, a little ruefully, that a retirement spent together on a hotel veranda might well have run out of conversation rather quickly anyway. Rumpole owns that matters are worse than just a lost hundred pounds -- he also promised Inspector Bellman this would be his last case, and told the jury exactly what he thought of "the mad bull" in open court -- but the following morning finds him walking into Temple as usual at a quarter to nine, asking Henry, without a trace of irony, whether there might be a small brief going spare at the Uxbridge Magistrates' Court.
