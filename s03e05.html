Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 3, Episode 5

Rumpole and the Sporting Life

A woman says she accidentally shot her husband; a second bullet suggests a second shooter; she is acquitted after revealing an affair.

Tuesday, 8 November 1983 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 8 November 1983 (Series 3), directed by Bill Hays, written by John Mortimer.

The episode opens at an amateur point-to-point race meeting, where Hilda (to Rumpole's bemused indifference to all things sporting) backs the winning horse, Atlantic Hero, ridden by the Honourable Jonathan "Johnno" Poston, and collects a tidy win. Mingling in the crowd are Claude Erskine Brown (Phillida absent on a long fraud case in Leeds), Uncle Tom, and Mr Justice Twyburn, who trades some pointed philosophical banter with Rumpole about whether a criminal trial's verdict is really any less a matter of chance than a horse race, a comparison Rumpole rejects but which hangs over the whole episode. Rumpole also meets, for the first time, Jennifer Poston (Johnno's wife and, as it turns out, the glamorous older sister of his own chambers protégée Fiona Allways), along with Fiona's rather dim, dog-loving solicitor boyfriend Jeremy Jarlence, and a socially awkward, much-mocked neighbor of the Postons, Maurice "Fish Face" Fishbourne, who has just taken a tumble from his horse to general amusement.

That evening at home, flush with her winnings, Hilda is already dreaming of country life, poring over Country Life classifieds for a "gentleman's lodge" near Tester with "access to good rough shooting," while Rumpole deflects with jokes about mud, cancelled trains, and the countryside's casual relationship with death. The scene cuts abruptly to the woods on the Postons' land, where Jennifer Poston is heard crying that she has shot her husband, and that it was an accident. Back in chambers, a distressed Fiona Allways begs Rumpole to defend her sister, but Rumpole firmly invokes one of his own cardinal rules of advocacy (never appear for friends or family, since the emotional stakes warp your judgment of the case's weaknesses and you're never forgiven if you lose) before, in practice, taking the brief on anyway and heading down to the country town of Tester to investigate.

Driven out by the accident-prone Jeremy Jarlence (whose boisterous dog Agatha monopolizes the car and whose vocabulary runs to acronyms like "GMBU," a grand military balls-up), Rumpole learns on the drive that the dead man had a reputation as a serial womanizer, with a particular, locally notorious pursuit of a woman named Debbie Pavia, a reputation that, Jarlence suggests, is exactly what has stopped the local police from simply accepting an "accident" explanation without further questions. In the prison visiting room, Jennifer Poston gives Rumpole an account that doesn't quite hold up to scrutiny: a quarrel that afternoon (partly a hangover-fueled row), her husband walking off to the woods to cool down, herself following soon after with her own twenty-bore shotgun (ostensibly to calm her nerves on a rabbit or pigeon, since pheasant season had closed) and then, coming upon him on a rough, bramble-choked track, tripping and discharging the gun by accident. Rumpole, no stranger to guns himself, is skeptical that a woman raised from the cradle around shooting, the daughter of "a terrific shot," could plausibly violate the most basic firearms rule (never, loaded or not, let the muzzle point at a person) by sheer clumsiness.

Touring the scene of the crime with Jarlence, Rumpole learns that the Postons' land backs onto that of the disliked, laxative-fortune-funded Fishbourne, who keeps a caged "calling" cock pheasant to lure the Postons' own birds onto his land so he can shoot them from his drawing-room window, a bit of petty poaching that Jonathan Poston apparently knew about and simply tolerated. More significantly, Rumpole learns of a tenant gamekeeper named Figgis, living in a cottage right by the spot where Poston died, who was "doing a bit of shooting, as usual" that very afternoon, a detail Jarlence is nervous about Rumpole following up on at all, warning that approaching a prosecution witness informally would be distinctly "not sporting, old bean." That evening, back in chambers, Rumpole telephones the pathologist Professor Ackerman to brief himself on exactly how many pellets a standard twenty-gauge shotgun cartridge should contain (somewhere between 250 and 270) in preparation for cross-examining the Crown's own, much less experienced, forensic witness.

At trial before Mr Justice Twyburn, a judge whose fussy insistence on correct grammar masks a man still quietly tormented by an old wrongful-conviction-adjacent case, that of Martin Muschamp, hanged (in essence) on Twyburn's own summing-up before another man's later confession cleared him, the housekeeper Mrs Hemp testifies to overhearing the fatal quarrel and the words "kill you," to Jonathan Poston walking off toward the woods, and to Jennifer following some ten or fifteen minutes later with her shotgun before a single shot was heard. Rumpole presses hard on the exact wording and emphasis of "kill you," suggesting Jennifer might just as easily have been warning her husband that someone else could kill him as threatening it herself. A second witness, the gamekeeper Figgis, confirms finding Jennifer standing over the body with her broken-open shotgun, only one spent cartridge in it, and hearing her say "I shot him, it was an accident", testimony Rumpole undercuts by exposing Figgis's own illicit pheasant-poaching racket and his unverified presence, with a gun of his own, in his nearby cottage at the very hour of the killing.

Over an awkward lunch recess in the judge's lodgings, Twyburn, seemingly looking for some kind of absolution, dwells at length on the Muschamp case and insists there was nothing else he could have done, showing Rumpole photographs of his roses and grandchildren in between; Rumpole, unwilling to either condemn or comfort him, deflects back to the case at hand and extracts, almost incidentally, a police inspector's firm concession that the official theory holds Jennifer fired exactly one shot and only one. That theory collapses spectacularly under Rumpole's cross-examination of the inexperienced local pathologist, Dr Overton (filling in only because the usual Home Office pathologist, Dr Gravely, was away at a conference), whom Rumpole walks, photograph by photograph, from absolute certainty that there was a single wound to a grudging admission that a second, smaller dark mark on the body could be the central wound of a second shot fired from much further away. The coup de grâce comes when Rumpole produces the actual pellet count recovered from the body (478, nearly double a standard cartridge's typical 250 to 270), making it far more likely that two separate shotguns, not one, struck Jonathan Poston that afternoon.

That night, dining alone at the dismal Tester Arms, Rumpole meets privately with Maurice Fishbourne and gently forces the real issue into the open: that Jonathan Poston's business with him that day was never really about riding ability, but about Jennifer's affair with Fishbourne, and about threats of violence between the two men. Fishbourne reveals he has an unbreakable alibi (he was unexpectedly in London that whole afternoon on a call from his lawyers, confirmed by numerous witnesses), which clears him of firing any shot, but also explains both his earlier visible distress at the races and his odd, guilty laughter afterward. Persuaded by Rumpole that honesty might, for once, actually help the case, Fishbourne agrees to come clean, setting up Jennifer's own long-withheld truth on the stand: she admits the affair, her husband's history of violence toward her, the quarrel and his threat to go beat Fishbourne up, and her own warning that Fishbourne, in his anger, might kill him first. Finding her husband's body on the woodland track and (not yet knowing Fishbourne's alibi) assuming in a panic that her lover had killed him, she went back for her own gun, fired a single cartridge into the already-dead body, and fabricated the "accident" story purely to shield Fishbourne from a murder charge she believed he deserved to face only in her worst imagination, not in fact.

In his closing speech, Rumpole returns explicitly to the racecourse metaphor raised at the start of the episode, insisting that an English criminal trial is nothing like a horse race decided by a blind pin stuck in a list of runners, what matters is not morality or sympathy but whether the jury can be certain Jennifer Poston deliberately killed her husband, when the medical evidence just as plausibly points to a stray, fatal shot fired from Figgis's cottage window, discovered by a grief-stricken and panicked wife who then took extraordinary, misguided steps to protect the man she loved from a crime he, as it turns out, never committed. Twyburn's summing-up proves unexpectedly fair (Rumpole privately credits the ghost of Martin Muschamp for that) and the jury returns a verdict of not guilty.

The episode closes back at the Tester Arms, with Rumpole gleefully declaring his handling of the case "absolutely brilliant" while deflecting Hilda's renewed pestering about finally going to view that country property; he praises Jennifer Poston's remarkable, if badly misguided, devotion to the man she loved, and muses, half in jest, about taking up shooting lessons once they've settled into a rural lodge of their own. But Hilda, perhaps having absorbed rather more about the realities of country life (and death) than she bargained for over the course of the case, quietly reverses herself: rather than exchanging the Gloucester Road flat for a Palladian fantasy, she decides, "for your sake," against Tester after all, leaving the decision, pointedly, as Rumpole's own.
