Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 6, Episode 2

Rumpole and the Summer of Discontent

A union activist is accused of manslaughter; Hilda goes on strike as cook over Rumpole's late hours.

Monday, 4 November 1991 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 4 November 1991 (Series 6), directed by Julian Amyes, written by John Mortimer.

Chambers is in the grip of a modernization drive when the episode opens. Sam Ballard QC, newly self-appointed "chairman of the board" of Equity Court, convenes a meeting to announce that Claude Erskine Brown will serve as "managing director" of a plan to run chambers on strict business lines: a nine-to-six working day, productivity forms, and an automatic coffee machine to replace the old kettle, over Uncle Tom's rambling reminiscence of an earlier such machine that once exploded and destroyed three wills. Rumpole mocks the whole enterprise, while young Liz Probert proposes the opposite direction entirely: a genuinely radical chambers that takes up civil-liberties work and defends trade unionists against what she sees as government strong-arming through the courts, a suggestion Erskine Brown and Ballard dismiss as bad for chambers' image.

That suggestion becomes the episode's main case almost at once. Liz brings Rumpole a brief, on her father's recommendation, to defend Bashar Baker, a union official charged with manslaughter after a coach driver died during a violent picket-line confrontation; Baker is alleged to have thrown the brick that caused the fatal crash. Rumpole, who insists he is taking the case not out of political solidarity but because it is not boring, is convinced of Baker's innocence on instinct: no guilty man sings "The Red Flag" over his victim's body in front of the police, and has to rein in the instructing solicitor, Mr Bernard, who is eager to try his own hand at advocacy at the committal hearing as a "dummy run" for proposed reforms that would let solicitors appear in the higher courts; Bernard's attempt at the magistrates' court is a stumbling disaster that nonetheless results in committal for trial, as Rumpole intended.

At home, Rumpole finds himself cooking for himself: Hilda, fed up with his late nights "carousing" with the clerk Henry over chambers politics, announces she too is "withdrawing her labour" in a domestic echo of the summer's wider industrial unrest, without actually leaving him. Henry, meanwhile, is contemplating industrial action of his own against Erskine Brown's plan to move him off a percentage and onto a fixed wage. Across the wider legal establishment, the same anxiety is rippling through the judiciary: Mr Justice Featherstone and his clerk Wilfred, and Featherstone's fellow judge Mr Justice Simon, are alarmed by a Lord Chancellor's proposal that would let senior solicitors become judges, and a group of senior judges (which Featherstone insists on calling nothing so undignified as a union meeting) begins to plot a response, with Featherstone privately resolved that this time Rumpole will not be allowed to "twist him round his little finger."

At the trial, before Featherstone, the prosecution's witness Gerald Jeb testifies that he saw Baker pick up a brick from the picket line and hurl it at the coach with full force. Rumpole's cross-examination chips away at Jeb's account: establishing that Jeb only came forward with his statement three weeks after the event, and drawing out the fact that Jeb himself nicknamed Baker "Bashar" because of his supposed fondness for "bashing people" on the employer's side, testimony Rumpole treats as revealing bias rather than helping the prosecution. Rumpole also presses, apparently irrelevantly, on the much-publicized prospect of solicitors becoming judges, provoking a flustered, overlong answer from Featherstone that foreshadows how exposed the judge's own anxieties have left him.

Visiting Rumpole at home that evening, the Timson family's Dennis Timson, ostensibly there simply to share holiday snapshots from Marbella, produces a photograph that becomes the case's turning point: it shows the hired coach belonging to the Malloy family, a notorious South London criminal firm, with Gerald Jeb posed alongside young "Peanuts" Malloy and his aunt. Dennis casually mentions that Jeb used to drive getaway cars for Peanuts's father. Rumpole immediately grasps the implication: that Jeb and the garage owner prosecuting the case, Mr Elbaugh, had reason to want the troublesome union man Baker discredited, and that the Malloy family's presence on the picket line that day was no accident of sympathetic solidarity but a hired provocation.

Meanwhile the chambers subplot and the romantic subplot intertwine: Liz Probert, at Rumpole's suggestion, sets about charming Erskine Brown back toward old-fashioned, un-businesslike barristering: flattering his bow tie, encouraging him to take snuff, and declaring her contempt for "consumer choice" and productivity targets, while fending off, for now, his hopes of anything more than an "entirely innocent" evening at the opera. The strategy works: at a later chambers meeting Erskine Brown abruptly withdraws his support from Ballard's business plan, declaring that barristers must preserve their centuries-old tradition as independent freelances, to Uncle Tom's open relief that the new coffee machine is off the agenda and to Ballard's irritation at losing his ally.

At court, Rumpole resumes his cross-examination of Elbaugh directly, forcing the admission that the dispute at his coach garage was over his use of untrained, non-union drivers: the very safety concern that had prompted Baker's picket in the first place. Rumpole puts it to Elbaugh that he recruited the Malloy family to swell the picket line with hired troublemakers, filmed the resulting violence from his office window, and that it was this manufactured chaos, not Baker, that produced the fatal brick-throwing, very possibly by young Peanuts Malloy himself; Rumpole further suggests Jeb's damning evidence against Baker was effectively bought with that Marbella holiday. Elbaugh's answers grow evasive and self-contradictory under the pressure, conceding the Malloys' presence and his personal favour toward Jeb without ever quite admitting the conspiracy outright.

Outside the courtroom, Featherstone's own difficulties catch up with him in a very public way: a newspaper report (seized on gleefully by his wife, Marigold) quotes him, accurately, as having equated the judges' threatened response to the Lord Chancellor's reforms with the industrial action of "shop stewards," and a leading article excoriates judges, drain-clearance operatives, and striking railwaymen in the same breath for inflicting a "summer of discontent" on the public. A chastened Featherstone is forced to see the Lord Chancellor, who gently but firmly makes clear that judges going on strike (let alone picketing) would be a public-relations disaster ahead of an election, deflating the senior judges' brief flirtation with solidarity.

Back at the Bailey, the case against Baker collapses outright: the witness Gerald Jeb, warned to remain available, has fled the country (apparently to Marbella) and Ballard, for the prosecution, concedes that Jeb's flight casts fatal doubt on his evidence, that the brick dust on Baker's trousers is in any case explained by his having genuinely been laying bricks in his own garden, and that the prosecution can no longer responsibly pursue the manslaughter charge. Rumpole accepts the capitulation with dry graciousness, noting that his lordship doubtless has other, more pressing business, a sly reference to Featherstone's own entanglement in the summer's labour troubles.

The episode closes on a note of restored domestic order rather than any lingering industrial strife. Hilda, having compared notes with the equally mortified Marigold Featherstone over their husbands' public embarrassments, decides that ordinary people (as opposed to judges, generals, and other "decision-makers") really have no business going on strike, and ends her own withdrawal of domestic labour by cooking Rumpole a proper Sunday roast beef and Yorkshire pudding. Rumpole, grateful and undemanding, sits down to dinner as instructed, a quiet return to the old order that the episode's title, and its running parallel between picket lines, chambers politics, and the Rumpole marriage, has been building toward throughout.
