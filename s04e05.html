Rumpole of the Bailey

S04E05: Rumpole and the Bright Seraphim

A soldier is accused of murdering his abusive sergeant in Germany; Rumpole shows the timeline is wrong.

In an army barracks in Germany, Captain Betridge of the Special Investigation Branch brings Mrs Wilson the news that her husband, Sergeant James "Jumbo" Wilson, has been found dead near a disco bar called Deros and Cavalier -- oddly dressed, in a way neither of them yet understands. Betridge then cautions a young trooper, Danny Boyne, over the sergeant's death and searches his married quarters while Boyne's wife, Honey, looks after their baby. Back in London, Hilda has brought home a new acquaintance, the retired Major Johnny, whose nephew Captain Sandy Ransom -- defending officer for the accused trooper -- is looking for "an ordinary sort of barrister," specifically not a flashy QC, to defend Boyne at his approaching court-martial. Rumpole, flattered and intrigued despite Hilda's dry reminder that he has never actually conducted a court-martial in his life, agrees to fly out to Germany.

At the airport, Captain Ransom -- a cheerful, practical-joking cavalry officer, scion of a family that has served in the regiment for generations -- collects Rumpole and explains the unit's pride: the 37th/39th, the Duke of Clarence's Own Lancers, nicknamed "the Bright Seraphim" for the sky-blue plumes they wore at the Charge of the Balaclava, with battle honours going back to Malplaquet and Oudenarde and running up through Passchendaele and Alamein. At a formal dinner in the officers' mess, Colonel Hugo Undershaft, Major Graham Sykes, and Lieutenant Alan Hanwick entertain Rumpole with regimental Bollinger and a toast "to the regiment," while Rumpole holds forth, half mockingly, about his own wartime service at RAF Dungeness (where the NAAFI, not the enemy, once scored a direct hit) and delivers a mordant little speech reminding the officers that an army which recruits boys off the street, teaches them to kill, and then cannot find them a decent soldier's death has no business being surprised when they turn out less than models of discipline -- and that the regiment, having picked Trooper Boyne, now owes him its best effort at getting him acquitted.

In the cells, Rumpole and Ransom hear Boyne's account: a quarrel with Sergeant Wilson three weeks earlier, when Boyne told him, in the common Glaswegian idiom, that he'd "carve him up" if picked on again; an alibi built on a pantomime rehearsal that let him out of married quarters at 2100 hours on the night in question; a visit afterward to the Rose and Cavalier disco where he claims to have seen Wilson, in civilian dress, deep in conversation with a mysterious German punk the barmaid calls "Helmut"; and a return to barracks over the back wall around 0100 to dodge the guardroom log, since he was technically confined to barracks at the time. The blood on his shirt cuff, he insists, came from an unrelated earlier scuffle with a German youth who happened to share Sergeant Wilson's rare AB blood group. Rumpole, unconvinced by the convenience of it all, tells Boyne plainly that the truth, whenever he's ready to supply it, would help a good deal more than a well-rehearsed story.

Visiting the married quarters, Rumpole presses Ransom on how little hard evidence there actually is for when Wilson left barracks or who, if anyone, heard or saw anything that night -- prompting Ransom to float, almost too readily, a theory that the mysterious Helmut was Wilson's secret boyfriend, driven to a jealous, remorseful confession-by-anonymous-phone-call after a lovers' quarrel. Back home in London, meanwhile, Hilda lunches on champagne and crab salad with Major Johnny, who pumps her gently for whatever Rumpole has let slip about the case; all she can report is that her husband telephoned asking her to post him a slim forensic pamphlet on the variable factors used in determining time of death -- a detail that, planted this early, promises to matter.

At the court-martial -- presided over by a scrupulously even-tempered president, prosecuted by Colonel Mike Watford (once an articled clerk who ran errands for Rumpole at London Sessions, and now, as Rumpole notes with some relish at the turn of fortune's wheel, his opposite number) -- Rumpole cross-examines the disco barmaid, Greta Schmerz, into conceding that soldiers come to the Deros and Cavalier in civilian clothes, that she had no way of knowing the man in the photograph was a sergeant at all, and that she never actually knew Wilson's name -- which makes it strange indeed that "Helmut" supposedly asked for him by that very name. Mrs Wilson, giving evidence, is led by Rumpole into revealing a raw dislike of Germans generally (her downstairs neighbours being Boyne and his German wife, whom she would never have invited up) and, more strikingly, describes the night of the murder by saying the Germans "took him" -- a word Rumpole insists the court must not simply read as a synonym for "killed."

The medical evidence unravels under Rumpole's questioning of Lieutenant Colonel Basil Borders, the army pathologist who examined the body. Borders cannot say whether Wilson was wearing the dress found on him when he was stabbed or had it put on him afterward; and when pressed on the forensic textbook Rumpole has had Hilda post out to him, Borders is forced to agree that the progress of rigor mortis, a six-degree drop in body temperature, and extensive hypostatic staining in the tissues all point to a time of death around nine o'clock the previous evening -- hours before Boyne claims to have seen Wilson alive at the disco -- and, further, that pale patches in the staining pattern show the body had clearly been moved after death. The president objects that this contradicts the defendant's own statement, but Rumpole's point is precisely that the official timeline, and the convenient German stranger within it, cannot both be true.

That evening, Captain Ransom triumphantly produces new "evidence": a bloodstained black leather jacket, allegedly Helmut's, found buried by a river half a mile from the disco -- and proposes waiting until after the colonel's dinner in the mess before even telling the prosecution. Rumpole, needled rather than reassured by this, delivers his real conclusion in the mess that night: that the entire German-stranger theory was engineered from the start by one officer -- "the joker in the pack" -- determined that it should never be said a Seraphim, rather than a conveniently foreign interloper, had killed Sergeant Wilson. Rumpole lays out what he believes actually happened: Boyne found Wilson already dead near the dustbins outside married quarters, fetched an officer with a Range Rover for help, and together they moved the body -- which is how the sergeant's own blood, not some mythical German's, ended up on Boyne's cuff -- before dressing the corpse in a dame's costume borrowed from the pantomime wardrobe and dumping it near the disco, while Boyne went inside to manufacture a sighting of Wilson, and "Helmut," that was never real.

Confronting Colonel Undershaft directly, Rumpole makes clear he does not believe Ransom -- for all his staged evidence, his invented German, and his own black leather jacket "discovery" -- capable of murder itself, only of covering up what he wrongly believed was Boyne's crime out of loyalty to the regiment's honour. The real motive, Rumpole suggests, lies not with a jealous foreign lover but far closer to home: it must have been bad enough to be the object of Sergeant Wilson's unwanted attentions, as Boyne apparently was, but it must have been far worse to be married to him. As Captain Betridge is heard, in an unmistakable echo of the episode's opening, cautioning Mrs Wilson that anything she says may be given in evidence, Colonel Undershaft bids Rumpole a rueful goodbye, conceding that whatever damage has been done to the regiment's honour, Rumpole has at least found out the truth -- though, as the colonel dryly notes, that is not the kind of service that earns a man a medal.

Back home, Hilda confesses to a wistful fit of what-might-have-been -- her singing, perhaps even the army itself -- brought on by Major Johnny's champagne and attention while Rumpole was away; Rumpole, noting drily that the major seems to have vanished now that his real job (finding his nephew a biddable, unquestioning barrister) has been accomplished, has no patience for nostalgia, and closes the episode raising a glass instead to "the regiment, coupled with the name of sergeant major -- She Who Must Be Obeyed."

Originally broadcast 16 February 1987 (Series 4), directed by Martyn Friend, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).
