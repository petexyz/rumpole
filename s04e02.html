Rumpole of the Bailey

S04E02: Rumpole and the Blind Tasting

A Timson is rearrested over a stolen case of wine; Rumpole is knocked out early in a blind wine tasting.

26 January 1987 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 26 January 1987 (Series 4), directed by Roger Bamford, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).

The episode opens with Detective Inspector Bellman raiding a Timson household and finding an unlikely haul: crates of fine wine stashed alongside the frozen peas, which a bewildered Tony Timson insists he only keeps for his chips. In court, Rumpole is already defending a different Timson relative, Hugh "Snakelegs" Timson, who inherited the family's fencing (receiving-stolen-goods) business from his uncle Percy on Percy's retirement to Benidorm, on a charge of receiving stolen silver sugar bowls. The presiding judge is the dour, funereal Judge Graves, who repeatedly cuts off Rumpole's trademark meandering addresses to the jury -- including a long digression about aunts who hide banknotes in tea caddies -- with weary, pointed interruptions. Rumpole nonetheless wins an acquittal: none of the supposed burglary victims will come forward to identify their silver, since they have already collected the insurance money and have no wish to get their sugar bowls back and repay it. Celebrating afterward at Pomeroy's with Claude Erskine Brown, Rumpole airily defends his taste for cheap "Pomeroy's Plonk" against Erskine Brown's connoisseurship, and is talked into attending a blind-tasting evening hosted by Erskine Brown's old schoolfriend, wine merchant Martin Vanbury -- an invitation Rumpole accepts mainly as a way to cheer himself up before the jury's verdict.

Outside court, newly qualified barrister Liz Probert introduces herself to Rumpole, eager for career advice and keen to needle him about outdated bar traditions and class politics; Rumpole, unbothered, points out that the Timsons are in fact committed small-"c" capitalists who despise closed shops and would open their fences all night if they could. At Vanbury's shop that evening, Rumpole is thoroughly out of his depth among serious wine people: Honoria Bird, longtime wine correspondent of the Sunday Examiner, and Monty Mantis, a garage owner from Luton with an improbably refined palate for cheap plonk, both outclass him in the blind-tasting game, guessing vintages and regions with ease while Rumpole guesses wildly and gets "knocked out" early. The final bottle, presented as a great rarity, is correctly identified by Honoria Bird -- with distinct, friendly prompting from Monty Mantis -- as a 1971 Cheval Blanc, a particularly fine Bordeaux. It is a scene that seems like a throwaway comic set piece at the time, but will matter a great deal later.

Back in chambers, Head of Chambers Sam Ballard QC is absorbed in a Church Times column by "Canon Probert" on crime and retribution, and laments that chambers lacks anyone with real moral fibre. The timing is pointed: Liz Probert is waiting to learn whether her pupillage application will succeed, and when Rumpole realizes her surname matches Ballard's favourite moralizing columnist, he deliberately lets Ballard believe she comes from a devout clerical family -- a convenient, unconfirmed inference that eases her path onto the Pupillage Committee over the objections of Uncle Tom (worried about too many mouths to feed) and Erskine Brown (who has his own, less professional reasons for wanting her around). Liz, for her part, privately tells Rumpole a very different story about her father -- that he is in fact "Red Ron" Threlfall, the radical Labour leader of a North London council -- and sworn Rumpole to secrecy about it, leaving the truth of her parentage, and Ballard's assumptions about it, charmingly unresolved. Ballard does separately entertain a visitor who introduces himself only as "Probert, Lizzie's father" and chats amiably with him about moral revolution and the "clerical workers' union," apparently never noticing his guest isn't wearing a clerical collar.

Liz is duly made Rumpole's pupil and thrown straight into his next case: Tony Timson, found with the cases of fine wine -- not, as he insists to Liz's visible exasperation, the product of a "broken home," since his parents were married forty years, but bought for cash from an unnamed man in a pub, in a story Rumpole notes is wearing thin with the judges. Instructing solicitor Mr. Bernard separately raises a knottier point of law on another Timson matter -- the House of Lords' recent ruling in a case called Swinglehurst on the doctrine of impossible attempts, relevant to a charge that Tony Timson attempted to steal three television sets that did not, in fact, exist -- a legal curiosity Rumpole fobs off onto Liz to research, admitting with some pride that after a lifetime at the bar he has almost no real interest in the law itself, preferring bloodstains, disputed typewriting, and a hair or two on a cardigan.

The stakes in the wine case sharpen once Rumpole learns that the chief loser of the stolen wine, and the Crown's principal witness, is none other than Martin Vanbury -- the same wine merchant who hosted the blind tasting, and by Rumpole's account "a highly respectable public school bully." Visiting the expert witness Monty Mantis at home (with Liz along, warned not to repeat to anyone that Mantis's own daughter is involved in local Labour politics), Rumpole hears Mantis's unexpectedly moving account of discovering cheap French railway-station wine as a schoolboy on a trip to Boulogne, and his lifelong preference for honest plonk over pretension -- plus his considered opinion that Vanbury's supposed Cheval Blanc, even the real thing, was "somewhat better" than what Rumpole had tasted. Back in chambers, a running gag plays out around which judge will hear the wine case: with Judge Bullingham safely on holiday in Pamplona, Rumpole and clerk Henry maneuver to get the gentler Judge Moulsey assigned instead of the dreaded Graves -- only for Moulsey to come down with a cold, sending the case straight back to Graves after all.

At trial, Rumpole's cross-examination of Vanbury is a slow build: he establishes that Vanbury has been trading as a wine merchant for only three years, having previously run a Chelsea gallery, "Vanbury Fine Arts," which also happened to suffer a major, fully insured burglary. With no documentary proof in hand, Rumpole gambles on a direct accusation that Vanbury has made a habit of staging break-ins for the insurance money -- a charge Vanbury deflects by blaming "the rising tide of lawlessness." The courtroom centerpiece follows: Rumpole applies to open and taste the supposedly priceless bottle of Cheval Blanc entered as exhibit four, over Judge Graves's incredulous objections, winning the point only by threatening an immediate appeal. The wine proves to be exactly what Rumpole suspected -- cheap, rough plonk, which he gleefully pronounces "Chateau Thames Embankment, 1986, a particularly brutal year" -- and not remotely the fifty-pound bottle Vanbury had claimed and insured it as.

Rumpole then calls Honoria Bird, who confirms under oath that the bottle she "correctly" identified as Cheval Blanc at Vanbury's own tasting event was, on reflection, nothing of the kind -- she had simply been steered toward the answer by Monty Mantis's helpful hints, following the label rather than her own palate. A further witness, Vanbury's employee Mr. Eastham, describes how a mysterious new consignment of wine arrived at the shop shortly before the burglary, how Vanbury had him move most of it to one side of the cellar while leaving a few bottles exposed in the old Cheval Blanc bin, and how calmly Vanbury took the subsequent theft -- "it's all insured." Judge Graves, struggling to follow the logic, wonders aloud why anyone would pass off cheap wine as expensive stock only to have it stolen rather than sold; Rumpole's answer lands cleanly: Vanbury never meant the wine to be drunk at all, genuine or not -- he had it in his possession purely to be stolen, so he could collect the insurance on a fraudulent claim.

In his closing address, Rumpole drives the point home to the jury: the theft was as false as the wine itself, meaning Vanbury had effectively arranged to sell his own fraudulent property to be carried away and disposed of. Tony Timson, buying the crates from the man in the pub without any idea of this scheme, was never receiving genuinely stolen goods, because the goods were never stolen in any sense the law recognises -- they were planted by their own owner to defraud his insurers. On that basis, Rumpole submits, his client has committed no crime at all, echoing almost exactly the shape of his argument in the sugar-bowls case that opened the episode.

That night at home, Hilda needles Rumpole about his latest win and about the state of affairs in chambers -- Ballard's continuing, oblivious conviction that Liz Probert's visiting father is some kind of clergyman, and the departure in high dudgeon of Hilda's old schoolfriend Dodo Mackintosh, who came to see Rumpole "in action" at the Bailey and instead witnessed what she took to be an inappropriate breakfast tête-à-tête between Rumpole and his glamorous young pupil at a café, and left declaring herself disgusted. Hilda plainly doesn't believe a word of Dodo's suspicion -- she knows Rumpole well enough to find the idea absurd -- and the episode closes on a softer note than its courtroom theatrics, with Rumpole quoting Keats's "Ode to a Nightingale" at his wife and, half in earnest, inviting Hilda to "fade away with me into the forest dim."
