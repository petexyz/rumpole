Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 1, Episode 4

Rumpole and the Married Lady

1975 divorce case; overheard calls lead Hilda to leave for Dodo's; opposing counsel is George Frobisher.

Aired Monday, 24 April 1978 · ITV (Thames Television)

The episode opens on a dramatised exchange of the spiteful little notes at the centre of the case to come: Mr and Mrs Thripp of Muswell Hill, addressing each other only as "my so-called husband" and "my so-called wife," trading accusations about shirts, biscuits, and who is bankrupting whom, before cutting to the Rumpoles' own kitchen. Horace Rumpole is lost in the crossword while Hilda ("She Who Must Be Obeyed") complains that chambers business has dried up, that he lounges about in his dressing gown, and that their son Nick, now gone back to America to await a university post in Baltimore and, presumably, a wedding, has left the house feeling emptier than ever. The phone call that interrupts them is clerk Henry, ringing to report an unexpected "con" waiting at chambers; to Rumpole's mixed relief and disappointment, it turns out to be a divorce brief rather than a crime.

In the taxi, Rumpole muses in voiceover on the odd durability of marriage next to the rising figures for divorce, and on chambers as a refuge of "peace and quiet" compared with Hilda's rule at home. At chambers he meets, for the first time, Miss Phillida Trant, a recent pupil of fellow barrister Erskine Brown's who has been sitting with them a few months and asks to observe the divorce conference to widen her experience; Rumpole grumbles that theirs is "about the only chambers without a woman" and that old clerk Albert, Henry's predecessor, never wanted one, but relents. Waiting outside with a toy aeroplane is young Norman Thripp, the "child of the family" in Thripp v. Thripp, brought along by his mother.

In conference, Mrs Thripp lays out her grievance: her husband has not spoken a single word to her in three years, communicating solely through typed notes left for her to find, demanding his shirts be done by "her," complaining of a damaged lounge suite that was her mother's present, accusing her of running up the gas bill on bathwater, threatening to take Norman and leave her to her mother-in-law in Rickmansworth. Rumpole, hunting for grounds of "intolerable conduct" under the Divorce Reform Act 1969 (a phrase Miss Trant, freshly out of pupillage, can recite by heart), presses Mrs Thripp for concrete cruelty (a black eye "would come in extremely handy," he observes dryly, since there is none) and is intrigued rather than convinced when she admits she once cut up her husband's evening trousers with nail scissors before he set off, alone, to his Masonic ladies' night. Despite the thinness of the case, Mrs Thripp is adamant: she wants her husband "taken to court," nothing less.

That night, over drinks at Pomeroy's Wine Bar, Rumpole tells lifelong bachelor George Frobisher that the brief is worth six days in court and six refreshers at fifty pounds a day, not bad for a hundred-and-fifty-pound divorce, while George muses wistfully on having avoided marriage altogether. Back at the Rumpoles' house that evening, Rumpole overhears a hushed, intimate telephone call, a voice promising "we've waited three years, haven't we" and arranging to meet at four o'clock the next day, "not here," and calls out "Hilda" in sudden, startled suspicion of an affair, though the matter is dropped rather than resolved in the moment.

The next morning in chambers, Erskine Brown teases Rumpole mercilessly about lending Miss Trant to "the seamy side" of family law, "Rumpole of the boudoir," while Rumpole needles him back about Erskine Brown's own dull conveyancing briefs. In a further conference, it emerges the Thripps' notes were all typed on their shared household Olivetti, which rules out any of the obvious suspects (Mr Thripp's secretary turns out to be an elderly grandmother), but Rumpole's questioning draws out an admission that undercuts Mrs Thripp's own case: she had, in fact, minded very much not being asked to the Masonic ladies' night, because she wanted to go with her husband. Miss Trant frets that her client will make a poor witness; Rumpole insists the opposite, pronouncing Mrs Thripp "an excellent witness" precisely because she broke down at the right dramatic moment.

Henry then hands Rumpole a far shabbier brief, twenty-five pounds to defend an ageing prostitute, Mrs Wayne, against a charge of keeping a disorderly house before the stipendiary magistrate "Archie" McPhee at Dock Street. Miss Trant, prosecuting as practice, asks Rumpole's advice on how much law to cite; not mentioning that he is her opponent, he tells her McPhee, a rose-growing legal pedant desperate to catch his train, will "love her for it" if she piles on every case she can find. In the Magistrates' Court she duly buries the prosecution under citations (Singleton v. Ellison, 1895; R. v. Jones; the Sexual Offences Act), visibly delighting and infuriating McPhee in equal measure, while Rumpole, defending, dismantles the police "observations" with a single line from Scripture about false witness. The summons is dismissed and Mrs Wayne walks free, a sabotage dressed up as mentorship that Miss Trant does not yet understand she has suffered.

Crushed in the corridor afterward, Miss Trant blames her own advocacy; Rumpole, almost kindly, tells her that knowing the law, she was top of her year, has "almost nothing to do with being a lawyer," and that her real failure was not reading McPhee's personality rather than his law reports. He reminisces about his glory days on the Penge Bungalow Murders and the Great Brighton Benefit Club forgery case (the source, he notes, of his expert knowledge of typewriters), ruefully charts his own decline from those cases to disorderly houses and divorce, and tells her that law and prostitution are "the two oldest professions in the world," both existing to please.

At home, Hilda announces plans to have tea with an old school friend, Dodo Perkins, who now runs a tea shop in Hampshire, to ask her advice about an unspecified "something." Tension escalates further that evening: Hilda, suitcase packed, accuses Rumpole of keeping "a harem" of female clients who ring him at home at all hours, but the call that interrupts them turns out to be only Mrs Thripp, ringing about the hearing being expedited to Monday, resolving the earlier mysterious phone call as professional rather than romantic (though Hilda, unmollified, still threatens to decamp to Dodo's). That same night, Rumpole and George rehearse their opposing cases by play-acting the Thripps themselves (it emerges George is representing the husband), their mock marital quarrel over bathwater, padlocked geysers, and ruined trousers sliding seamlessly into their own real grievances about bachelor and married life.

In the Family Division before Mrs Justice Appleby, sitting alone without a jury, Rumpole opens with florid "Bluebeard" rhetoric about a wife imprisoned, denied biscuits and bathwater and human conversation, rhetoric the judge repeatedly slaps down, uninterested in oratory and demanding only dates. Under Rumpole's cross-examination, Mr Thripp is driven, haltingly, to admit that he wanted his wife at the ladies' night because he still loves her, a damaging, almost unplanned admission that visibly threatens to collapse the whole adversarial case, prompting the judge to warn Rumpole he has "asked one question too many." Sensing a reconciliation, Mrs Justice Appleby urges both sides to meet in counsel's chambers for young Norman's sake and adjourns overnight; in the corridor afterward George grumbles that women have no business on the bench since reconciliation will cost them both their refreshers, while Rumpole wryly notes Mrs Thripp has "been playing bingo," his private, undercutting phrase for her secretary alibi, even as the case drifts toward settlement.

At a cafe the next morning, Mrs Thripp produces a fresh and vicious note, supposedly found propped against the cornflakes packet that morning, and declares she means to fight on after all. Rumpole spots that it is typed on a different machine, a standard Imperial, not the Thripps' old Olivetti, and, over doughnuts, gently interrogates young Norman alone. He deduces that the boy forged the note himself, on the Imperial in Rumpole's own chambers that he had been fiddling with the previous evening, to keep the profitable and attention-getting case, and the "arms race" of rival parental bribery it was generating for him, alive. Invoking the four-year sentence handed out in the real Brighton forgery case, Rumpole talks Norman into confessing to his parents and abandoning any ambition for a career in crime in favour of becoming a chartered accountant like his father. Back in court, Rumpole announces the reconciliation is genuine; the petition is dismissed, with costs against the husband, and Mrs Justice Appleby tells a deflated George that he should "stick to crime" rather than amateur detective work in the family division. That evening at home, Rumpole tells Hilda that his six lucrative refreshers have gone "with the wind," reflecting that however silent, venomous, and unbearable a marriage becomes, neither party can truly stand to be alone, that couples would "rather have war together than a lonely peace," while Hilda, unmoved by the philosophy, answers only by reminding him to switch off the lavatory light, closing the episode on that note of restored, bickering domestic equilibrium.
