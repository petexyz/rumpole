Rumpole of the Bailey

S05E06: Rumpole and the Quality of Life

A noblewoman is charged with murdering her elderly husband; subplots on discrimination at Equity Court and Rumpole's diet.

The episode opens on a squabble at Derwent House, where Perdita Derwent, her stepdaughter Helen Derwent, and Perdita's mother-in-law Mrs. Barbara Derwent ("Bunty") bicker over a forgotten order of fish for Sir Daniel Derwent's Friday fish pie -- a trivial domestic flashpoint that the trial will later make much of. The scene cuts to Rumpole's own doctor's surgery, where a solemn doctor runs through Rumpole's daily diet item by item -- fried eggs, bacon, sausage and tomato at the Tastee Bite, steak and kidney pudding and a pint of "draught Guinness" at Pommeroy's for lunch, "crumpet at tea time," claret in the evening -- and prescribes a punishing regime of no wine, meat, fish, eggs, bread, butter, milk, sugar, or pastry, substituting a free-energy cereal mixed with water. Rumpole, half-serious, tells the doctor that on the days he has the medical evidence running and the jury on his side, Horace Rumpole could live forever -- but concedes he won't.

At chambers, Head of Chambers Sam Ballard QC has taken up a fitness crusade, marching Rumpole along the Embankment and brandishing a chest-expander he intends to use "in the odd free moment" to keep chambers fit now that "the war is over." Ballard reveals, with transparent self-importance, that he is briefed to lead Rumpole in a major murder case -- the killing of the well-known painter Sir Daniel Derwent, R.A. -- and that he intends to lead "from behind" despite having, by his own account, "brought victory home alone and without a leader" in the (fictional) Penge Bungalow Murders. In the corridor, pupil Liz Probert and Claude Erskine Brown spar over an Islington council gay-and-lesbian-rights demonstration; Erskine Brown's clumsy needling and Liz's pointed question -- how many gay or lesbian members do these chambers have? -- plant the seed of the episode's chambers subplot.

At a conference at Derwent House, Ballard questions Lady Perdita Derwent with heavy-handed tact about the allegation that she administered a fatal overdose of diamorphine to her husband after he had made a will leaving her his entire fortune, something over two million pounds. Rumpole pulls him aside to recite the first rule of murder defense -- never ask the client if she did it, in case she tells you -- and the questioning proceeds obliquely instead. Perdita's history emerges: she was a student when she fell in love with her professor, Daniel, who divorced his first wife (Helen's mother) to marry her when she was twenty-three; Helen, some five years older than her stepmother, never forgave Perdita for "taking Daniel away," and the household, which also includes the genial, overweight former dancer Bunty Derwent, has been tense ever since. Rumpole establishes that Daniel was already terminally ill and would have died soon regardless of any inheritance, which makes the money motive look shaky -- but a pamphlet called "Across the River," advocating euthanasia, was found among Perdita's clothes, and she cannot explain how it got there. Rumpole, feeling suddenly unwell, cuts the conference short and asks Perdita to drive him back to the Temple.

That evening at home, Hilda defends the doctor's new regime against Rumpole's complaints about his "nourishing low-calorie soup" (which Rumpole insists on calling, dismissively, "Fenevite"), and the two spar over whether mere survival -- battery hens, veal calves, and Judge Graves are all still technically alive, Rumpole points out -- counts as a life worth having without "something to live for," by way of example, steak and kidney pudding. The next morning at a cafe, Liz Probert recruits a young barrister named Dave Inchcape to apply to Rumpole's chambers, cheerfully admitting she plans to use Claude Erskine Brown's evident attraction to her to get Dave an interview and a place, "all in a good cause."

Erskine Brown, standing in for the convalescing-case-laden Ballard, interviews Dave Inchcape as deputy head of chambers and, primed by Liz's talk of non-discrimination, wrongly assumes the relevant "discrimination" concern is that Dave is gay rather than simply young and inexperienced. The interview becomes a sustained comedy of crossed wires, Erskine Brown earnestly assuring Dave that his "experiences" with a colleague called Tommy Tomkins are entirely his own business, invoking his own children Tristan and Risolda as proof he has no prejudice, and finally proposing, with elaborate tact, that Dave share a room with Liz Probert rather than with any of the male members of chambers -- a decision whose rationale neither Dave nor the audience is ever quite let in on.

The trial opens in a courtroom where prosecuting counsel Mr. Marcus Griffin lays out the Crown's case: Sir Daniel Derwent died of a massive diamorphine overdose; he had left his entire two-million-pound estate to his wife Perdita, making no testamentary provision (though generous lifetime provision) for his mother Bunty or his daughter Helen, both of whom lived with him at his house in Chelsea. Ballard, ostensibly leading, proves hopeless at the practicalities, repeatedly needing Rumpole to feed him questions from behind, which draws the judge's dry observation that "your junior is trying to tell you something." Nurse Gregson then testifies to the afternoon of the death: she gave Sir Daniel his routine top-up injection of diamorphine while Perdita sat for a portrait, half-undressed, in the studio; she left her medical bag unattended in the hall while Bunty made tea and Perdita briefly left to help; the next morning she discovered a quantity of diamorphine ampoules missing from that bag, and learned that same morning that Sir Daniel had died in the night. Ballard, cross-examining badly, gets Nurse Gregson to concede that Sir Daniel could in theory have injected himself -- but she insists, firmly, that he had a horror of hypodermic needles and could never have done it alone.

Back in the robing room, Ballard knocks himself unconscious with his own chest-expander, and is carted off to hospital by Matron, leaving Rumpole to carry on the trial alone with Liz Probert as his junior -- over the judge's bemused objection that an accident in Queen's Counsel's own robing room seems an unlikely basis for an adjournment. Helen Derwent then testifies that she found the telltale ampoule wrappers, the syringe, and the euthanasia pamphlet in Perdita's bedroom the morning after her father's death. Rumpole's cross-examination draws out, bit by bit, Helen's deep contempt for her stepmother -- "very young and feckless," unable even to boil an egg, and guilty, on the night in question, of the unforgivable sin of forgetting to order the fish for her father's beloved Friday fish pie, so that the household ate omelettes (cooked, it emerges, by Bunty) and mulligatawny soup instead. Rumpole suggests to Helen that her father, a painter whose illness was robbing him of the use of his hand, might simply have chosen to take his own life rather than live without his art -- and presses her on why she searched Perdita's room alone the next morning, floating the possibility that she went there not to find evidence but to plant it, since a murder conviction would cut Perdita out of the will entirely and split the two million pounds between Helen and her grandmother.

In Rumpole's closing speech to the jury, delivered without a leader for the first time in the trial, he argues that the Crown's "pathetic prosecution" rests on nothing but the word of a cold, calculating, and financially interested witness, and invites the jury to picture a dying artist, robbed of his gift, choosing his own end rather than be convicted on evidence that could as easily have been planted as found. Immediately afterward, in a corridor confrontation, Rumpole turns not on Helen but on Bunty Derwent, the mother -- pointing out that she is herself a member of the Across the River euthanasia society and that the pamphlet was hers; that she, not Helen, cooked the individual omelettes and the mulligatorny soup that night, perfectly placed to disguise the taste of diamorphine in her son's food; and that she had every reason, as a mother watching her son lose the one thing -- his painting -- that made his life worth living, to end it for him herself, with Helen later finding the ampoules and quietly moving the evidence into Perdita's room to protect her. Bunty does not really deny it, telling Rumpole only that "a mother knows," that there was no need to discuss it with her son first, and that it was "for the best." The jury returns a verdict of not guilty, and Perdita is discharged.

In the aftermath, a recovered but self-congratulating Ballard returns from hospital to claim much of the credit for the acquittal, with Matron hovering over him as his "ministering angel," while Hilda pointedly reminds Matron that Rumpole conducted the murder case "largely on his own," just as he once did the Penge Bungalow Murders. At a chambers meeting, Dave Inchcape's admission is formally proposed; Uncle Tom contributes a rambling, dated anecdote about an earlier chambers' first Black member going on to become a prime minister "of Limpopoland," while Erskine Brown, still under his original misapprehension, is visibly startled to discover Dave flirting with a woman at the chambers party and declares him "a raving heterosexual." Ballard then makes his own announcement: during his hospital convalescence he and Matron -- Mrs. Marguerite Plumstead, a widow, her late husband having "passed over," in Ballard's phrase, "across the river" himself -- have become engaged, and the episode closes at their wedding reception, where Hilda and Matron trade barbed pleasantries over the wedding cake, Henry announces that his own wife has become Lady Mayoress of Bexleyheath, and word arrives that Rumpole's diet doctor has dropped dead, considerably younger than Rumpole himself. Rumpole closes the episode with a toast to Ballard and his bride that folds the whole hour's preoccupation with mortality, dieting, and euthanasia into one dry line: it is not life alone but "the quality of life" that counts, and he raises his glass to it, and to Pommeroy's, accordingly.
