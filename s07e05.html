Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 7, Episode 5

Rumpole and the Family Pride

A suspicious drowning on a lord's estate; he invites Rumpole and Hilda for a weekend and asks Horace to represent him at the inquest.

Thursday, 26 November 1992 · ITV (Thames Television)

The opening breakfast-table montage of gossip-column items (society names, a "triple murder" confession, a charity ball) is especially garbled and is only loosely reconstructed below; a later reference to the poem behind Dave Inchcape's chosen surname is likewise garbled but is clearly meant as Robert Southey's "The Inchcape Rock." Originally broadcast 26 November 1992 (Series 7), directed by John Gorrie, written by John Mortimer.

Over breakfast, Hilda reads aloud from a society gossip column (old acquaintances, a triple-murder confession, a charity ball) until she lands on a feature about Sackbutt Castle in the north of England, home of the 17th Baron, Richard Sackbutt, and his young second wife, Rosemary. Rosemary, Hilda explains with some pride, is her first cousin once removed; the family's failure to invite the Rumpoles to the wedding rankles more than Rumpole can be bothered to care about. Meanwhile, up at the castle's lake, the local coroner, Dr Hugo Swayby, a showy, hunting-obsessed amateur sleuth who clearly relishes the drama, begins inquiring into the death of an unidentified elderly woman pulled from the water, whom Lord Richard Sackbutt flatly denies ever having set eyes on before.

Back in London, Rumpole visits a new client, Walter "the Wally" Wilkinson, who has walked into a police station to confess to a notorious triple murder and is clearly enjoying the minor celebrity this earns him on remand: extra biscuits, cigarettes, newspaper clippings saved by an obliging constable. In chambers, Liz Probert is uncharacteristically distraught over some secret about her boyfriend, junior barrister Dave Inchcape, that she refuses to name but calls "unmentionable."

A letter arrives from cousin Rosemary at last, inviting the Rumpoles to Sackbutt Castle for the weekend. Hilda frets over bringing the wrong clothes; once there, the couple meet Lord Richard, Rosemary, and Richard's sullen son by an earlier relationship, Jonathan, who wants nothing to do with riding, hunting, or any of it. Richard treats the drowned vagrant woman as a tiresome bore rather than a tragedy, irritated mainly that the local coroner, Dr Swayby, seems determined to turn a routine accident into a sensational inquest for his own glory.

That evening, Richard privately asks Rumpole, newly famous among the servants and locals for his courtroom reputation, including the long-ago Penge Bungalow Murders, to represent the family's interests at the inquest, over the quiet objection of the family's own solicitor, Mr Curceter, who cannot understand why he was passed over. Around the estate and at a country dog show, Rumpole picks up pieces of Sackbutt family history from a gossipy colonel and from Richard himself: Richard's mother, nicknamed "Maggi," left his father for an Italian prisoner of war working on the estate farm after the war; Richard, informed by his headmaster at the age of nine that his mother had died, never questioned the story and never tried to find out more, deferring entirely to a father he could not imagine lying to him. Rumours have apparently circulated locally for years that she did not, in fact, die.

Dr Swayby produces what he clearly considers his trump card: a photograph found among the dead woman's possessions, showing a man unmistakably identifiable as Richard's father in 1940s army uniform, alongside a woman and a baby, on the castle terrace. The implication hangs unspoken but obvious: that the dead vagrant might be Richard's own long-vanished mother, returned after decades to ask the family for money, giving Richard a motive to want her silenced before she could make a scene or a claim on the estate.

At the formal coroner's inquest, pathologist Dr Malkin testifies that a head wound and the absence of water in the lungs point toward a possible unlawful killing, though Rumpole's cross-examination, raising the woman's high blood alcohol level, an almost-empty gin bottle, and a blood-stained tree stump on the bank, forces him to concede that an accidental fall remains entirely possible. Far more damaging is the testimony of the castle gatekeeper, Mr Saggers, who says he saw the dead woman talking with Lord Richard in the formal garden the previous afternoon, directly contradicting Richard's sworn statement, given at the scene and repeated under oath, that he had never laid eyes on her before her body was found.

Rumpole confronts Richard with this contradiction in open court, and, over Mr Curceter's furious objections and Dr Swayby's barely concealed delight at the scandal, presses him on whether his father might have lied decades earlier about his mother's death out of wounded pride after her desertion, and whether Richard, deep down, feared this unidentified vagrant might be that very same mother, finally come looking for him. Richard insists he never spoke to her and that his father would never have lied to him, but the photograph, and his own contradicted denial, leave the suggestion hanging damningly before the jury.

The resolution comes from a source Rumpole has quietly arranged himself: unbeknownst to the family, he had solicitor Mr Bernard place personal advertisements in the broadsheets addressed to "Ricardino" (his mother's old Italian pet name for Richard), asking her to come forward urgently. The advertisement works: Mrs Petronelli, formerly Lady Margaret Sackbutt, appears as a witness, very much alive, now a widow living in Southwark and doing charity work among London's homeless. She testifies that the dead woman, whom she knew only as "Bertha," was not her at all but a homeless woman she had briefly taken in for a night out of kindness, to whom she had confided her own family history, and who then stole the old family photograph from her desk before disappearing, apparently intending to use it to talk her way into Sackbutt Castle and extract money from Richard by implying some connection to his lost mother.

The inquest jury returns a verdict of accidental death, exactly the outcome Rumpole had quietly predicted to Hilda beforehand: turned away by the gatekeeper for lacking the entrance fee, and later rebuffed by Richard himself when she hinted she had "news" worth paying for, the woman spent the rest of the afternoon drinking gin around the village before stumbling, unsteady and alone, into the lake that evening and striking her head on a tree stump as she fell. Rumpole privately concludes that Richard had lied about ever speaking to her not out of guilt, but out of a half-formed, unexamined fear that she might genuinely have been his long-lost mother: a possibility too dangerous to admit, since it would mean confronting the fact that the father he revered had lied to him his entire life.

Back in London, the parallel plots resolve neatly. The prosecution against Walter Wilkinson collapses once the real triple murderer is caught elsewhere, and a street acquaintance named Paddy reveals to Rumpole the embarrassing truth: Wilkinson had only knocked out a fellow derelict, Bronco Billington, in a drunken argument and wrongly believed he had killed him (Bronco turns out to be very much alive, drinking and singing in the gutter), but Wilkinson had confessed to someone else's glamorous triple murder rather than face being jailed for nothing more than a minor assault, pure snobbery about his own notoriety. Rumpole also resolves Liz Probert's crisis over Dave Inchcape, learning at the dog show that Inchcape is in fact the Honourable David Luxter, grandson of a peer, who adopted a pseudonym borrowed from a schoolroom poem rather than trade on his aristocratic name; drawing an explicit parallel to Richard Sackbutt's own cold, lied-to upbringing, Rumpole persuades Liz that children of the aristocracy are themselves a kind of deprived class, and she agrees to take her "Honourable" boyfriend back. The episode ends with Hilda certain the Rumpoles will never be invited to Sackbutt Castle again, and Rumpole entirely unsurprised to find that both of his cases, high and low alike, had come down in the end to family pride and the lies families tell to protect it.
