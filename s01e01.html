Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 1, Episode 1

Rumpole and the Younger Generation

1967 case: Rumpole defends a Timson clan member; his own son seems to pick up Timson traits.

Monday, 3 April 1978 · ITV (Thames Television)

After a brief flash-forward to a butcher's-shop robbery in progress (two old men minding the Friday takings, a scuffle, someone shouting "get out of here"), the episode proper opens at the Rumpoles' house, where Horace Rumpole recites Wordsworth's "Intimations of Immortality" at breakfast and twists its lines into a private joke about his son Nick, who is coming home from his public school that day. His wife Hilda reminds him he promised Nick a "show" and is herself off to Horsham to visit her father, Rumpole's head of chambers C. H. Wystan, referred to throughout, with weary affection, as "Daddy." The only show Rumpole can offer is a robbery-with-violence case in Number Two Court, and he regrets aloud that it isn't a murder, since Nick so enjoyed his murders. In the Old Bailey cells, the accused, teenage Jim Timson, is visited by his father Fred, who coaches him on courtroom etiquette ("call the judge my lord, not sir") and reassures him that the family has got him "the best brief in the business, Mr. Rumpole." Jim is anxious about a boy called "Peanuts" giving evidence against him.

At chambers, clerk Albert hands out briefs while Erskine Brown grumbles that he is sick of crime and wants civil work, drawing Rumpole's rejoinder that "a person who's sick of crime is sick of life." George Frobisher gets a nuisance case at Bloomsbury County Court; old Uncle Tom, long retired from real practice, pottars about. Rumpole learns his opponent in the robbery case is again Guthrie Featherstone, who affects exhaustion from an all-night Commons sitting and needles Rumpole, not for the first time, about never having taken silk. Rumpole deflects ("Rumpole QC" would only be called "Rumpole Queer Customer") and heads for the Bailey, inviting Erskine Brown along to watch and take a note. At the Bailey itself he is swarmed by what feels like an entire extended family of former clients: the Timsons. Solicitor Mr. Bernard introduces Fred and his wife Vi, and between them they walk Rumpole through a rogues' gallery of Timson relations: Uncle Cyril (housebreaking), Uncle Dennis (forged logbooks), Dennis's wife Doris (receiving stolen scampi), establishing the family as habitual, good-natured criminals who nonetheless insist Jim is innocent this time.

Over cafe coffee, Rumpole hears the case properly. Jim is charged with robbing the till from a butcher's shop with a gang of youths, all of whom escaped except him; the prosecution's evidence is not a police confession but an alleged admission Jim made to a boy named Peanuts Malloy while the two shared a painting class on remand. Fred Timson is scandalized at the idea his son would ever speak to a Malloy at all: the two families have been estranged for a generation, ever since Peanuts's grandfather informed on Fred's father over the old Streatham Co-op robbery; the Timsons and Malloys, as Rumpole later tells the court, are "rather like the Montagues and the Capulets." Fred and Vi swear Jim would never have spoken to Peanuts. When Rumpole meets Jim directly, the boy offers a ready-made alibi: tea at Auntie Doris's, the television on with two uncles, all timed precisely to cover the hour of the robbery. Rumpole recognises it instantly as a family production, "clubbed together for it like a new bicycle," and refuses to use it: cross-examining a parade of Timson relatives on their own criminal records would only confirm the jury's worst assumptions about the boy, even if the alibi happens to be true.

In Court Number Two, the case opens before the patrician, faintly baffled Mr. Justice Everglade, nicknamed "Flottie," a judge more at home with international trust companies than Old Bailey slang, who repeatedly loses the thread of Rumpole's jokes and Jim's own idiom ("ejusdem generis," borrowed from Latin and deployed by Rumpole as a running gag at the judge's expense). Featherstone, prosecuting, is careful to tell the jury that other youths involved were never caught. Nick arrives to watch his father work, delighted by the "ejusdem generis" exchange, while Rumpole privately notes that his client "looks as if he ought to be in the fifth form."

Over lunch in the court cafeteria, Nick haltingly confesses a scrape of his own: he and friends, including a boy called Timson at school with him (a different Timson, used by the episode to point up the "like father, like son" theme), have been breaking into a derelict vicarage opposite the school for poker nights with smuggled cherry brandy, and possibly girls. Rumpole runs through the technical offences with lawyerly relish (forcible entry, burglary at common law, breaches of the gaming and lotteries and licensing acts) but is relieved to learn the housemaster, Bagnell, means to deal with it privately rather than call the police, and that Nick came clean before any letter arrived.

Back in court that afternoon, Rumpole cross-examines Peanuts Malloy at length, needling him about his sudden, uncharacteristic enthusiasm for an art class he had never attended before meeting Jim there, and suggesting, to the judge's continued bewilderment, that Peanuts was planted in the class as an "amateur witness" rather than a genuine painter. Walking Peanuts through what the two boys actually talked about, Rumpole draws out that they discussed the Rolling Stones, had been to a Stones concert together at the Hammersmith Odeon, and had in fact spent an entire evening together (the cafes, then "up west," then the concert itself), with Peanuts insisting they were "together the whole time." Rumpole asks the court, pointedly, to note the date of that concert against the date given in the indictment for the robbery, and the court adjourns for the day on that unresolved note.

That evening at the Rumpoles' house, Rumpole worries aloud to Hilda and Nick that he may be heading for a loss, reminiscing about past Bailey triumphs like the Penge Bungalow Murder. He and Nick fall into their old game of "Holmes and Watson," walking back through Peanuts's evidence line by line; it is Nick who first fully grasps the implication: if Peanuts and Jim were together at the concert the whole evening, Peanuts could not also have received a confession from Jim at the scene of the robbery, and the dates may simply not line up at all. Rumpole is delighted, telling Nick he has "the instinct... a nose for the evidence" and a brilliant career at the bar ahead of him. The next morning at chambers, Albert reports that Wystan has written from Horsham General Hospital confirming his retirement as head of chambers, and assumes, prematurely, that Rumpole, as senior man, will succeed him, with Nick one day joining a Rumpole-and-son set.

Back in Number Two Court, Mr. Bernard confirms the date Rumpole needs, and Rumpole uses it to finish off Peanuts: the Rolling Stones' only Hammersmith Odeon date that year falls squarely across the time of the alleged robbery, meaning Peanuts's own evidence places him and Jim together, nowhere near the butcher's shop, precisely when the Crown says Jim was confessing to committing it there. The prosecution's case collapses. Afterward in the cells, a jubilant-sounding Jim thanks Rumpole and prepares to leave, and it emerges that Jim actually knew about the concert alibi all along: he simply hadn't dared mention it, because "Dad wouldn't have liked it" so soon after the family had built him a rival, fabricated one to "keep it in the family." Rumpole is furious rather than pleased: he presses Jim about escaping a life mapped out for him as the Timsons' next "head of the family," but Jim, loyal to his mother and his role, cannot imagine leaving. Outside, Chief Inspector "Chalky" White, who led the original investigation, concedes the loss with weary fatalism ("he's a Timson, runs in the family, we'll get him sooner or later"), and Rumpole, subdued rather than triumphant, agrees he probably will.

That evening chambers celebrates not Rumpole's win but Guthrie Featherstone's elevation to silk and, more pointedly, his emergence as the consensus choice for the new head of chambers over Rumpole, "the good of chambers" being better served, in Wystan's and Featherstone's view, by someone who can attract profitable civil and tax work rather than an "old Bailey hack" trailing the Timsons through the waiting room. Daddy Wystan, Uncle Tom, George, and Featherstone's wife Marigold (who supplies a running joke about the suspender belts that let Guthrie's silk stockings stay up) toast the change while Rumpole absorbs the slight with brittle jokes, settling at last for "C.T. -- Counsel for the Timsons" instead of Q.C. The episode closes the next morning at home, as Nick, about to leave for school and already being steered toward PPE and, eventually, the law, listens to Rumpole erupt in genuine anger at the whole idea of "keeping it in the family": little Timsons bred into crime, little Everglades and Featherstones bred into the bench and the bar, all of them following fathers' footsteps whether they chose to or not. He sends Nick off to Victoria Station with "no more following in father's footsteps" as his parting word, ending the episode on that unresolved, unsentimental note rather than on any courtroom verdict shown onscreen.
