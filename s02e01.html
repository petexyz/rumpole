Rumpole of the Bailey

S02E01: Rumpole and the Man of God

A vicar is accused of shoplifting three shirts; the client refuses to testify on his own behalf.

29 May 1979 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 29 May 1979 (Series 2), directed by Brian Farnham, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).

In Rumpole's room in chambers, a new client waits with his instructing solicitor, Mr. Morse: the Reverend Mordred Skinner, an absent-minded clergyman accompanied by his forceful, overbearing sister, Miss Skinner. The charge is shoplifting -- three sports shirts lifted from the haberdashery counter of a department store -- and the Reverend's own account of events amounts to no account at all: he insists the whole thing is "a mystery," and that mystery is, professionally speaking, his business. Rumpole's clerk Henry shows them in while Rumpole, quoting Browning to himself in his room at the Temple, grumbles good-humoredly about clergymen as witnesses, who in his experience make the court believe a defendant's piety rather than his innocence and thereby add years to a sentence rather than subtracting them.

In the chambers corridor, Erskine Brown and Phillida Trant rib Rumpole about his unlikely new client -- "the man of God" come to seek earthly advice from a famously irreligious defender of villains -- and Guthrie Featherstone, the Head of Chambers, joins in, musing that a vicar is at least a cut above his usual rogues' gallery of property developers. Rumpole fences back that his own religion is "getting off as many villains as possible" and keeping the prisons empty. George Frobisher, Rumpole's oldest friend in chambers, catches him to confirm a dinner invitation that evening at the Rumpoles' -- he is bringing a lady friend, he says, with "a little news" he wants Horace and Hilda to be the first to hear.

Back with the Skinners, Rumpole presses the Reverend on the one question that matters -- how, exactly, three shirts came to be in his shopping basket -- and gets only "I prayed about it" and talk of loaves and fishes for his trouble. Miss Skinner, protective and bullying in equal measure, waves away the case as trivial and then, contradicting herself, insists her brother's reputation is never trivial; she overrides Rumpole's suggestion of a quick, quiet hearing before the magistrates and demands trial by jury instead, on the theory that no reasonable person could convict him. Rumpole agrees to take instructions no further than "a very good day to you," unconvinced but obliged. At home that evening, Hilda -- "She Who Must Be Obeyed" -- extracts a promise that Rumpole won't be late for George's dinner and reels off a shopping list; Miss Skinner, meanwhile, paints her brother as childlike and dependent, a man who has never managed anything without her, and promises to tell the jury exactly that.

The dinner party introduces George's intended: Mrs. Ida Tempest, a bustling, talkative widow he met complaining about lukewarm bathwater at the Royal Borough hotel where she had been a guest. Over Hilda's Charlotte russe, Ida chatters about her export-reject wine glasses from the Army & Navy Stores, her ballroom-dancing lessons with George at Miss Mackay's academy in Rutland Gate, and her plan, once married, to take up running a small hotel again -- she ran one before, with her first husband, somewhere in Kent. Rumpole, needling gently, asks whether that hotel might have been in Ramsgate; George, who has also just confided that he has applied for a circuit judgeship in some rural area, seems not to notice Rumpole's interest sharpen. Alone with George afterward, Rumpole mounts a comic, impassioned plea against both marriage and the judicial bench in one breath, casting domestic life and judgeship alike as a kind of guilty plea to an indefinite sentence without parole, and lamenting the end of their evenings together over Chateau Fleet Street at Pomeroy's wine bar. George, stung, says Rumpole has never really been someone he could share everything with.

That night, alone in chambers, Rumpole runs through the roll of his old cases for his own amusement -- the Penge Bungalow Murders, the Cheltenham Circus killing, and, pointedly, "the Ramsgate arson case: unexplained destruction of the Saracen's Head Hotel." At Pomeroy's the next evening, Erskine Brown delights in telling Rumpole that he, Erskine Brown, has just been briefed to prosecute the Reverend Skinner -- a case, he says, where Rumpole has no defense at all and will have to advise a guilty plea, "against his religion" or not. Phillida Trant, newly and happily engaged to Erskine Brown, teases both men. George arrives distracted and preoccupied, and begs off a proper drink with Rumpole, leaving Rumpole alone with a large glass of plonk and, by his own account, "nothing to celebrate."

The trial opens at the Inner London Sessions before Judge Bullingham -- "the Bull" -- whom Rumpole introduces to the jury, and to the audience, as a judge with an unreasoning prejudice against black defendants, defense counsel, and probation officers alike. Cross-examining the store detective, Mr. Batten, Rumpole methodically establishes that nobody actually saw Skinner take the shirts from the counter, that the two sales assistants were too absorbed in their own conversation to serve him, and that the first anyone noticed the shirts was well after Skinner had moved on to another department -- all while Bullingham interrupts, heckles, and accuses Rumpole of turning a serious case into a music hall act. Rumpole parries every interruption and, by the lunch adjournment, has built a textbook argument that the prosecution has simply failed to prove dishonest intent.

In the court corridor during the break, Rumpole corners the Reverend and finally gets the truth he suspected all along: it was not Skinner who took the shirts but his sister, a lifelong, compulsive petty thief -- "she would take things she couldn't possibly need," he says, boiled sweets and spectacles and bead handbags since childhood -- who slipped them into the basket she had been carrying while her brother's mind was on his sermon about the problem of evil. Skinner refuses point-blank to say any of this under oath, preferring conviction and professional ruin to exposing his sister; when Rumpole asks how he could lie to his own barrister, Skinner answers, gently, that he has never been in danger of mistaking Rumpole for Almighty God.

Back in court, with his client refusing the witness box, Rumpole delivers his closing speech on faith rather than facts: invoking the "golden thread" that runs through English law -- that the prosecution must prove guilt and the defense need prove nothing -- he argues that no jury should convict a man merely for failing to explain himself, and that this principle was bought at the cost of Magna Carta and the executioner's block for King Charles. To Rumpole's own open astonishment, Bullingham, cornered by the law he cannot openly flout, all but directs an acquittal over Erskine Brown's objections. Outside, a strangely subdued Skinner admits that a conviction might almost have been restful, sparing him the pretense of sanctity; Miss Skinner, oblivious to what has actually happened, declares the judge "extremely fair" and invites Rumpole to tea, which he declines on grounds of "pressure of work."

That night chambers throws George a party: he has indeed been made a circuit judge, assigned to Luton, to Uncle Tom's rambling delight and the room's mock toasts of "Judge George Frobisher." But Ida Tempest is nowhere to be seen -- she has left the Royal Borough without a forwarding address. George bitterly blames Rumpole, who, it emerges, was junior counsel years earlier for Ida's first husband in the Ramsgate arson case: the husband got seven years for burning down the Saracen's Head Hotel, and Ida herself served three years as an accessory. Once Ida recognized Rumpole at that dinner, she realized he was bound to tell George eventually -- and, rather than let him hear it secondhand, or let it cost George the judgeship a judge's wife with an arson conviction could hardly survive, she told George herself and then vanished. George tells Rumpole his only crime was simply "knowing" -- and that he'll find it hard to forgive him. At home afterward, Rumpole muses to Hilda, half-seriously, that he might have made a better vicar than a lawyer -- faith, not facts -- while Hilda, pleased that George's promotion means she'll see more of her husband, reminds him that if he hadn't joined her father C.H. Wystan's chambers all those years ago, he would never have met her at all. "That's true," Rumpole concedes. "Damn it, that's very true." The episode ends there, without any coda on Skinner's fate beyond the acquittal already given, or on whether George and Ida are truly finished.
