Rumpole of the Bailey

S03E03: Rumpole and the Old Boy Net

A couple is accused of blackmailing a senior civil servant who was secretly an investor in their brothel; Rumpole loses the Head of Chambers post to Sam Ballard.

Tuesday, 25 October 1983 · ITV (Thames Television)

A short burst of nonsense ("Da, da, da, da...") during the chambers-meeting scene reads as a musical sting rather than dialogue, and is omitted below. Originally broadcast 25 October 1983 (Series 3), directed by Tony Smith, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).

The episode opens with a police raid on a house in Barnardine Square, Victoria, late at night: officers arrest the house's proprietors, Napier Lee and his wife Lorraine, who treat the whole business with unruffled, upper-crust unconcern ("can't we be just a little bit civilized... have your young men a nice cup of tea"), while a third figure present, identified only as Mr X, refuses to give his name, citing "national security." Rumpole, newly back from his harrowing Naranga murder trial, returns to chambers to find Henry still being blamed for the cable that nearly got him executed, a letter from the Lawyers As Churchgoers society (LAC) inviting his "contribution," and Claude Erskine Brown smugly announcing that he has applied for silk -- timed, Rumpole notes dryly, to fall conveniently after the upcoming vote for the now-vacant headship of chambers, left empty since Guthrie Featherstone's elevation to the bench.

In Featherstone's old room, Rumpole meets its new occupant, Fiona Allways, a young pupil to Phillida Erskine Brown (the former Phillida Trant) who is anxious about her prospects as a woman at the Bar and resigned to ending up doing conveyancing at Arundel's if chambers won't have her. Rumpole, unbothered by her sex, puts her straight to work taking a note on his new brief. At home, Hilda is deep in preparations for what she clearly already regards as her own coronation: recruiting Dodo Macintosh's cheese-and-onion canapés and little sausage arrangements for the chambers party that will mark Rumpole's expected elevation to head of chambers, musing about Marigold Featherstone's unsuitable outfits in the old regime and clearly relishing the idea of being "the head lady."

Rumpole's new brief, from the solicitor Bernard, is for Napier and Lorraine Lee, who run -- by their own prim description -- a "friendly house," and who face not merely a charge of keeping a disorderly house but the far graver charge of blackmail, demanding money with menaces from a highly placed client. The Lees are obsessed with class and discretion: Napier, a Lawnhurst old boy fallen on hard times after a bad patch in the City and a dicky heart from the war, treats his clientele's confidentiality as a sacred schoolboy code -- "one does not sneak" -- even though honoring it is actively hurting his own defense, since he and Lorraine refuse to name the "old friend," plainly another Lawnhurst man, who originally staked them the capital to set up the house. Rumpole is baffled and faintly disgusted by their snobbish loyalty to a man who is, in effect, accusing them of a crime that could put them away for five years.

At the Old Bailey, prosecuting counsel turns out to be Sam Ballard QC, the pious, newly encountered founder-in-spirit of the Lawyers As Churchgoers, who opens with a sermonizing speech about "decent men, family men" tempted and fleeced by the house of ill repute, and who successfully applies to Judge Bullingham -- a short-tempered, impatient fixture of the court -- to have the blackmail victim's name withheld from press and public as "Mr X." Rumpole objects furiously: his own clients have already been splashed across every front page as a "top people's madam" and her husband, so why should the alleged victim alone enjoy the law's protective anonymity while presumption of innocence runs only one way? Bullingham overrules him, reasoning that unmasking Mr X would hand blackmailers exactly the leverage the law exists to prevent. Fiona, impressed, hails it as a fight for press freedom and introduces Rumpole to her own hero, a television reporter named Isabel Vincent of Home Counties News, keenly interested in women's liberation.

In the cells, the Lees confirm to Rumpole that they do, in fact, know Mr X's real name -- he signed his deposition -- but refuse point-blank to use it, insisting it would be "against the code" even as they face conviction. Back in the robing room, Ballard tries to recruit Rumpole to LAC with talk of Christian forgiveness; Rumpole counters slyly that if Ballard really believes two sinners repenting is cause for heavenly joy, he might show it by dropping the five-years-carrying blackmail charge in exchange for guilty pleas to the mere six-months disorderly-house offense. Ballard, unmoved by his own rhetoric when it costs him a conviction, refuses, prompting a volley of dueling scripture and Blake quotations between the two men.

Cross-examining Mr X, Rumpole patiently walks him into contradictions: Mr X claims he was introduced to the house by "an old friend" he'd "known nearly all his life" but met again, by chance, in a Victoria pub about five years ago -- a description that, Rumpole observes aloud, fits Napier Lee himself (a guess he admits afterward was a flagrant breach of the first rule of cross-examination, never ask a question you don't know the answer to, which happens to pay off anyway). Pressed further, Mr X all but concedes he was an investor who put capital into the Lees' "business," though he exercises his right not to answer the self-incriminating question directly. Rumpole also draws out a telling detail: unlike his usual cash visits, Mr X paid the two supposedly extorted gas bills by traceable cheque -- oddly deliberate behavior for a man who otherwise prized concealment, and suspicious evidence of someone manufacturing a paper trail rather than genuinely yielding to menace.

That evening, the case blows wide open outside the courtroom: Isabel Vincent's Home Counties News broadcast names Mr X on air as Sir Cuthbert Pericles, a senior Foreign Office civil servant, in flagrant contempt of Judge Bullingham's order. Bullingham, apoplectic the next morning, vows to hunt down and punish whoever leaked the information and to refer the matter to the Director of Public Prosecutions, while Rumpole privately muses that the "harm" cuts both ways. Seizing the opening, Rumpole calls a surprise defense witness, Steven Lucas of the Foreign Office Legal Department, who testifies that Sir Cuthbert -- whom he knows well, and who was not, notably, a Lawnhurst man -- had quizzed him over lunch a year earlier about the newly passed Contempt of Court Act's provision letting blackmail witnesses' names be kept secret indefinitely, musing aloud that anyone wanting to keep a scandal quiet need only accuse someone of blackmail. Lucas says he only remembered the conversation once he heard Sir Cuthbert's name on the television news the previous night and felt it his public duty to come forward.

In his closing speech, Rumpole lays out the devastating implication: Sir Cuthbert, trapped by his own addiction to the house at 66 Barnardine Square and terrified of exposure, devised an elaborate, expensive, and ultimately futile insurance scheme -- manufacturing a paper trail of cheques to support a trumped-up blackmail accusation that would buy himself permanent, court-ordered anonymity. It was futile, Rumpole argues, precisely because the Lees would never actually have betrayed him: unlike their supposed victim, for whom it was all cynical self-protection, for the Lees discretion really was, as they kept insisting, a matter of honor and the code. The jury evidently agrees enough to acquit, or substantially undercut, the blackmail charge; in the cells afterward the Lees cheerfully accept six months each for simply keeping a disorderly house, worrying more about being separated in a sex-segregated prison system (and about whether "Custard" -- their old nickname for Sir Cuthbert from his schooldays -- will think they "sneaked" on him, which they insist they never did) than about the sentence itself, while celebrating a tidy sale of the house's freehold and goodwill.

Rumpole counsels a chastened Fiona Allways, and by extension the publicly exposed Isabel Vincent, on the importance of working inside rules even while fighting and resenting them -- and quietly takes on Vincent's looming contempt-of-court defense himself rather than let a promising young reporter become a martyr. Back in chambers, the long-deferred question of the headship resolves itself in an anticlimax: a letter arrives from the newly elevated Mr Justice Featherstone, nominating his own old schoolfellow Sam Ballard -- not Rumpole, and not the silk-seeking Erskine Brown -- as the obvious choice to succeed him as head of chambers, on the grounds of Ballard's piety and freedom from any taint of the recent contempt-of-court scandal. Despite some muttered support for Rumpole's seniority from Uncle Tom, the vote goes comfortably to Ballard. At the chambers party that follows -- catered down to the last cheesy bit by Dodo Macintosh, and attended by a Hilda who has spent the whole episode treating it as her own coronation -- Uncle Tom's toast to the new head of chambers lands as a surprise to her: "You never told me," she says. Rumpole's reply is quiet and uncharacteristically unguarded: "No, Hilda, I've funked it" -- he had let his own name go forward after all, and lost, and simply hadn't told her rather than face this moment. Hilda calls it "what a failure," and Rumpole, deflecting back into form, proposes they drink the failure anyway, since champagne tastes just as good either way; the two toast the defeat with the same bottle they'd meant for a victory.
