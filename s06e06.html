Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 6, Episode 6

Rumpole for the Prosecution

Rumpole prosecutes for the first time, in a private complaint from the rich father of a murdered girl.

Monday, 2 December 1991 · ITV (Thames Television)

The episode opens with estate agent Christopher Jago being questioned at a police station about the death of Veronica Fabian, found battered to death at 13A Gissing Mews. Jago's account is that he phoned Fabian Winchelsey, the estate agency where Veronica worked for her father, to arrange an 8:30am viewing, arrived to find the front door open and Veronica already dying on the floor, panicked without calling an ambulance or the police, and fled to the airport and on to Spain on existing timeshare business before voluntarily returning. Detective Inspector Slogat makes clear the police are not charging him for lack of evidence, but will want him as a witness, a decision that leaves Veronica's father and brother, Mr Fabian and Roger Fabian, convinced Jago has bought his way out of trouble, and they come to Rumpole's chambers determined to mount a private prosecution for murder. Rumpole is wary of private prosecutions on principle but is intrigued by one detail: Jago's police statement never explains an "Arthur Morrison," a name that recurs several times, unexplained, in Veronica's own appointments diary.

Two unrelated chambers irritations bracket this new brief. Sam Ballard, newly obsessed with the idea that "the power of evil is everywhere," including Equity Court, enlists Rumpole to investigate Claude Erskine Brown over a civil brief originally sent to Dave Inchcape by the solicitor Patricia Benbow, on which Inchcape's name has suspiciously been scratched out and Erskine Brown's substituted: "brief-nicking," in Ballard's eyes, serious enough to demand a formal chambers prosecution, though Rumpole protests that prosecuting is not his line of work at all. Meanwhile, at home, Hilda has swapped her usual Christmas guest, her old school friend Dodo McIntosh, for another old schoolmate, the domineering and widowed Charmian Nichols, who descends on the Rumpole household issuing opinions about dishwashers (a "Crocker Gleam," in her relentless phrasing) and making pointed comparisons between her own wealth and the comparatively threadbare proceeds of Rumpole's legal-aid practice.

Stung by Charmian's needling about money and modern appliances, Rumpole accepts the Fabians' private prosecution after all, drawn by its unusually generous fee (two thousand pounds plus five-hundred-pound daily refreshers) with the specific, half-joking aim of buying Hilda a dishwasher for Christmas. He visits the murder scene at the mews with the instructing solicitor, Mr Pycroft, and calls on the Fabians at home, where Mr and Mrs Fabian reveal that Veronica, a would-be novelist with a passion for nineteenth-century fiction, especially the Brontës, was not in fact their biological daughter but an adopted child they had always felt needed special protection, a detail that gains weight only much later. They describe Christopher Jago as a flashy rival estate agent suspected, without proof, of a scheme to undervalue clients' houses for sale to secret nominee buyers who resell at the real price and split the profit, but insist Veronica had no connection to him and no plausible reason to have been his victim.

Back in chambers, young Liz Probert is furious to learn Rumpole has taken a brief for the prosecution on behalf of a wealthy, well-connected family against a man the police have already declined to charge, accusing him of betraying everything he claims to stand for. Her fury turns out to be well informed: she and her partner Dave Inchcape bought their own flat through Jago, who she insists was "absolutely honest, reliable and trustworthy" throughout, got them a fair price against other agents' inflated asking prices, and arranged their mortgage without complaint, a personal testimonial that undercuts the Fabians' portrait of Jago as a predatory cowboy. In an ironic twist, Jago ends up being defended by Liz herself, briefed by Trisha Benbow and led, to everyone's amusement, by none other than Sam Ballard QC, setting up the spectacle of "Rumpole for the prosecution and Ballard for the defence" in the same courtroom.

On Christmas Day itself, flicking through a companion to English literature while needled yet again by Charmian, Rumpole stumbles on the answer to the diary mystery: Arthur Morrison was a real, prolific late-Victorian novelist, born 1863, best known for a novel about the deprived East End of London called A Child of the Jago, confirming Rumpole's hunch that the literary-minded Veronica had used "Morrison" as a private code name for meetings with Jago that she did not want her disapproving family to know about. Pressing his private investigator, the perpetually sniffling Fig Newton, for background on Jago's personal life, Rumpole learns that Jago was a regular at the Warrington Hotel pub near the Regent's Canal, known for a string of blonde girlfriends, but also for meeting, far more frequently than any of them, a notably unglamorous woman the bar staff eventually identify from a photograph as Veronica Fabian herself, proof that the two had known each other well despite Jago's claim to the police of a single, businesslike appointment.

At trial before Mr Justice Ollivant (the same bluntly common-sense judge who presided over Rumpole's Gunster case), Rumpole's prosecution keeps slipping, almost helplessly, into defence-style cross-examination: he draws out from a forensic officer that there are no fingerprints of any kind, let alone Jago's, on the African knobkerrie used as the murder weapon, and gets Detective Inspector Slogat to confirm that Jago had always been the prime suspect behind the "Morrison" alias, that he volunteered the crucial fact of having found the body entirely of his own accord, and that this honesty was itself one of the reasons the police chose not to charge him, all of it, despite Rumpole's nominal role, working steadily in the defendant's favour while Ballard, ostensibly leading the defence, sits by uselessly declining to ask any questions at all.

Called to give evidence, Jago finally explains what Veronica actually told him during their secret meetings at the Warrington: that she had discovered her father's firm was systematically undervaluing clients' houses to sell to secret nominees who resold at the proper price and split the profits, that she was frightened by what she'd found and had nobody else to confide in, and that she had sought Jago's advice precisely because he worked in the same business and would understand the scheme. Roger Fabian erupts from the public gallery, accusing Jago of trying to destroy the family because "we know he killed Veronica," an outburst the judge has to restrain, but one that strikes Rumpole as oddly revealing in itself. Rumpole requests and is granted an unusual adjournment to the scene of the crime, where a physical demonstration involving Jago and the murder weapon makes clear that the mews room's low ceiling would have prevented a man of Jago's height from swinging the weapon as the prosecution's own case required, a demonstration that, in the finest Rumpole tradition, gets his own client acquitted outright.

After the not-guilty verdict, Rumpole privately confronts Mr Fabian with the conclusion his own investigation has actually reached: that it was not Veronica but Roger (the Fabians' own son, and the one who kept accusing others of exactly the misdeeds he himself had committed) who was running the undervaluing racket, that Veronica had learned of it and gone to Jago rather than her own family for advice, and that Roger had followed her to the mews house, quarrelled with her, and swung the knobkerrie off the wall in a rage that turned fatal, in a room whose low ceiling was never going to constrain a man of his own, shorter build the way it constrained Jago. Rumpole realises, to his own horror, that Mr Fabian had engaged him from the start not for justice but to help secure a conviction of an innocent man and thereby protect his real son forever: "the one child who needed special protection" was never adopted Veronica but Roger all along. Rumpole declares he has been made an unwitting party to a conspiracy to pervert the course of justice, refuses the fee, and is met with a final, chilling confirmation when Fabian's only regret is voiced as "poor Roger," not "poor Veronica."

The episode closes with its lighter subplots resolving in quick succession. At a celebratory drink with the defence team at the Warrington Hotel, Rumpole fields a call from Henry, fending off an inconveniently timed visit from Marguerite Ballard, who has turned up looking for her husband at what both men privately understand to be a far less wholesome gathering than the "Lawyers' Christian Society" meeting Ballard claimed to be attending. Rumpole also resolves the chambers mystery of the altered brief: it was Dave Inchcape himself who scratched out his own name and substituted Erskine Brown's, having balked privately at being briefed to evict a lesbian couple from their home and unwilling to admit as much to Liz; Liz goes off at once to clear Claude's name. At home the next morning, Rumpole learns that the expensive dishwasher has been returned, not for any mechanical fault, but because Hilda discovered, via a forwarded letter to her old friend Dodo, that the supposedly "charming" Charmian Nichols had written that Christmas with the Rumpoles was "about as exciting as watching fingernails grow." Chastened by the whole private-prosecution misadventure, Rumpole vows that from now on he will only ever defend, and Hilda, with weary affection, agrees that whenever he tries to do what he thinks is right, the results tend to be disastrous.
