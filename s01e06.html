Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 1, Episode 6

Rumpole and the Heavy Brigade

Rumpole defends a stuttering petty crook on a murder charge.

It is a dull November, and Rumpole, alone in the Temple, recites gloomy verse to himself about a season with "no warmth, no cheerfulness, no health, no ease" and reflects that after months of unsolicited-grope cases at the Uxbridge magistrates' court, he is back where he started forty years ago. In chambers, his clerk Henry brings welcome news -- a new instruction from the solicitor Mr. Nooks concerning a Bermondsey stabbing that has been in the papers -- and Rumpole brightens at the prospect of "a murder," assuming he will be led by Guthrie Featherstone, QC, MP, the head of chambers. Henry, however, has graver business: Rumpole's disreputable old hat has become the standing joke of the Temple's clerks, unfit, in Henry's view, to be seen going down to the Bailey "on a top-class murder." Rumpole refuses to be shamed out of a hat that served faithfully through his glory days, invoking the Penge Bungalow Murders and reminding Henry that his former clerk Albert never once complained of it.

Nooks arrives with the client's brothers, Basil and Leslie Delgado, to brief Rumpole on their brother Peter -- "P.T." -- who has been moved to the prison hospital charged with the murder of one Tosher McBride, described vaguely as a "rent collector," stabbed outside a Bermondsey pub. The evidence is grim: bloodstains on Peter's sleeve consistent with McBride's blood group, and the sheath knife itself recovered from Peter's old Daimler. Nooks reveals that Featherstone has in fact declined to lead after all, reasoning (relayed via Nooks) that a silk MP appearing might make the case look more important than it is and "overegg the pudding" for the jury; Rumpole, delighted at the thought of running his first murder alone in years, is less delighted moments later to learn his trial judge will be the fussy, hat-fixated Mr. Justice Prescold.

At the prison hospital, the defense-instructed psychiatrist Dr. Bleen examines Peter, who presents as thoroughly disoriented and amnesiac about the stabbing, and who Nooks hopes can support a plea of guilty but insane. Bleen mentions, with no apparent sense of impropriety, that he has already "had a word" over a game of squash with the Home Office's own psychiatrist, Dr. Stotter -- a minor celebrity who hosts a Thursday radio phone-in on listeners' neuroses -- about how the case ought to go. Rumpole is unimpressed by the cosy arrangement and unwilling to settle for an insanity plea on faith; left alone with Peter, who speaks with a severe stutter, he presses him directly on whether he actually used the knife. Peter insists McBride was "already cut" when he found him and that he only knelt to hold him -- enough for Rumpole to decide, over the family's unease, that the case must be fought honestly rather than compromised away.

Guthrie Featherstone makes repeated overtures toward chambers harmony: an offer of unofficial cross-examination tips Rumpole brushes off, and an invitation -- via his wife Marigold -- to the Scales of Justice Ball at the Savoy, which Rumpole finds absurd given that Marigold is privately known to her own husband as "She Who Must Be Obeyed." Featherstone also passes on, with visible embarrassment, Mr. Justice Prescold's complaint over lunch at his Inn that Rumpole's hat sets "the worst possible example to the younger men at the bar," which Rumpole receives as fresh proof of the judiciary's priorities. That evening at home, Hilda presses her own case for the ball, dismisses Rumpole's claim that he cannot dance by recalling the night they met and waltzed together at an Inns of Court Ball on 14 August 1938 -- when her father, then head of Rumpole's chambers, brought her along and praised Rumpole's usefulness on blood groups -- and goes ahead and accepts the invitation on his behalf regardless of his protests.

Working out his defense at the kitchen sink while doing the washing-up, Rumpole reasons aloud to Hilda about bloodstain patterns: a man stabbed repeatedly would spray blood widely over his attacker's clothing and pockets, not leave one single soaked patch in the crook of an arm, as if merely cradling a dying man. Hilda, meanwhile, notices he has quietly bought a new bowler and credits it, half-seriously, for his sudden run of luck -- a theory Rumpole rejects, insisting his expertise in blood, not his headwear, is what matters.

At the Old Bailey, Rumpole and Nooks turn up a potential weapon against the chief eyewitness, Bernie "Four-Eyes" Welpton -- a prior perjury conviction for forging a passport application, discovered to date from his East End minicab days. Welpton has testified that Peter had threatened to "cut" McBride after McBride mocked Peter's stutter in front of a girl at the Paradise Rooms club, and that Peter was seen cradling the dying McBride's head rather than tending him like someone with any aptitude for first aid. Rumpole, to the fury of Leslie Delgado and the boy's mother, deliberately declines to use Welpton's conviction in open court, explaining afterward that doing so would open the door to Peter's own record -- a 1970 conviction for unlawful wounding -- being put before the jury instead; some tactical victories, he insists, are won by what you choose not to say.

Cross-examining the investigating Inspector and the forensic officer Mr. Thistleton, Rumpole builds his real defense: the Daimler was found unlocked, so the murder weapon could as easily have been tossed through its open window or door by the true culprit fleeing the scene as placed there by Peter; and Thistleton is forced to concede that the single bloodstain found -- in the crook of Peter's arm, with none at all in his pockets or cuffs -- is "not inconsistent," which Rumpole translates pointedly for the jury as meaning exactly consistent with Peter's own account. Pressing the Inspector further, Rumpole establishes that McBride's "rent collecting" was in fact protection-racket collection from cafes, pubs, and minicab offices that had an unfortunate tendency to catch fire when payments lapsed -- planting, under Mr. Justice Prescold's visible irritation, a motive that has nothing to do with Peter at all.

In his closing speech, Rumpole conjures an absent figure he calls "Mr. X" -- the unseen racketeer, perhaps sunning himself on the Costa Brava, who employed McBride as a collector, discovered his treachery, and stabbed him three times in the neck before melting away, content to let Peter Delgado hang for it. Despite Mr. Justice Prescold's summing-up warning the jury that the prosecution's case is "almost overwhelming," the jury acquits Peter of murder. In the cells afterward, a strangely despondent Peter -- rather than celebrating -- stammers out to Rumpole that his own brothers chose him as defense counsel precisely because his mocked reputation (the hat, the Bailey gossip about an old barrister who is "sure to lose") made him look like a man who was bound to fail: Peter was meant to be convicted, not acquitted, and Rumpole's unexpected brilliance has thrown someone else's plan into disarray.

That night at the Savoy, Hilda surprises the Featherstones by championing Rumpole's unglamorous gift for bloodstains rather than his wardrobe, while Marigold turns visibly pale at talk of blood and Guthrie presses Rumpole on how he can be so certain of a client's innocence. Rumpole answers with a speech about the law's "mumbo-jumbo" -- wigs, gowns, ritual Latin phrases -- somehow still managing, against all the pageantry, to produce the plain truth at the end of it. The evening's real revelation arrives with Nooks: the ten o'clock news has reported that Leslie Delgado has been arrested for Tosher McBride's murder, with new witnesses now saying they heard Leslie setting the killing up and that McBride had been working for Leslie all along -- confirming that Rumpole's invented "Mr. X" was real, and that the Delgado brothers' scheme to let Peter take the fall has spectacularly backfired. Rumpole, energized, instructs Nooks to start gathering evidence for the new case -- then, astonishing Hilda, declares that he wants, after all, to dance, and leads her onto the floor as the series closes.
