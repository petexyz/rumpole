Rumpole of the Bailey

S07E04: Rumpole and the Reform of Joby Jonson

A juvenile delinquent is accused of assaulting an elderly woman; Hilda is worried when a burglar tries to steal evidence from Rumpole's briefcase.

Late one night, Hilda wakes Rumpole convinced she can hear an intruder in their flat; Rumpole, half asleep and quoting Shakespeare at her, dismisses it until morning reveals that a burglar really did get in -- and, strangely, took nothing of value except the proof of evidence and defence statement for Rumpole's current client, a young man named Joby Jonson, lifted straight out of his briefcase. Hilda insists on calling the police back to take fingerprints, over Rumpole's amusement, and later has a full alarm system installed. In a lighter parallel thread, Claude Erskine-Brown is once again anxiously awaiting the Lord Chancellor's annual list of new QCs -- his fifth application -- while his wife Phillida, herself already a QC and much in demand on big cases abroad, teases him about his nerves without revealing that she has news of her own.

Rumpole visits Jonson in a remand prison, where he is already being bullied by fellow inmates as a "granny basher," and finds him defensive and uncooperative. The charge is brutal: an elderly widow, Mrs Parsons of Pondicherry Road near Euston Station, was visited twice on the same day by a young man -- first a brief, odd call asking "are you still living here, Mrs Parsons?", then, hours later, a masked, balaclava-wearing attacker who forced his way in, beat her, tied her up, and stole her Post Office savings book containing a mere five pounds seventy-nine. Mrs Parsons later identified Jonson at an identification parade. Jonson's proposed alibi -- that he was at the time taking ecstasy and dancing with some girls from Manchester outside the Superloo at Euston -- conveniently ignores the one piece of evidence Rumpole considers fatal: Jonson's own palm print was found on Mrs Parsons' front door. Jonson insists he will not "put his hands up," and hints, without explaining, that there are people he is too frightened to name.

Back in chambers, Sam Ballard reveals to a delighted-then-horrified Erskine-Brown that he has already given his "frank views" on Erskine-Brown's silk application to a contact at the Lord Chancellor's office, citing the sexual-harassment complaint lodged against him by typist Dot Clapton as evidence of a want of "gravitas" and "bottom." An enraged Erskine-Brown calls Ballard "a pompous prick" loudly enough to be overheard by solicitors in a nearby pub, to his wife Phillida's amusement. Ballard, undeterred, turns his moral attention to Dot's nose stud, which he and a chambers meeting -- egged on by a newly feminist-skeptic Phillida and a baffled Rumpole -- debate as an unacceptable "statement of female submission"; Ballard deputizes Henry to have a word with Dot about it, but Henry flatly refuses, leaving Ballard to handle it himself.

Rumpole is summoned to the Home Office by Tom Motram MP, an undersecretary with responsibility for prisons and Jonson's own constituency MP, who has been pressured by Jonson's distressed mother to ensure the boy is "properly defended" -- code, it becomes clear, for making sure Rumpole does not run some "silly defence." Motram praises his former business partner, Sir Sebastian Pilgrim, a celebrated former England cricketer who now runs the Youth Enterprise and Reform Trust (YERT), a charity that tries to reform delinquent boys through cricket, discipline, and group "confession" sessions. Rumpole, intrigued despite himself, visits YERT's clubhouse, where Pilgrim and his driver and chief counselor Fred Brie -- himself a reformed armed robber -- explain their philosophy that admitting one's crimes, even just to oneself, is the first step to rehabilitation; Pilgrim, troubled that Jonson has been a repeat disappointment to the program, offers to appear as a character witness at trial.

A separate thread quietly converges on the same ground: Dot Clapton confides in Rumpole that her estranged father, Peter Clapton, lives nearby on McGlinty Terrace behind Euston Station and is being relentlessly pressured -- repeated letters, doorbell visits at all hours -- to sell his house to make way for a new development, and refuses. Rumpole, filing this away, later learns from Hilda's reading of a newspaper profile that Sir Sebastian Pilgrim is also chairman of Maiden Over Holdings, a company planning precisely such a hotel-and-shopping-centre development behind Euston Station, once in partnership with Tom Motram before Motram entered government. The geography -- Mrs Parsons' house, Dot's father's house, and Maiden Over Holdings' project -- all line up.

Meanwhile, in a long-running chambers subplot, Phillida begins cultivating Ballard with lunch invitations -- first to a health-food bar he suggests, then, at her insistence, the Savoy Grill -- flattering him shamelessly ("I can't stop thinking about you... in Hong Kong") until he agrees to ring his Lord Chancellor's office contact back and reverse his negative report on Erskine-Brown, now crediting him with ample "gravitas" and "bottom" after all. Phillida's real motive, carefully withheld from both Ballard and her own husband, is a secret she is not yet ready to share.

At trial before the notoriously humorless Mr Justice Graves -- who boasts of his perfect courtroom acoustics whenever Rumpole mutters an aside -- the prosecution is led, to Rumpole's wry amusement, by none other than his own colleague Phillida Erskine-Brown QC. Mrs Parsons testifies to both visits and identifies Jonson from the morning call, though she concedes the afternoon attacker's face was partly hidden by a balaclava; Rumpole gets her to admit, over Phillida's objection and the judge's agreement that it is mere "speculation," that the household had been under sustained pressure to sell up before any of this happened. Cross-examining Jonson himself, Rumpole extracts an admission that he did pay the harmless morning visit -- accounting for the palm print -- but was elsewhere, drugged and dancing, during the actual robbery, and that the group behind it burgled Rumpole's own flat specifically to find out what his defence would be, out of fear Jonson might tell the truth about who really did it. Jonson, frightened, still won't name names.

The courtroom confrontation comes to a head when Rumpole calls Sir Sebastian Pilgrim himself as a defence witness and turns to cross-examining him instead: establishing that Pilgrim's company, Maiden Over Holdings, employed estate agents Jeba and Jonas to pressure both Mrs Parsons and Dot's father, Mr Clapton, into selling their homes for the same Euston-area development, and suggesting that Pilgrim used "reformed" YERT boys like Jonson as intimidating errand-runners for the easy, non-violent warnings while someone more trusted handled the real violence. Mr Justice Graves shuts this line of questioning down forcefully, calling it an outrageous attempt to drag "this most distinguished gentleman" into Jonson's crime and declaring it is simply "not the way the game is played" in his court. The jury convicts; Graves sentences Jonson to five years, condemning him as "unreformable" for having let his counsel attack a man who had tried to help him.

Late that same night, Detective Sergeant Appleby telephones the Rumpoles' house with news that the fingerprint found during the flat burglary has finally been matched -- to Fred Brie, Pilgrim's driver and YERT's resident reformed ex-convict, previously convicted of robbery with violence. It is confirmation, too late to help Jonson, that Rumpole's theory had been correct all along: Pilgrim's organization really had broken in to find out what the defence would say. Hilda, vindicated in her insistence on chasing up the fingerprints despite Rumpole's teasing, gets the last word about "how important it is to have a woman on the case." In chambers, the Erskine-Brown subplot resolves with a final irony: Claude, still celebrating Ballard's reversal on his silk application, discovers from the newspaper's front page -- rather than from his own wife -- that it is Phillida, not he, who has just been made a QC; her cryptic remark to Ballard about "leaving the bar" turns out to have meant leaving the junior bar for silk, not leaving him for romance, and the episode closes with Claude, still not a QC himself, finding that Phillida will now be the one leading him in court.

The defendant's surname is spelled "Johnson" throughout the dialogue transcript even though the broadcast's own title (per Wikipedia) spells it "Jonson"; this synopsis follows Wikipedia's title spelling but otherwise treats the two as the same character. Originally broadcast 19 November 1992 (Series 7), directed by Martyn Friend, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).
