Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 6, Episode 1

Rumpole à la Carte

Rumpole defends an elitist restaurateur he dislikes, after a live mouse jumps out of a gourmet meal.

Aired Monday, 28 October 1991 · ITV (Thames Television)

The episode opens at La Maison Jean-Pierre, a self-importantly gourmet restaurant where Horace Rumpole is dining with his wife Hilda and her visiting cousin Everard, a wealthy, humourless businessman up from Saskatoon. Everard holds forth on a lucrative stock-market coup involving a Winnipeg soap company while Hilda needles Rumpole for never having made comparable money as a mere "Old Bailey hack." When the waiter George recites a menu of wafer-thin duck breast in celery remoulade, Rumpole loudly demands mashed potatoes, a request the restaurant's proprietor, the self-styled culinary genius Jean-Pierre O'Higgins, refuses with theatrical contempt. The two men trade insults (Rumpole needling him about "pleasuring" the customer as the menu promises, O'Higgins boasting of having thrown out an Arabian king and reduced film stars to tears) until, at a nearby table, barrister Claude Erskine Brown and his dinner companion, solicitor Trisha Benbow, lift the cover off their main course to find a live mouse running loose on the plate. Benbow leaps onto a chair screaming, to the ruin of the evening and, as becomes clear, of O'Higgins's reputation.

The next morning at the Rumpoles' house, the mouse incident becomes the occasion for a real rupture. Hilda accuses Rumpole of needing to "be a character" in public at every opportunity (the cigars, the steak and kidney pudding, the arguments at full volume) and declares she is tired of it. She announces she is going off with Cousin Everard on a tour of England's gracious-living hotels and three-rosette restaurants; Everard, she explains, lost his wife years before and does not need to argue with anyone. Rumpole, startled, presses her on whether she is serious, but Hilda is already gone by the time he leaves for chambers, and the episode spends its first act with Rumpole uncharacteristically alone.

At chambers, two parallel bits of business are underway. Head of chambers Sam Ballard QC is in a lather over a trivial theft: someone has stolen the nail brush from the chambers lavatory, which he treats, in his usual self-important way, as a matter of real moral seriousness, eventually vowing to chain up its replacement. Meanwhile Erskine Brown, mortified that his dinner with Trisha Benbow is about to become public knowledge through the restaurant prosecution, lies to his wife Phillida (away "doing a spot of town planning" in Cardiff) on the telephone about having stayed in and cooked himself an omelette, and begs Ballard to keep his name out of the case. Ballard instead informs him, with some relish, that as a material witness to the mouse he will have to give evidence for the prosecution and submit to cross-examination by Rumpole.

The solicitor Mr Pinhorn then brings Jean-Pierre O'Higgins himself to Rumpole's room, explaining that O'Higgins is being prosecuted under the Food and Hygiene Regulations for the state of his kitchen and the mouse incident, and wants the man who humiliated him over mashed potatoes to defend him, reasoning that anyone with the nerve to do that cannot be intimidated by a judge. Rumpole tells O'Higgins frankly that he finds him pretentious, self-satisfied and a bully, but agrees to take the brief regardless, on the old principle that a barrister is available to even the most unattractive client. In the course of the conference it also emerges that O'Higgins's ex-wife, Simone, divorced him under French community-property law and is entitled to half the restaurant, a detail that will matter later.

Rumpole visits the restaurant's kitchen to interview the staff: O'Higgins describes his waiters, the commis waiter Alberto Pasquale and the long-serving station waiter Gaston Leblanc, who has a sick wife and a son at university and works every spare hour. Gaston's account is that he found only two plates under the cloches that night, with no mouse under either, and handed one to Alberto to carry out, meaning the mouse, if it was a trick rather than an accident, must have been introduced after the food left the kitchen. Back in chambers, cleaner Mrs Rafferty turns up the long-missing lavatory nail brush, which had simply been lying on the toilet floor; Rumpole, musing to her and to young barrister Liz Probert about whether he himself has become "a character" in chambers folklore (recalling a predecessor, old "Rumpole," who was eventually certified insane), quietly pockets the brush with a view to a mischievous use of his own.

Liz Probert, newly active in a self-styled group of Young Radical Lawyers with colleague Dave Inchcape, notices Rumpole breakfasting alone at a café and learns that Hilda has left him for her cousin. Touched, and amused by the idea of doing some improvised "social work," Liz and the others organise a surprise party at Rumpole's house to cheer him up, interrupted partway through by a telephone call Rumpole has to take. Separately, Rumpole engages private investigator Fig Newton, a chambers regular ("not so dusty"), to find out what the waiters Gaston Leblanc and Alberto Pasquale get up to in their spare time, suspecting the mouse was no accident. That evening Rumpole, dining alone again since Hilda's departure, calls in on O'Higgins at the restaurant, who feeds him a private supper and gossips about the restaurant's cashier, young Mary Skelton, over whom two of the male staff have apparently been fighting, and about the bitterness of his divorce from the business-minded Simone, who insisted on a French wedding and now stands to gain half of everything.

The trial opens before the chronically humourless Judge Graves, prosecuted by Sam Ballard, who catalogues the restaurant's violations: cracked tiles, open waste bins, no nail brush in the kitchen handbasin, and finally the live mouse served to a customer, and argues that as proprietor, O'Higgins is answerable regardless of who is actually to blame. Erskine Brown is called as the prosecution's star witness and is excruciatingly reluctant to admit, under Ballard's and then the judge's prompting, that his dinner companion was Patricia Benbow; Rumpole's cross-examination, far from damaging him, instead extracts (to Erskine Brown's evident relief) that he was dining with Benbow purely to discuss a forthcoming brief, the Ballum Minicab murder, on which Benbow intended to instruct his wife Phillida QC. The point scored, and Erskine Brown's marriage apparently protected, the judge presses Rumpole to "come to the mouse," and the morning session ends without the key question of who planted it being resolved.

Back in chambers over the recess, Rumpole lays out his theory to Mr Pinhorn and O'Higgins: the mouse was no accident but a deliberate sabotage, timed to wreck the restaurant's reputation and value just before the divorce settlement, but not by Simone, who stands to profit from a prosperous restaurant, not a ruined one. The beneficiary of ruining it would be someone who wanted Simone to get as little as possible out of the business. Gaston Leblanc, it turns out, has a sideline conjuring act producing "livestock out of hats," and was paid to slip the mouse onto the tray; Alberto passed the dish on to the unsuspecting table. Rumpole directly accuses O'Higgins himself of having arranged it out of spite toward his ex-wife, prompting a flustered denial, at which point cashier Mary Skelton, overheard nearby, gives herself away: it was her own doing, out of love for O'Higgins and a wish to see Simone denied her share, and Rumpole has Pinhorn take a statement from her to call as a witness. Meanwhile, back at Rumpole's house, Liz Probert, who has called round to collect something she left behind, runs into Hilda, who has returned to fetch her own belongings, and tells her, with some exaggeration, how heartbroken and lonely Rumpole seemed at breakfast without her; Hilda is visibly moved by the report.

In his closing speech, Rumpole tells the jury that Mary Skelton, acting out of love rather than malice toward the restaurant itself, arranged for the mouse to be served in order to sabotage Simone's divorce settlement, and that O'Higgins himself knew nothing of the plot. Judge Graves interjects that on the admitted facts O'Higgins is guilty in law regardless of motive or knowledge, and threatens to direct the jury accordingly; Rumpole retorts that no British judge has the power to direct a British jury to convict, and that the jury's task is not law but justice and their own consciences. The verdict itself is not shown, but the aftermath in the court corridor plays as a vindication: O'Higgins calls Rumpole brilliant, and a relieved Erskine Brown thanks him for the cross-examination, learning that the business about Phillida's brief was not invented on the spot but genuinely arranged by Rumpole beforehand. Back in the robing room, Ballard, still fuming about the nail brush and vowing to chain up its replacement, discovers a mysterious blackened brush has turned up in his own QC's locker: Rumpole's doing, left unexplained and unapologized for. That evening, Hilda is home again, having sent Cousin Everard back to Saskatoon; she and Rumpole reconcile, with Hilda admitting she is glad to have been told how much she was missed, and the two return to La Maison Jean-Pierre, where George serves them steak and kidney pudding "on the house" (or, as Rumpole puts it, on the mouse), closing the episode on a note of domestic truce.
