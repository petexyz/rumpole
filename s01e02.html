Rumpole of the Bailey

S01E02: Rumpole and the Alternative Society

1970 case: hippie commune schoolteacher on a cannabis charge; an entrapment defence; Rumpole is drawn to her lifestyle.

Monday, 10 April 1978 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 10 April 1978 (Series 1), directed by Herbert Wise, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).

Horace Rumpole takes the train down to the Western Circuit, happily escaping the Old Bailey, his clerk, and "the lady wife, she who must be obeyed," for a few days' "working holiday." In a comic restaurant-car exchange he discovers that the circuit's old gastronomic glories (brown Windsor soup, Chateau Great Western) have shrunk to grapefruit segments and fried-egg-and-burger, and arrives at Coldsands station to be met by the local instructing solicitor, Mr. Friendly, of Friendly, Sanderson and Friendly. Rumpole's brief is Kathy Trelawney, a young woman living at Nirvana, 34 Balaclava Road -- one of the "lotus eaters," as he calls the commune's residents -- charged with possession of a suitcase of cannabis resin. He declines to visit the house itself, preferring to see Kathy at his hotel that evening, and reveals he isn't even staying at the circuit's usual haunt, the George, but with old wartime friends who keep a pub called the Crooked Billet.

At the Crooked Billet, Rumpole is reunited with Bobby and her husband Sam, a former bomber pilot he served with in the RAF. The reunion is warm and a little wistful: Bobby confides that the doctor has told Sam, whose drinking and heart are both in poor shape, to get out of the licensing trade and onto soft drinks, something Sam refuses even to discuss; Rumpole is drawn in as an arbiter but deflects the question back to her. Sam, delighted but uncomprehending that his old friend is defending one of "that lot" from Balaclava Road, needles Rumpole good-naturedly about defending a client he assumes must be guilty; Rumpole insists, as a matter of principle, that he doesn't know she's guilty and won't assume it -- he currently believes she may be the victim of a police trap.

That night at Nirvana, Kathy's housemate Dave Hawkins is hostile to the idea of a lawyer at all, reminding Kathy pointedly that she hasn't forgotten "where Pete is tonight" -- an allusion, not yet explained, to her brother. Kathy meets Rumpole anyway, brought to the pub's snug by Friendly, who nervously advises her to answer only Rumpole's questions and nothing more. Rumpole, needling Friendly, deduces at once from the undercover officer's own slang-laden patter that the man who infiltrated Nirvana, "Jack the hippie," is Detective Sergeant Jack Smedley of the local drug squad in disguise. His plan is to argue, as a preliminary point before the jury is even sworn, that Smedley's evidence should be ruled inadmissible as having been obtained by trickery -- the testimony of an "agent provocateur" who wormed his way into the house to manufacture a crime rather than detect one. He is counting on the trial judge, James Crispin Rice -- known on the circuit as "Rice Crispies" or "the Duke" -- a product of the navy and a minor public school instilled early with a horror of sneaks, to share his distaste for police trickery.

Afterward, in the pub bar, Rumpole introduces Kathy to Sam and Bobby; Sam's blustering, flirtatious banter (he assumes any girl from the commune must drink and sleep around) bounces off Kathy's calm self-possession. Walking her to the bus along the seafront, Rumpole gets his first real glimpse of her: she quotes Wordsworth from memory ("It is a beauteous evening, calm and free"), says the household reads poetry together before bed, and, unprompted, tells him that her brother Pete is locked up in Turkey, serving twelve years on a drugs charge -- a boy who could never sleep with his bedroom door shut. Back at the pub that night, a mellow, half-regretful Rumpole tells Bobby that if he had ever exercised "a free and independent choice" he might have spoken up for her at a wartime NAAFI dance instead of marrying Hilda, whom he was all but assigned to as head of chambers' daughter; Bobby's only reply is that if you never say anything, nobody ever knows.

The hearing opens the next morning with prosecuting counsel Vernon Tuck, bluff and class-conscious about "rent-a-hippie" squatters, and the judge probing Smedley's elaborate disguise -- beads, embroidered jeans, an Afghan goatskin coat, and a drooping moustache he has since shaved off after mockery at the station (Rumpole likens it, to the judge's bewilderment, to Eva Perón's). Rumpole presses Smedley on what, if anything, led police to suspect Kathy before he ever drove out to Balaclava Road in his "Viva Zapata"; Smedley can offer only vague "information" that she was "the type to get involved." The neighbour, Miss Tigwell, is then called to describe the "perverted," mixed-race, mixed-sex household she has been watching from her own "Crow's Nest," and the dreadful-looking, long-haired, bead-wearing man she once saw pass Kathy a wad of ten-pound notes -- a description that, to the court's amusement, turns out to be an exact match for Detective Sergeant Smedley himself, undermining the Crown's case still further. The judge, visibly troubled that the whole episode may be a police-manufactured crime rather than a detected one, signals he might exclude the evidence outright if no independent suspicion against Kathy can be shown; proceedings are adjourned overnight.

That evening Rumpole turns down the judge's circuit dinner and goes instead to Nirvana itself for a meal with Kathy and Dave, telling Kathy, half in earnest, that he'd rather live among the "lotus eaters" of Balaclava Road on scutter cushions and Andean flute music than go back to Hilda and the Old Bailey, and that when her trouble is over the two of them should "drop out together." Dave presses him to use the trial to make a political point about archaic drug laws and to help "get through to them" on Pete's behalf; Rumpole flatly refuses -- he is there to keep Kathy out of prison, not to turn the dock into a platform. The evening ends, as before, with Wordsworth read aloud over wine, Kathy again handing Rumpole the book to read the same poem from the seafront.

Back in court the next day, the legal argument is going well for the defence -- the judge all but concedes that if the drugs came to Kathy only because of Smedley's trap, with no prior involvement, the case collapses entirely -- when, during the lunch recess, Dave insists the "truth" has to come out "for Pete's case." Over Rumpole's objections the full picture emerges: Kathy had acquired the cannabis in Istanbul long before Smedley ever appeared, intending to sell it to raise the roughly ten thousand pounds needed for lawyers and bribes to free Pete, and hoping in the process to draw attention to his twelve-year sentence. This admission is fatal to the entrapment argument Rumpole has just built -- Smedley tricked her into a sale, perhaps, but not into possession, and not into the underlying intent to deal.

Rumpole is now caught by his own professional code: once a client has told him outright, in "clear crystal-ringing tones," that she did what she is charged with, the etiquette of the bar forbids him from continuing to run a defence he knows to be false, however sympathetic the cause. He announces he is retiring from the case; Dave accuses him of cowardice and hypocrisy, and Kathy, hurt and bitter, tells him he cares for nothing but his "book about legal etiquette" -- that the poetry and the warmth of the last two evenings meant nothing. Alone with him, she asks whether he could simply forget what she's told him; he says he could, and would, gladly, if he weren't a barrister. In the end it is Kathy herself who asks him to stay on, on the only terms left: a guilty plea, in exchange for whatever leniency Rumpole can privately secure. Rumpole, visibly moved, agrees to try.

The next morning, over coffee in the judge's room after the circuit's dinner (which Rumpole again missed), he sounds out "the Duke" about a suspended sentence for a girl of good character who "knows a great deal about Wordsworth" -- the judge is unmoved, lecturing him that cannabis is a Class B drug under the Dangerous Drugs Act regardless of anyone's literary taste, and bluntly asks whether Rumpole has become personally "involved" in the case; Rumpole denies it. In court, Kathy pleads guilty; the judge, unpersuaded by Rumpole's mitigation and pointedly noting that if she had really wanted to help her brother she should have gone to the Foreign Office or the Red Cross rather than commit "serious crime," sentences her to three years. Even Smedley, watching her taken down, remarks that it "seems a shame, a girl like that"; Rumpole, uncharacteristically subdued, calls Holloway prison "an evil place" and tells Tuck only that "to prison she had to go." The episode closes back at the Crooked Billet, with Sam in high spirits over a round of drinks and the whole pub breaking into a boisterous, repetitive singalong of "Roll Out the Barrel" -- Rumpole toasting "the good old Duke" and insisting, not entirely convincingly, that he too is happy.
