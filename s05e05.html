Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 5, Episode 5

Rumpole and Portia

A scrap dealer is accused of selling arms to terrorists; Phyllida presides as a newly appointed recorder.

Wednesday, 21 December 1988 · ITV (Thames Television)

In particular several passages involving Boxy Horne's colonial reminiscences and some of the Culp family's dialogue are badly mangled, and a handful of exchanges are merged under a single speaker tag; attributions and sense below are reconstructed from context rather than a confident speaker reading where that happens. This episode was originally broadcast 21 December 1988 (Series 5), directed by Roger Bamford, written by John Mortimer.

The episode opens on two parallel domestic mornings. At the Erskine Browns' house, Claude bickers with his wife Phillida (now a QC, newly made "Portia" by her chambers colleagues) over sending their son Tristan off to the boarding school Bogstead, insisting the character-building misery he himself endured there (breaking ice in the basin, running laps on Saints' Day) is somehow a gift, while Phillida wonders pointedly why he assumes their daughter Isolde would not enjoy the same "treat" if girls were admitted. At the Culps' shop, meanwhile, a very different household is broken up: antiques dealer Stanley Culp, who has raised his twelve-year-old son Matthew alone since his wife left him years ago for the much older manager of a Tesco's, is taken into custody, and Matthew into council care, after police and Special Branch officers raid the shop. Rumpole, visiting Stanley in the cells, hears his account: a man calling himself "Mr. Banks," claiming to represent a charity for Northern Irish terrorism victims and wearing distinctive gold-rimmed glasses with tinted lenses, paid Culp 500 pounds to store some heavy packing cases, which turned out to contain firearms destined for a loyalist paramilitary buyer named McRobert, who was shot dead by police the moment he arrived to collect them, taking with him the only other witness who might have identified "Banks."

At a West Middlesex magistrates' court in a separate, much lighter case, Phillida successfully defends the movie star Cy Stratten against a minor cannabis possession charge, winning him a 300-pound fine rather than prison with a speech praising his opposition to hard drugs and his need for "mercy." Stratten, smitten, later lures Phillida to a park picnic and makes her a lucrative, thinly romantic business proposition: come run the entire legal side of Cy Stratten Enterprises from a house on the beach in California, children and husband welcome to tag along if useful. Phillida, flattered and genuinely tempted by the idea of a life outside the law's grind, does not say no outright, and the ensuing tension between the Erskine Browns escalates when Phillida, back home, admits to Claude that there is "someone" she might want to spend more time with, in a different sort of life, just as a letter arrives from the Lord Chancellor's office. It turns out, deflatingly, to concern not Claude's suspicions but Phillida's own appointment: she has been made a Recorder, to sit occasionally as a criminal judge at the Old Bailey, a promotion Sam Ballard QC announces to chambers with pointed, backhanded remarks about the Lord Chancellor's eagerness to promote women.

Chambers life is further disrupted when a Special Branch superintendent, Rodney, calling on Ballard, is struck on the ankle in the clerks' room by a golf ball driven by the elderly, briefless Uncle Tom, who has taken to practicing his swing indoors for lack of anything better to do. Ballard, furious, orders Uncle Tom to vacate his room, and Rumpole, defending the old man's right to bring some color to "a dusty, dreary little clerk's office," threatens to resign from chambers in solidarity if Uncle Tom is forced out. The crisis is compounded at home, where Hilda's visiting second cousin, the blustering ex-colonial "Boxy" Horne, has overstayed his welcome with endless reminiscences of Kenyan coffee plantations, tiger shoots, and a brass box containing, among his worldly goods, an old photograph of a young and "alluring" Hilda, stirring a wave of wistful what-might-have-been nostalgia in both Hilda and, more sardonically, Rumpole, and inspiring Uncle Tom, newly smitten with tales of Darkest Africa, to actually hand in his resignation from the Bar and announce plans to go adventuring himself.

Rumpole, scheming to get Stanley Culp's gun-running trial listed before a sympathetic judge, enlists his clerk Henry (currently rehearsing a Noel Coward play for the Bexley Thespians opposite Miss Osgood of the Old Bailey List Office, his usual amateur-dramatics co-star and, not coincidentally, the person who actually assigns cases to judges) to nudge the Culp case onto newly minted Recorder Phillida Erskine Brown's list, hoping "Portia" will bring some maternal sympathy to the proceedings. The plan works a little too well: Phillida does preside, but Sam Ballard QC turns up to prosecute, and the trial becomes a genuine contest rather than a formality. Rumpole's defense is that the mysterious "Mr. Banks" was in fact a Special Branch officer running an entrapment operation against McRobert, using Culp's shop as an unwitting storage depot for a sting, and that it was Banks himself, not some outside loyalist sympathizer, who was seen getting out of the police car and walking away unquestioned at the moment of Stanley's arrest. Superintendent Rodney, under Rumpole's pointed cross-examination, repeatedly declines to confirm or deny Banks's identity or involvement, citing the secrecy of Special Branch operations, until Phillida, in her first substantive ruling from the bench, sides with Rumpole and allows the questioning to continue, over Ballard's objection that the proceedings touch "the security of the realm."

The case's emotional core turns on young Matthew Culp, called as a witness to confirm that the man who called himself Mr. Banks at the shop is the same man he later saw getting out of the police car on the day of the raid. Ballard's cross-examination of the boy is pointed and faintly brutal for a child witness: he presses Matthew on his devotion to his father and his evident wish to help him, needling him until Matthew's confident identification softens into "I think it was the same man... he looked the same," a concession that visibly damages the defense's strongest piece of evidence. Phillida, uncomfortable at the spectacle, cuts the cross-examination short and excuses the boy, who leaves the witness box asking his father, heartbreakingly, "Did I let you down, Dad?"

Away from court, the episode's domestic threads resolve in quieter, more deflating ways. Cy Stratten, dropping by chambers looking for Phillida, is charmed rather than put off by Uncle Tom's eccentric golfing and even suggests to an exasperated Ballard that the "old guy playing golf in reception" is exactly the kind of quaint, traditional detail that will draw American lawyers' business, a pitch that quietly undercuts Ballard's case for throwing Uncle Tom out. At the Rumpoles' house, Boxy Horne vanishes just as abruptly as he arrived, having first asked Hilda for a thousand pounds to start a battery-hen smallholding (a request she refused), and it emerges he has probably never travelled further than Bognor, his whole African saga almost certainly invented wholesale from Rider Haggard novels. Uncle Tom, deprived of his inspiration and his prospective traveling companion, quietly does not go to Zimbabwe after all, and Rumpole wryly notes that he himself "lost that case against Ballard" regarding Uncle Tom's room, even as the practical threat of eviction seems to fade along with Boxy's stories.

In court, the jury convicts Stanley Culp of distributing prohibited weapons. In a plea for mitigation, Rumpole makes his most impassioned point of the episode: whatever Culp's guilt, Matthew Culp is entirely innocent, a devoted, hardworking boy with no mother in his life, and any prison sentence imposed on his father is in effect a sentence of years in care imposed on the son as well, cutting him off from the only family he has ever known. Phillida, visibly moved and acknowledging Rumpole's eloquence, nonetheless concludes that she must protect society from a man who dealt, however unwittingly entangled, in weapons that might have left children orphaned in Northern Ireland, and sentences Culp to three years' imprisonment: her judicial duty overriding her personal sympathy, and Matthew's fate settled exactly as Rumpole warned.

That evening at Pommeroy's Wine Bar, a shaken Phillida apologizes to Rumpole for the outcome, admitting she hates the part of her new role that requires condemning people, deciding their fates, sending them "downstairs." Rumpole, genuinely moved, tells her she did her job "so bloody fairly" that his own client was convicted anyway, like anyone else caught in life's traps, and confesses his relief that his own profession only ever requires him to defend, never to judge, "take him down" being a phrase he has, happily, never had to use himself. Pressed about what will happen to her own son now, Phillida reveals that the question of Tristan's schooling has resolved itself without either parent's intervention: rather than being sent away against his will, Tristan, finding home life with a father perpetually lost in opera recordings and a mother perpetually buried in briefs more lonely than any boarding school could be, has decided for himself that he wants to go to Bogstead after all. The episode closes at the school gates, where Tristan cheerfully bids his parents goodbye, more interested in the promised fried bread on Sundays than in any tearful farewell, "passing judgment on himself," as Phillida wryly puts it, far more decisively than any court ever could.
