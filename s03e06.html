Rumpole of the Bailey

S03E06: Rumpole and the Last Resort

A mobile-home park owner is charged with fraud; Rumpole fakes his own death to smoke out a former solicitor.

The episode opens with the arrest of Frank Armstrong, chairman and managing director of Free-as-Air Holidays (also trading as Sanson Mobile Homes), on suspicion of fraud -- a good number of his advertised holiday caravans, it seems, are either nonexistent or have a habit of mysteriously vanishing. In parallel, Rumpole's own finances are revealed to be in dire shape: cashing a cheque at the United Metropolitan Bank, he's cornered by the manager, Mr Medway, over an overdraft Rumpole blithely dismisses as "peanuts" compared to his outstanding fees -- fees that include a particularly stubborn debt owed since 1972 or 1973 by the solicitors' firm of Blythe, Winterbottom and Paisley, whose senior partner, Perivale Blythe, has perfected the art of always having "just slipped out of the office" whenever chambers calls to collect. Back at chambers, Sam Ballard pointedly introduces Rumpole to young Archie Featherstone -- yet another Featherstone nephew angling for a seat -- while gently suggesting Rumpole can't stay in his room forever; meanwhile Rumpole's own umbrella stand, a treasured heirloom from Hilda's father C.H. Wystan, has mysteriously disappeared, a minor mystery he pursues with comic indignation.

Henry confirms the precise scale of Blythe's debt -- £2,765.93, dating all the way back to an indecency case in Swansea -- and reveals that Blythe has form for this kind of stonewalling, having once talked a solicitor's grieving widow, Mrs Brushwood, into accepting a fraction of her late husband's outstanding fees by frightening her with the prospect of a long, costly dispute over the estate. A new brief duly arrives for Rumpole: the Armstrong fraud case, instructed, inevitably, through the very same elusive Mr Blythe. Fiona Allways, assigned to dig through Free-as-Air Holidays' tangled accounts, finds herself stonewalled in exactly the same way by Blythe's firm, which has never returned a single letter or call since Armstrong's arrest. Determined to force the issue, Rumpole and Henry hire a private investigator, the cheerfully named "Fig" Newton, to track Blythe down and serve him with a witness subpoena in the Armstrong case -- partly for the defense, and partly, Rumpole makes no secret of it, to finally extract payment of the debt.

At home, a stack of red final-demand bills -- gas, electricity, water, telephone -- prompts Hilda to lecture Rumpole on getting Blythe to pay up before announcing she's off to the Lake District with Dodo Macintosh and an old school friend, the recently widowed Pansy Rawlins, leaving Rumpole, pointedly, to "find this Blythe" while she's away. Fig Newton's inquiries turn up little beyond snapshots of Blythe's secretary dancing with her boyfriend rather than minding the office. At the Old Bailey, meanwhile, Rumpole explains to Frank Armstrong exactly how his once-thriving fruit-barrow-inherited business went wrong: Armstrong's estranged brother Frederick, who went into the computer trade rather than sticking with the family's hardware shop, supplied and installed the very office computer system now blamed for the firm's chaos of double-booked, nonexistent, and vanishing mobile homes -- and had recently tried, and failed, to buy Frank's Cornish holiday site, secretly knowing (though never telling his brother) that it sat on a valuable, unprospected tin seam.

Before Judge Bullingham -- the short-fused "mad bull" of the Old Bailey -- Rumpole makes application after application for an adjournment to locate the vanished Blythe, whose evidence he insists is essential, while cross-examining prosecution witnesses about the grim reality behind Free-as-Air's glossy brochures: a Cornish site that looks, in the jury's photographs, like "next door to an industrial tip" rather than anything resembling a holiday paradise, and fifty separate instances of deposits taken for mobile homes that turned out not to exist. By the ninth refused adjournment, Bullingham, thoroughly out of patience and quoting Hamlet's "law's delays" back at Rumpole as a reason to get on with it rather than to pause, flatly refuses to grant any further postponement -- at which point Rumpole collapses dramatically in open court, apparently suffering a stroke, and is carried out, the case adjourned not on legal grounds at all but because of his sudden medical collapse.

Word spreads through chambers that Rumpole has died, triggered by a deliberately ambiguous telemessage -- "Deeply regret, Rumpole gone up to a higher tribunal, signed Rumpole" -- that everyone assumes must actually have been sent by a grieving Hilda. What follows is a blackly comic scramble dressed up as mourning: Ballard wonders aloud whether anyone still wants Rumpole's old umbrella stand (Erskine Brown, who regularly entertains solicitors with their own umbrellas, modestly puts in a claim), Uncle Tom muses that Rumpole at least "went in harness," and the chambers meeting pivots almost immediately to redistributing his caseload and -- more cynically still -- repositioning chambers toward lucrative, image-polishing obscenity and indecency prosecutions, with a room earmarked for young Archie Featherstone as a useful prosecutor-nephew-in-waiting, all nominally "in view of the sad event."

In fact, Rumpole is very much alive and resting, somewhat theatrically, at home, coached through his supposed convalescence by a visiting Fiona Allways and, crucially, in on the whole scheme with Hilda. When Perivale Blythe himself turns up at the house to pay solemn condolences -- and, true to form, to float a reduced-settlement arrangement on the overdue fees in the name of resolving the "estate" quickly and painlessly, exactly as he once talked round the widow Brushwood -- Hilda turns the tables with relish. Invoking her father's old friendship with this year's president of the Law Society as leverage, she bluntly threatens to have Blythe struck off unless he writes a cheque, on the spot, for the full £2,765.93, confiscates any thought of a hasty exit through the window, and then has Fig Newton step out of hiding to serve him with his witness subpoena for the Armstrong trial after all.

With the Sanson Mobile Homes case restored to Judge Bullingham's list, Rumpole makes a triumphant return to court just moments after Bullingham -- unaware the "death" was ever anything but genuine -- has delivered a surprisingly warm, rambling eulogy to the jury about his old courtroom "friend" and "reliable old soldier of the line," Horace Rumpole. Rumpole's reappearance ("the report was greatly exaggerated, my lord") leaves Bullingham and his clerk Mason thoroughly flummoxed, but the judge, perhaps embarrassed into leniency, allows the trial to proceed exactly as Rumpole wants. Calling Blythe to the stand, Rumpole extracts the truth behind the fraud: Frederick Armstrong, denied the chance to buy his brother's tin-rich land, had been sabotaging Frank's computer system all along to manufacture the appearance of incompetence and fraud and force a sale -- a revelation capped by the dramatic disclosure that Frederick was arrested that very morning for breaking into the office overnight to reprogram the computers again, and now faces his own charge of perverting the course of justice. Frank Armstrong walks free.

Afterward, Ballard confronts Rumpole in the corridor over the "unnecessary grief" his faked death caused chambers -- not least because Ballard had already quietly begun reletting Rumpole's room to young Archie Featherstone -- while Rumpole, unrepentant, needles him with a pointed misquotation about mourners whose shoes were barely worn out before they moved on to self-interest. Hilda cheerfully confirms her full complicity in the ruse, producing Blythe's cheque from her handbag as proof it was, in her words, "the only way Rumpole could get his fees from that appalling Mr Blythe" -- and notes dryly that he's never managed to die properly in the middle of a case yet, "popping back like a bloody opera singer" every time chambers thinks they've seen the last of him.

The episode closes at Pomeroy's wine bar, where Rumpole, thoroughly delighted with himself, proposes a toast to his own "positively last reappearance," needling the assembled chambers members -- who had, within the space of a single afternoon, divided up his room, his umbrella stand, and his clients -- over a round of the bar's indifferent house claret. Raising his glass to "the great fraternity of the bar," Rumpole toasts his own survival and the whole episode's foundational irony: that it took the mere appearance of death, rather than any amount of honest asking, to finally shake a decade-old debt loose from a dishonest solicitor.

Originally broadcast 15 November 1983 (Series 3), directed by Stuart Burge, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).
