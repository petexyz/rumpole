Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 2, Episode 5

Rumpole and the Course of True Love

An English teacher is found guilty of seducing a pupil; Phyllida's pregnancy and marriage to Erskine-Brown; Featherstone's judgeship pressure.

Aired Tuesday, 26 June 1979 · ITV (Thames Television)

The episode opens inside a school classroom rehearsal of Romeo and Juliet, with schoolmaster Mr. Ransom playing Romeo opposite a pupil, Francesca Capstick, as Juliet, a scene that bleeds disturbingly into reality when the Headmaster produces a bundle of Ransom's own love letters to Francesca, confirming an evening together at the Festival Hall, a Vivaldi concert, and dinner at an Italian restaurant, though Ransom insists "we didn't go to bed together." The Headmaster, unconvinced, warns he will have to report the matter to the proper authorities, and that the outcome will depend heavily on what Francesca herself says. Rumpole takes the brief, and in chambers that morning runs into Guthrie Featherstone, who is avoiding Marigold at breakfast after confessing an affair with a temp from the clerks' room; Marigold has threatened divorce unless Guthrie secures a judgeship, so Guthrie is plotting to play golf with the influential Mr. Justice Vosper and a Lord Chancellor's-office contact named Keith that very Saturday, a piece of transparent careerism Rumpole finds mildly scandalous even by his own relaxed standards.

In the clerks' room, Rumpole airily muses to Erskine Brown that love has been wildly overrated by the poets, only to be reminded, pointedly, that Erskine Brown and Phillida Trant's engagement has just ended. Rumpole's own new client, Ransom, admits that Francesca told the Headmaster intimacy took place at a friend's empty house in Hampstead after the concert, and, against Rumpole's advice, insists on pleading guilty outright rather than let Francesca be cross-examined, since "she told the old boy I did it." Rumpole talks him out of an unembroidered guilty plea with blunt warnings about prison conditions for "S.O.s" (sexual offenders) and the realities of "cocoaing," but Ransom still balks at the idea of exposing Francesca to hostile questioning in open court. The case gets a strange lift, in Rumpole's eyes, when the solicitor reveals the trial judge will be none other than Rumpole's old chambers friend George Frobisher, newly elevated to the circuit bench: "fate has spun the wheel and handed you the jackpot," Rumpole tells his startled client.

At a café, Phillida Trant, sick, pale, and short with Rumpole, confides that she is pregnant by Erskine Brown and has no intention of telling him, fearing he'll insist on marriage and domesticity just as her career as a barrister ("so much keener on being barristers than barristers are") is taking off; she happens also to be prosecuting Rumpole's own carnal-knowledge case the following week, before Judge Frobisher. In the judge's chambers before trial, Rumpole and Phillida try, half-seriously, to get George to indicate leniency given Ransom's decision to spare Francesca cross-examination, trading Romeo-and-Juliet references (George recalling, inaccurately, "wasn't she locked up in a tomb?"), but George, scrupulously fair and newly conscious of judicial propriety, refuses to rule anything out, leaving Rumpole furious and Ransom facing real prison time. Needled past his tolerance by George's new self-importance (and his own client's selfless scruples), Rumpole decides to fight the case properly after all, provided Ransom confirms he never actually slept with Francesca at the other alleged locations, which Ransom does, giving Rumpole grounds to cross-examine her hard and, as he puts it, "teach old George a lesson."

A subplot runs alongside: on the golf course, Mr. Justice Vosper lectures Guthrie Featherstone that judicial appointments hinge less on legal brilliance than on "complete respectability" and a scandal-free chambers, pointedly dismissing "that fellow Rumpole" and a cautionary tale about a QC whose chambers was riddled with divorces and pupil affairs, who never got near the High Court bench. Guthrie, rattled, deliberately loses the round. Back in chambers, he summons Erskine Brown "as a matter of urgency," ostensibly over "respectability," and, via a roundabout account of gossip passed between Marigold and Hilda Rumpole at Bar Choral Society rehearsals, blurts out that Phillida is pregnant, assuming Erskine Brown already knows. Erskine Brown, hearing the news for the first time from Featherstone rather than from Phillida herself, is overjoyed rather than alarmed, much to Guthrie's bewilderment, since Guthrie's real point (that an unmarried pregnant tenant reflects badly on his bid for the bench) gets lost entirely in Erskine Brown's delight.

In court, Ransom's trial proceeds with the Headmaster's evidence of Francesca's complaint admitted over Rumpole's objection (to George's visible discomfort at having to rule against his old friend), and the solicitor's own son, Martin, a schoolmate of Francesca's, recruited by Rumpole as an informal source, helps Rumpole build a devastating cross-examination. Rumpole establishes that Francesca kept copies of her own love letters to Ransom (the first, quoting Juliet's pledge of "all my fortunes," predating anything from Ransom) and that she handed the whole correspondence to a classmate, Charles "Chas" Moesby, for "safekeeping," the same Moesby who loathed poetry, loathed Ransom for reporting his insolence and recommending he "continue his education elsewhere," and who, it emerges, was Francesca's real boyfriend throughout. Rumpole walks Francesca through a camping trip to France where she swapped tents to spend the night with Moesby, and establishes that the entire epistolary seduction of Ransom was Moesby's idea: a deliberate scheme to entrap and discredit the teacher who had humiliated him.

The cross-examination's final twist lands hard: Francesca admits that she only actually slept with Ransom after discovering Moesby was secretly seeing her friend Mary Pennington, not out of love, but out of fury, using Ransom's attraction to her as a form of petty revenge, and finding the experience itself distinctly unromantic ("he kept on spouting poetry"). Rumpole has, in effect, proven the entire prosecution case was built on a vindictive schoolyard plot rather than any genuine corruption of an innocent girl by a predatory teacher, a defence George Frobisher cannot simply wave away, even as he visibly struggles with it. Outside court, Erskine Brown finally catches up with Phillida to celebrate the pregnancy news she never meant him to have, over her exasperated protest that Rumpole "can't keep anything" to himself; Erskine Brown, having evidently been reading parenting manuals, offers to take on feeds and let her keep working in court, a practical olive branch Phillida receives with surprised approval rather than her earlier dread of domestic confinement.

Despite the strength of Rumpole's cross-examination, George Frobisher convicts Ransom anyway, sentencing him, with evident personal reluctance but a stated sense of judicial duty, to the minimum he feels the law allows: two years' imprisonment, on the grounds that Ransom "used his position, and indeed the poetry he was employed to teach," to corrupt a young girl's morals, whatever her own faults. Rumpole, furious, gets his client out of the courtroom immediately rather than let him linger.

The episode closes not on the courtroom but at a wedding reception: Erskine Brown and Phillida Trant have married after all, despite her earlier refusal, and Rumpole moves through the celebration quoting Shakespeare's closing line from Romeo and Juliet ("there never was a story of more woe...") about a very different pair of star-crossed lovers than the one being toasted. Marigold Featherstone, moved by the wedding, has apparently forgiven Guthrie's affair unconditionally and proposes the two of them treat the occasion as a fresh start on their own marriage; Guthrie, delighted but still anxious about his judgeship, mentions that Mr. Justice Vosper himself will be dining with them that very evening. George Frobisher seeks Rumpole out at the reception, visibly troubled by the sentence he felt bound to pass and asking whether Rumpole, or Ransom, blames him personally for it. Rumpole, his earlier fury spent, tells George not to blame himself at all: "blame life, blame love, blame youth, blame Shakespeare, blame the law," adding, with rueful self-awareness, that Ransom likely hates Rumpole himself even more than he hates the judge who took away his liberty, since it was Rumpole's cross-examination that had cast him, in the end, not as Romeo but as the fool.
