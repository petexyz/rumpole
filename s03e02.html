Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 3, Episode 2

Rumpole and the Golden Thread

Rumpole defends a government minister accused of murdering a bishop in the African state of Neranga; acquittal, then his political ruin.

Aired Tuesday, 18 October 1983 · ITV (Thames Television)

At home doing the washing-up, Rumpole recites Flecker's "Golden Road to Samarkand" to himself, chafing at the dullness of his recent practice (three weeks on an unpaid VAT case over plastic egg timers in Sydenham) while Hilda needles him about gravy left on the plates and announces, with more enthusiasm than he shares, that her old school friend Dodo Macintosh is coming to stay. The mood changes when the phone rings: it is Justicia International, a human-rights legal charity, asking Rumpole to take on the defence of David Mazenze, a name he recognises at once: a former pupil of his from his crammer days, now caught up in a capital case overseas. In the fictional African republic of Naranga, we see why: Mazenze, the country's Minister for Home Affairs, has just been refused bail by Chief Justice Banzana on a charge of murdering Bishop Kariri, and pointedly asks whether any of the "experienced Naranga counsel" on offer are not in the Prime Minister's pocket.

At Justicia International's office, Miss Pinkerton fills in the politics for Rumpole: Naranga, once the British colony of "New Somerset," is riven between the Apu and Matatu tribes, whose mutual loathing runs deep enough that intermarriage between them is cursed by both families. Mazenze, an Apu moderate who bore the British no grudge even after a decade in a colonial jail, was made a token cabinet minister by the Matatu Prime Minister, Dr Christopher Mabili, a Jesuit-and-Moscow-educated Marxist nicknamed "Dr Death" for his enthusiasm for the death penalty. A cable from Mazenze's younger brother Jonathan confirms that Rumpole, specifically, has been cleared to fly out and take the brief; Miss Pinkerton is blunt that the Prime Minister "can't wait to hang David" and that Rumpole has to save his life. Back in chambers, Rumpole lords this exotic assignment over a sceptical Henry, declaiming Flecker all the way out the door while Henry and the clerks privately fret about getting him back from "central Africa" in time for an unrelated, much more mundane Old Bailey murder (a Guinness-bottle killing in a Kilburn pub) already fixed for the 29th.

In Naranga, customs officials give Rumpole the cold colonial once-over before he's met by the prosecuting Attorney General, Rupert Taboro, all smiles and old-boy bonhomie ("we learned friends have got to stick together"), and by his instructing solicitor, Freddie Rwingo. On the drive in, Rwingo explains that Mazenze deliberately asked for an unglamorous British lawyer rather than a big QC, as a demonstration of faith in ordinary, incorruptible British justice. At police headquarters, the sardonically hospitable Superintendent Akimbo of the Special Branch (fond of mentioning that he once trained in Croydon) leads Rumpole down to see his old pupil in a cell. Mazenze, cheerful even under sentence of death, recalls Rumpole fondly from his student days and the two reminisce in a very English register (P.G. Wodehouse, the Cotswolds) before getting down to the case: Mazenze admits he quarrelled publicly with Bishop Kariri outside Parliament and was overheard threatening to kill him, but insists he spent the evening of the murder driving around alone composing a speech on Apu-Matatu unity, then came home to his wife Grace for coffee and music: a thin, uncorroborated alibi. Worse, Naranga abolished jury trials for murder on the British model, leaving the verdict to Chief Justice Banzana, whom Mazenze dryly calls "Dr Death's chicken." Rumpole is delighted to learn Mazenze still remembers, verbatim, the "golden thread" speech on the presumption of innocence that Rumpole taught him years ago: better that ten guilty men go free than one innocent man be convicted.

At a reception that evening, Rumpole meets Mazenze's devoted wife Grace, who insists nothing can harm her husband because "David's one of the immortals," and Mazenze's younger brother Jonathan, who flatters Rumpole's reputation for pulling judges' legs and then, more alarmingly, offers to simply produce as many alibi witnesses as Rumpole wants: an offer Rumpole firmly refuses, insisting he wants only a witness who will tell the truth. Jonathan warns that David's real insurance policy isn't legal: ten thousand armed Apu fighters are hidden in the bush, ready to rescue him by force if he's convicted. Later, at the Hotel Majestic bar, the British High Commissioner Sir Arthur Rumnant (affectedly nicknamed "Mr Old England" among the diplomatic corps) treats the whole capital trial as colourful local theatre, relishing the "zest" the death penalty adds to a case. The next morning, Taboro visits Rumpole's hotel room bearing a gift hamper and a pointed warning: the cheering crowd of Jonathan's Apu People's Party supporters outside may not actually want Rumpole to win the case at all.

At trial, Chief Justice Banzana runs a markedly less deferential court than Rumpole is used to: there's no jury to address, procedural customs differ, and the judge repeatedly needles Rumpole about his famous former pupil-master, C.H. Wystan (Hilda's father), while briskly overruling his objections. The prosecution's first witness, Mazenze's secretary Magnus Nagoma, confirms the heated argument and the words "I will kill you" addressed to the Bishop, though under cross-examination he admits he can't say whether the threat was meant seriously or was just angry rhetoric over a stalled religious-education bill. A second witness, the Reverend Kenneth Kwazango, claims he identified Mazenze through his car windscreen as the attacker fled the scene, until Rumpole produces a meteorological report showing torrential rain during the exact window of the shooting, casting real doubt on how clearly anyone could have been seen through a rain-streaked windscreen at night; Banzana reserves judgment on how much weight to give the identification rather than ruling it out.

That night, Jonathan Mazenze reveals a secret: there is a witness who could give David a solid alibi, but David would never allow her to be called, because she is a Matatu woman: David's secret second wife, married to him in a tribal ceremony he has hidden even from his own brother, since exposure would bring shame and ostracism from both families under the Apu-Matatu taboo on intermarriage. Rumpole, unmoved by the politics of it, decides that the truth matters more than David's wishes. Freddie Rwingo then lets slip the real reason Rumpole was brought out to Naranga at all: the plan was never to win. David's own faction wanted Rumpole to deliver a moving "golden thread" speech on British justice for the foreign press, lose with dignity, and thereby hand the Apu rebels in the bush a clean pretext to rise up. A conviction, not an acquittal, was the desired outcome, with Banzana and Dr Mabili as the intended targets of the resulting uprising. Rumpole is furious at having been used as a prop for martyrdom and insists he fights every case to win, because he knows no other way to try one.

Rumpole forces the issue in court over David's own violent objection (Mazenze shouts "I object" from the dock and is warned he'll be removed if he doesn't stay silent) and calls Mabel Mazenze, the secret Matatu wife, as his final witness. She testifies that she and David were married by tribal custom exactly one year earlier, on the 8th of March, and that he spent the anniversary evening with her (from before nine o'clock until a quarter past eleven, the entire window of the murder) before going home to sleep beside Grace, since he had a big speech the next day. Taboro, to Rumpole's visible frustration, declines to cross-examine her at all. In his closing argument, delivered directly to the judge rather than to any jury, Rumpole invokes the "golden thread" of the presumption of innocence as Britain's most lasting legacy, greater even than its breakfasts or its verse, and asks how any court could be certain beyond reasonable doubt that Mabel Mazenze has not told the plain truth. Chief Justice Banzana (disapproving of the secret bigamous marriage on moral grounds but unwilling to depart from the "golden thread" principle Naranga inherited from British law) acquits David Mazenze and orders him discharged.

Taboro congratulates Rumpole on the win but immediately points out its political cost: broadcasting to the world that the Apu people's own leader secretly married a Matatu woman is, whatever its legal merits, a serious blow to Mazenze's standing with his own tribe. That night, Superintendent Akimbo turns up at Rumpole's hotel room to "arrest" him over an intercepted cable about a "murder fixed for the 29th... Henry": a scare that resolves into farce once it's clear the cable is about the unconnected Kilburn murder case waiting for Rumpole back at the Old Bailey, not a coded plot. Dr Mabili himself appears, cheerfully congratulating Rumpole on a "first-rate show" and all but admitting he wanted this outcome: the Apu fighters, Sir Arthur explains, won't rally behind a leader who married a Matatu woman in secret, so the rebellion Mazenze's own faction had been counting on will not come. Rumpole asks Sir Arthur, almost as an afterthought, who actually did kill the Bishop; the question is waved off with colonial indifference. A closing news report delivers the final, bitter twist: David Mazenze has been found murdered, apparently killed by a faction of his own Apu People's Party now led by his brother Jonathan, with no arrests made. The episode ends back in London, Rumpole home among the ordinary business of entertaining the still-visiting Dodo Macintosh at cards, telling Hilda (who asks whether he's off to "Samarkand" again) that no, he's off to chambers; Samarkand, this time, is definitely off.
