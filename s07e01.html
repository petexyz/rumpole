Rumpole of the Bailey

S07E01: Rumpole and the Children of the Devil

A well-meaning social worker takes custody of a young Timson girl after claims of devil worship in the family.

29 October 1992 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 29 October 1992 (Series 7), directed by James Cellan Jones, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).

The episode opens on a brief flash-forward at the Savoy Hotel -- Rumpole muttering "Tracy Timson, whoever possessed you?" -- before cutting back to the real start of the story at the Rumpoles' house. At breakfast, Hilda ("She Who Must Be Obeyed") produces an invitation to the Scales of Justice Ball, to be held at the Savoy on her birthday, and needles Rumpole about the pirouetting judges and waltzing barristers who will be there, including Claude Erskine-Brown and his wife Phillida ("Portia"), who, Hilda pointedly notes, "dance rather well together." Hilda asks, not quite lightly, why Rumpole never leads her onto a dance floor any more, and recalls dreaming of exactly that happening at this ball. Rumpole deflects with a joke about having no time to practice "down the Old Bailey," and Hilda sends him off to work with a barb that he'd rather defend a murderer than dance with his wife -- planting a domestic subplot that will run under the whole episode.

Meanwhile, a social worker named Mirabelle Jones calls on the Malloy family on behalf of Crockthorpe local authority's social services, questioning young Dominic Malloy -- with his mother Peggy hovering -- about a frightening game he once played with his friend Tracy Timson. Dominic describes being led, after dark, to a churchyard, where a grown man wearing an orange-and-black devil's mask told the children they were to be "the devil's children" and made them dance around him. Jones presses Dominic to identify the man, and he blurts out, "Tracy's dead" -- a line the episode leaves hanging rather than explaining outright. That same night, Jones arrives at the Timsons' house with a court order and removes eight-year-old Tracy Timson from her parents, Cary and Rose, over their furious objections, refusing even to let the child bring a doll for comfort.

Cary Timson -- a minor, familiar member of the vast Timson clan of South London villains who have kept Rumpole in "gainful employment for many years" -- comes to Rumpole's chambers to retain him for the Family Proceedings Court hearing into Tracy's care. Rumpole learns that social services suspect devil worship in the Timson household, triggered by the children's masked ritual in the churchyard. Cary and his wife insist they know nothing about Satanism; Rumpole, skeptical of the whole theory, presses Cary on what he assumed the police visit was actually about -- it turns out Cary has an unrelated history of police suspicion over a burglary at a shop on Gunter Avenue, for which he was questioned but not charged, and for which he suspects a rival family, the Malloys, blame him for informing on their relative Gareth Malloy.

In a separate interview, Jones sits Tracy down with a set of family dolls, walks her through "Mum," "Dad," and "Grandma and Grandpa" in a way that edges toward suggestion, then produces a picture of a devil's face and asks whether it reminds Tracy of her father. Back in chambers, Rumpole meets the new typist, Dot Clapton, hired by senior clerk Henry after the previous typist, Diane, abruptly married a junior clerk from a bankruptcy set; and runs into his former pupil Liz Probert, now a working barrister, who turns out to be briefed for Crockthorpe local authority -- against Rumpole -- in the very same Timson care case, on the theory that the family is "deeply into" devil worship. Rumpole needles her with the question of whether she herself believes in the devil, and jokes that he is content to be "on the side of the devil" if the devil has all the best lines.

A second chambers subplot takes hold: Claude Erskine-Brown introduces Charlie Whizbeach, a well-connected young barrister currently in commercial chambers, who wants to move in and share Rumpole's room -- a room with a view over the Temple churchyard that both men claim overlooks either Dr. Johnson's or Oliver Goldsmith's grave, a running bet neither will let drop. Rumpole is openly hostile to being squeezed out of his own room in his old age, especially once he learns Whizbeach has already been shown around it in his absence. Head of chambers Sam Ballard QC, glimpsed attending a service of his "Lawyers as Christians" group at Temple Church, is scandalized when Erskine-Brown tells him -- misleadingly -- that Rumpole has been entertaining actual devil worshippers in his room (meaning only the Timson parents, in for a conference about Tracy's case). Ballard confronts Rumpole directly, quoting Milton's Satan at him and hinting that Rumpole's tenancy in chambers, and his room, could be brought to an end in Whizbeach's favor; Rumpole refuses to be moved, mocking Ballard's credulity and suggesting he look up an "unfrocked bishop" in the Yellow Pages for an exorcism instead.

Unbeknownst to Hilda, Rumpole has also been placing furtive lunchtime calls to a Miss Isadora Fern, arranging to meet her "at the usual place" -- apparently a dance studio, where Fern, a former Southern Counties ballroom champion, is giving him secret lessons. Hilda and her friend Dodo Macintosh happen to spot Rumpole coming out of Knightsbridge station looking pleased with himself, and Dodo is convinced he is "up to no good." Hilda, suspecting an affair, resolves not to confront him immediately but to choose her moment -- "exactly when it suits me... when he least expects it" -- a thread that runs in parallel with the courtroom story for the rest of the episode.

At the Family Proceedings Court, the bench -- presided over by an unnamed Chairwoman -- watches a video of Jones's interviews with Dominic and Tracy. Rumpole objects repeatedly to Jones's leading questions and to the hearsay nature of a child's recorded statement that was never tested by cross-examination, but is firmly reminded by the Chairwoman, and by Liz Probert, that the Family Proceedings Court is not bound by the strict rules of evidence that govern the Old Bailey. Cross-examining Jones directly, Rumpole gets her to concede that she never investigated the long-standing feud between the Timson and Malloy families -- rooted in Gareth Malloy's belief that Cary Timson had informed on him over the Tobler Road supermarket robbery -- and suggests that Dominic Malloy was coached by his family into falsely implicating the Timsons as devil worshippers out of revenge, not because anything supernatural had actually happened.

Outside court, Rumpole leans on the Timson family's code of silence to find out where the devil masks actually came from, eventually appealing to Cary's love for his daughter over "honour among thieves." This breaks the case open: another family member, Dennis Timson, admits he and an accomplice burgled Wedge's Carnival Novelty Shop on Gunter Avenue, stashed boxes of stolen goods -- including a stock of cheap devil masks -- in Cary's garage without telling him, and that Tracy had simply found the masks there and used them in a game with her friends. Dennis also explains, when recalled as a witness, exactly how the rumor reached the Malloys and then the authorities: a chain of gossip running from Peggy Malloy to Barry Pilgrim's wife to Dennis's own wife Doreen at the pub, confirming that Gareth Malloy's resentment over the supermarket robbery was the real motive behind Dominic's accusations. The Chairwoman admits this hearsay too, under the relaxed family-court rules, and with the devil-worship theory in ruins, Tracy is returned to her parents.

Back in chambers, the Whizbeach subplot resolves in Rumpole's favor through a bit of engineered mischief: having overheard Whizbeach celebrating his presumed new tenancy with champagne and taunting Rumpole yet again about Dr. Johnson's tomb, Rumpole telephones Ballard -- still working late -- to tip him off that "something absolutely appalling" appears to be going on in the Temple churchyard outside Rumpole's window. Ballard goes to look, catching Whizbeach's drunken antics there firsthand; whatever he sees is evidently disqualifying, since Ballard later tells Erskine-Brown, cryptically, that Whizbeach's arrangement with chambers has come "unstuck," and that Rumpole's room, and tenancy, are safe after all.

That evening at home, Rumpole arrives in high spirits, having freed Tracy Timson and picked up a prospective new brief defending Dennis Timson over the novelty-shop burglary. Hilda, bracing herself, asks if there isn't something he ought to tell her, clearly expecting a confession about his mysterious lunchtime absences; Rumpole, playing along, "pleads guilty" to spending a small fortune out of "temporary madness" -- then produces two tickets to the Scales of Justice Ball as a birthday surprise. The episode closes at the Savoy, where Ballard confirms to Erskine-Brown that Whizbeach will not be joining chambers after all, and where Rumpole -- to Hilda's visible delight -- leads her onto the dance floor and performs a confident chassé, revealing that the secret lessons with Isadora Fern were for her birthday all along. Hilda, unsurprised, admits she knew about the lessons the whole time, and calls her husband, fondly, "an old devil."
