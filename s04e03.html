Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 4, Episode 3

Rumpole and the Official Secret

A civil servant is prosecuted under the Official Secrets Act; a colleague confesses to staging the leak and takes his own life.

Aired Monday, 2 February 1987 · ITV (Thames Television)

Over dinner, Rumpole reads Hilda an absurd news item about the Ministry of Defence's extravagant spending on tea and biscuits for civil servants, enough, by his reckoning, to have paid for the Crimean War three times over, while Hilda presses, as she periodically does throughout the episode, for a serving hatch to be cut through to the kitchen. At the Ministry itself, the "biscuits" story turns out to have a real victim: Rosemary Tuttle, an amiable, eccentric economist in the civil service, is confronted by her superior Mr Thorakley with a glove found by the photocopier and then handed over to Detective Inspector Fellows and Detective Sergeant Hamley, who are investigating a leak of classified information to a gossip column, "Chatterbox," in the Sunday Fortress. Thorakley insists that a breach of the Official Secrets Act is a breach regardless of subject matter; more senior officials, Sir Frank Fawcett and Oliver Bowling, are inclined to find the whole business a bit pathetic, but the papers go to the Attorney General regardless. In the background of this same meeting, almost in passing, the Ministry also discusses something called "Operation Blueberry," evidently a far more sensitive NATO matter: a detail that will turn out to matter a great deal more than the biscuits.

Oliver Bowling, nicknamed "Batty" at school, where he knew Claude Erskine Brown as "Colly," rings his old schoolfriend for legal help for "an old girl" in a spot of bother, and Erskine Brown, sensing a harmless, theatrical case, recommends Rumpole. Erskine Brown promptly puts Rumpole in his debt in a less flattering way: he asks Rumpole to tell Phillida, if she should ever ask, that the two of them went together to the opera (Die Meistersinger) on a particular date, an alibi, it emerges, for an evening Erskine Brown actually wants to spend squiring his and Rumpole's new pupil, Liz Probert, whom he has been pursuing with a mix of self-pitying complaints about his wife's long working hours and clumsy compliments about her eyes. Liz, unimpressed and uninterested, later blows up the whole scheme by telling Phillida directly that Erskine Brown took her, not Rumpole, to Covent Garden, leaving Erskine Brown enduring a silent, furious breakfast table and owing Rumpole yet another favour.

The Tuttle brief arrives in chambers inside a locked ministry security box, with Henry refusing to say the combination aloud "in public" and instead whispering it so indiscreetly that Rumpole cracks it instantly, a running joke about official paranoia that recurs when Rumpole idly muses about tipping off "Mr Chatterbox" himself about the Ministry's own absurd secrecy. At home, the Rumpoles' telephone develops a mysterious hiccuping noise on the line; Hilda, taking a call in the kitchen rather than in front of her husband, cites her own entitlement to a few secrets, and shortly afterward the household phone appears to pick up a stray, very different conversation: a classic dead-drop arrangement, complete with a park bench near the bridge off the Mall, a package wrapped in a newspaper, and a 2 p.m. deadline, hinting that Rumpole's domestic phone troubles (really just an unpaid bill) have become entangled, at least in the audience's ear, with the Ministry's much larger and still-unexplained leak.

In conference, Rumpole meets Rosemary Tuttle properly, with his pupil Liz Probert taking notes, and rehearses a grand civil-liberties defence built on Section 2 of the Official Secrets Act being "the raving of governmental paranoia," offering, in effect, to turn Tuttle into a Joan of Arc of Whitehall if she will simply admit to the leak as an act of principled defiance. Tuttle, however, insists flatly that she did not do it and cannot explain how her glove came to be by the photocopier; faced with her plain determination to tell the truth rather than accept a heroic fiction, Rumpole drops the rhetoric and agrees they will actually test the evidence, starting with a typewriter comparison.

Sam Ballard QC, freshly elected to the Sheridan Club, turns out to be prosecuting, and invites Rumpole to a club lunch that is really a soft pitch for a guilty plea, made rather less soft by the presence of the Attorney General himself, who treats the case as the tip of a much larger iceberg, jokes broadly about the anti-nuclear protest camp at the American air base at Molesworth, and persistently mishears Rumpole's name as "Molesworth." The case's seriousness is confirmed when it is listed to be tried, unusually, before the Lord Chief Justice in person. Meanwhile Detective Inspector Fellows drops a pointed, faintly menacing warning to Rumpole about keeping a close eye on his "acquaintances," naming a suspected IRA sympathiser, Seamus O'Rourke, a name that means nothing at all to Rumpole, or, when he asks her that night, to Hilda either.

At trial, Ballard tries to have Liz Probert barred from assisting in court once he learns, to his evident dismay, that she is not, as he had assumed, a clergyman's daughter but the daughter of the left-wing council leader "Red Ron" Probert, supposedly unsuitable for a "sensitive" national-security case; the Lord Chief Justice and Rumpole both brush the objection aside. Character evidence from Tuttle's boss, Oliver Bowling, establishes her as almost comically punctual and reliable, a regular outdoor-lunch-in-the-park eater who could be set by, which will matter once the park-bench detail resurfaces. Cross-examining the journalist who received the leaked notes under his pen name "Mr Chatterbox" (in reality a Mr Warboys), Rumpole draws out that Warboys never actually met or clearly saw Tuttle at the park bench exchange, never confirmed her handwriting, and is giving evidence against her largely to avoid his own two-year exposure for receiving secret material. A document examiner, Mr Royce Williams, confirms that the leaked notes and a sample later typed by Tuttle at Scotland Yard came from the same machine and the same standard-issue government paper, but, pressed by Rumpole on the differing weight and evenness of the keystrokes between the two, concedes that they could plausibly have been typed by two different people on that same shared machine.

The case takes a darker turn when Sir Frank Fawcett, testifying about Tuttle's background (her Austrian immigrant parents, her education, her full security vetting), discloses an unconfirmed report that she was once seen at a peaceful demonstration at Molesworth, which she denied, after which she was quietly passed over for promotion and placed under "special surveillance" that proved, in this very case, "somewhat ineffective." Pressing further, Rumpole gets Fawcett to admit that the figures actually leaked to the press about catering costs were not even accurate: the Ministry, if anything, spends more than was reported, and, more significantly, that a far more serious and still-unresolved leak concerning Operation Blueberry has recently come out of the same department, with no charges yet brought against anyone over it. Rumpole begins to suspect that the trivial, easily-mocked "biscuits" prosecution of an eccentric, defenceless spinster has been engineered (planted glove, ambiguous typing samples, a wholly invented Molesworth sighting) precisely so that if Tuttle ever says anything true and important, nobody will believe a word of it.

Using his fresh debt to Erskine Brown (and a Foreign Office contact's loan of the royal box at Covent Garden) to get backstage during a performance of Tosca, Rumpole corners Oliver Bowling directly during the interval and lays out his theory to his face: that Bowling himself set Tuttle up, using a showily literary quotation lifted from Congreve (the very habit of literary one-upmanship Erskine Brown remembers from their schooldays) in the anonymous note to "Mr Chatterbox," and inventing the Molesworth sighting to make Tuttle look like an unreliable, gossiping old busybody well in advance of whatever real information might one day need discrediting. Bowling, visibly rattled, refuses either to confirm or deny it, telling Rumpole only that his own loyalty is to his client: "a question of loyalty." Shortly afterward, a small paragraph in the Times reports a "regrettable accident" at Covent Garden Tube station; Ballard, separately, reports that the Attorney General is now offering no further evidence against Tuttle, on the convenient grounds that the leaked catering figures were "apparently inaccurate" anyway, and Tuttle is discharged without a stain on her character. Rumpole, far from triumphant, tells Fawcett bluntly that stupid, unnecessary secrets lead to death. The episode closes at home, where the long-threatened disconnection of the Rumpoles' phone finally happens, removing, Rumpole notes with weary relief, both the eavesdroppers and the followers from his life, while the "intruder" Hilda had warned him about turns out only to be Mr O'Rourke, hired at last to cut the long-debated hatch through to the kitchen.
