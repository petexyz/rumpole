Rumpole of the Bailey

S07E02: Rumpole and the Miscarriage of Justice

Rumpole defends a police officer charged with falsifying a confession.

Thursday, 5 November 1992 · ITV (Thames Television)

Originally broadcast 5 November 1992 (Series 7), directed by Robert Tronson, written by John Mortimer, per Wikipedia's "List of Rumpole of the Bailey episodes" (checked 2026-11-23).

The episode opens with the aftermath of a murder trial: a young man known as "Pinhead" Morgan has confessed, under questioning by Detective Superintendent Roy Gannon, to stabbing PC Ted Yeomans to death, and Gannon has personally promised the widowed Betty Yeomans a conviction. Mr Justice Featherstone, presiding, duly sentences Morgan to life imprisonment with a recommended minimum of twenty-five years, declaring from the bench that "every minute this trial has lasted has made me surer and surer of your guilt" -- a remark that will come back to haunt him. Rumpole, who argued the defense at trial, meets Featherstone outside court afterward and needles him good-humoredly about securing a conviction on wafer-thin evidence -- no blood on the accused, no weapon traced to him, no witness placing him at the scene -- calling it a feat of "legal genius" to make a confession do all the work.

Rumpole takes the case to the Court of Appeal, where he cross-examines Detective Inspector Peplow and Detective Sergeant Chesney Lane about the physical mechanics of Gannon's handwritten confession. Using a forensic "machine" that reads indentations left on paper, Rumpole demonstrates that the confession's incriminating second page -- the one containing the words "I came tooled up, I cut the copper" -- was written on top of a stack of blank statement forms taken from Gannon's own office, suggesting it was composed separately and substituted in later rather than written at the time, on a single sheet, as the officers had sworn. "There is something that stinks to high heaven about this Morgan confession," Rumpole concludes. At home, a comic domestic aside has Hilda discovering Rumpole bought mincemeat instead of minced meat for her shepherd's pie, while the radio or papers report that bishops and the Arts Council are calling for the Morgan case to be reopened -- the first sign of the public pressure building around the appeal.

At the Sheridan Club, Mr Justice Paeslow warns his old friend Featherstone, gently but pointedly, that his unwisely confident sentencing remarks have left him exposed: the Home Secretary has been questioned about the case in the House, and the Court of Appeal -- about to rule -- may find the conviction unsafe. Sure enough, in a formal Court of Appeal judgment, an unnamed Lord Justice quashes Morgan's conviction, criticizing Superintendent Gannon for obtaining "this worthless confession" and openly rebuking Featherstone's "unwise" courtroom remark. The fallout lands hardest on Betty Yeomans, who is left bewildered -- if Morgan did not kill her husband, who did? -- and on Gannon himself, who confesses to Betty that he has lost interest in whether Morgan was guilty and now fears he, not Morgan, will be made to "suffer" for the collapse of the case.

A parallel, lighter plot follows Featherstone's increasingly undignified efforts to cope with professional humiliation while his wife Marigold is away visiting her sister in Coventry. Wandering alone at night, he runs into chambers clerk Henry and a group of amateur actors from chambers -- "the cream of the Bexleyheath thespians" -- returning from a theatre outing to see Diana Rigg, and lets himself be talked into a drink at a disco in Leicester Square, where he ends up dancing (having "a bit of a bop," in the slang Henry and Dot Clapton have to explain to him) with Dot, the new chambers typist. Puffed up afterward, Featherstone boasts to Rumpole and Claude Erskine-Brown over lunch at the Sheridan Club -- Erskine-Brown having just been made a member -- about a supposed romantic conquest with a much younger woman, holding forth on how many "girls" these days prefer an older man "in every sense of the word." Erskine-Brown, noticing that Dot received mysterious red roses from a "judicial admirer" that same morning, draws his own conclusions and cannot resist repeating the story around chambers.

With the Morgan conviction overturned, suspicion turns to how the confession was faked, and Superintendent Gannon himself is charged with forging it. Rumpole, briefed by solicitor Mr Bernard, takes on Gannon's defense. Betty Yeomans, giving Rumpole an unplanned lift in the minicab she now drives for a living, loyally insists Gannon would never lie to her -- but lets slip that her late husband Ted's old school friend, Detective Sergeant Chesney Lane, and Lane's wife Doreen have avoided the family since Ted's death, and that Ted once distinguished Gannon as reliably "clean as a whistle," implying someone else in the force was not. Bernard separately briefs Rumpole on an older case, that of Superintendent Pertwee, hounded out of the force and convicted of perverting the course of justice after apparently falling foul of a senior officer -- a pattern Rumpole suspects is about to repeat itself with Gannon, and which points toward Chief Superintendent Belmont.

Rumpole meets Lane privately in a park, where Lane -- torn between protecting Gannon, who has been good to the Yeomans family, and his own conscience -- admits he has lost sleep over the case but hesitates to come forward, caught between Doreen's advice to keep his head down and his memory of Ted as an honest, if unambitious, uniformed officer. Meanwhile the Featherstone subplot escalates: Erskine-Brown, hearing yet more detail about the judge's night out from an acquaintance at his bridge club, decides it would be unfair to let Marigold remain "deceived," and tells her everything over lunch. The resulting row follows the Featherstones home and even interrupts a recess in Featherstone's own courtroom, where Guthrie pleads, half-joking and half-desperate, "Marigold, darling, I appeal to you" -- only for her to reply, "I'm sorry, Guthrie. You've lost your appeal."

At Gannon's trial, Rumpole dismantles the case against his client piece by piece. Under cross-examination, Chief Superintendent Belmont admits he secretly removed blank statement forms from Gannon's office while Gannon was away and ran the indentation test without telling him -- conduct Rumpole frames as corrupt rather than merely cautious, accusing Belmont to his face of being the actual source of the rot: "at the top, with you, sir!" Rumpole then produces a photostat of the confession's second page, retrieved by Lane from Belmont's own files, showing that someone had deliberately traced over every letter with a sharp point -- manufacturing false indentations to make the page look forged after the fact. Lane, finally persuaded to testify fully despite Doreen's misgivings, admits that Peplow threatened Morgan with being handed over to Yeomans's police friends "to be done over" if he didn't confess, and explains that he held on to the incriminating photostat because it looked like an attempt to frame Gannon. Gannon is acquitted, in a markedly quieter courtroom than the one that cheered Morgan's release -- a contrast Gannon himself wryly notes to Rumpole on the court steps.

Rumpole, still turning over the Featherstone affair, tracks down Dot in the street and asks her directly whether anything actually happened between her and the judge on the night of the dance; she laughs outright at the idea and denies it flatly. That evening, over drinks at the Rumpoles' house with Erskine-Brown (amid a running joke about an exaggerated golf score), Rumpole lays out his theory explicitly: Featherstone's boastful "confession" of a conquest was no more reliable than Morgan's confession to murder or the forged evidence used against Gannon -- the self-serving invention of an unhappy, lonely man trying to impress his friends at the club, not a truth to be trusted just because it was spoken aloud. Erskine-Brown remains skeptical that Guthrie would simply lie about such a thing. The episode closes at the Featherstones' house, where Marigold, still cool but relenting, allows Guthrie back into the house -- with a final, pointed instruction never to boast about his exploits at the Sheridan Club again.
