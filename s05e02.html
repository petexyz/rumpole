Rumpole of the Bailey · Season 5, Episode 2

Rumpole and the Barrow Boy

Nigel Timson, one of the few legitimate Timsons, is defended on an insider-trading charge.

Aired Wednesday, 30 November 1988 · ITV (Thames Television)

The episode opens at a City wine bar where young stockbroker Nigel Timson is being toasted on his birthday by colleagues, including Rosie Jayfett (daughter of his firm's chairman, and his live-in girlfriend) when Detective Inspector Arbuthnot and Detective Sergeant Rainer of the Fraud Squad arrive to arrest him for offences under the Companies Act. In the cells, Fred Timson, head of the Timson clan, asks Rumpole to take the case as a family favour: Nigel is a cousin by marriage who broke with Timson tradition by going straight and working his way up from office boy to broker at the City firm of Jayfett Jarroway, and Fred is anxious that the one upwardly mobile Timson not end up disgracing the family after all. Rumpole, admitting fraud is not his natural territory, nonetheless takes the brief. Meanwhile, on television, Nigel's prospective father-in-law, the pompous Sir Christopher Jayfett, gives a City-affairs interview lamenting the decline of gentlemanly standards in the Square Mile and blaming the new wave of "barrow boys" and "spivs" who have supposedly brought insider dealing into a profession that once ran on a gentleman's word alone, plainly, if obliquely, aiming his remarks at people exactly like Nigel.

At home, Hilda needles Rumpole relentlessly about money and status: she has bought a small shareholding in the newly privatised British Gas and has taken to lecturing him about North Sea gas ownership, and more pointedly presses him on why he has never taken silk when Phillida Erskine Brown, a mere contemporary, is already a QC. Rumpole brushes this off, but the seed has been planted. In chambers, Nigel explains his case to Rumpole in painstaking, patient City jargon: a week before Worldwide Groceries Incorporated launched a "dawn raid" takeover bid for the small firm Cornucopia Preserves and Jams, Nigel bought 68,000 pounds of Cornucopia shares on behalf of a client, a Miss Mabel Globe, whom he says he has never actually met: she telephoned out of the blue, said she had a legacy to invest, and gave only a post office box number in Harrogate, Yorkshire. When the takeover sent the shares soaring, Miss Globe's holding doubled in value, and shortly afterward an anonymous benefactor paid 20,000 pounds into Nigel's own bank account: money Nigel insists must have been Miss Globe's unprompted gratitude, though he never told her, or anyone, which bank he used. The knowledge of the impending takeover, he explains, originated in Jayfett Jarroway's corporate finance department, safely on the other side of a "Chinese wall" from Nigel's own department, a wall Sir Christopher Jayfett, the firm's chairman, is said to police zealously.

Rumpole and his instructing solicitor, Mr. Bernard, set out to trace the elusive Miss Mabel Globe, with Bernard eventually recalling a phone number he once used to return her call: a pub, the Old Yorkshire Grey in Harrogate, whose landlord has never heard of a Mabel Globe but recalls an elderly regular, a "Mrs. Prescott," who used the pub telephone each morning to ring a stockbroker and chat about shares over a Guinness, and who was said once to have been a nursemaid to a smart city family. Fred Timson mobilises the family's web of Yorkshire cousins to help run the lead down, while back at chambers a parallel, much smaller scandal is brewing: Head of Chambers Sam Ballard QC has lost a 50-pound fee for a breathalyser opinion and all but accuses clerk Henry of pocketing it, threatening him with dismissal and possible prosecution unless he can account for the missing cheque, over objections from Uncle Tom and the rest of chambers. Ballard also still holds Claude Erskine Brown's Kitten Agogo Club photograph over him, threatening to blackball his application to the Sheridan Club on grounds that members must be "like Caesar's wife, above suspicion," even though Erskine Brown was fully exonerated of any wrongdoing in that earlier libel affair.

A despondent Henry, convinced his career and marriage are both finished (his wife, newly elected to the local council and absorbed in endless civic committees, leaves him to eat cheese on toast alone most nights), confides to Rumpole over drinks at Pommeroy's that he has been dreaming of a new life with the chambers' typist Diane, running the entertainment at a hotel in Australia's Dandenong Mountains, complete with a nostalgia cabaret act of wartime songs learned from his father. When Rumpole returns home that night the worse for drink, he finds Hilda away staying with her old school friend Dodo Mackintosh, ostensibly to let him "think things over," and Dodo reveals, to Rumpole's horror, that Hilda has written an unauthorized letter to the Lord Chancellor's office formally applying for Rumpole to be made a Queen's Counsel, on the theory that a man about to take silk could hardly go on being seen out at his working men's club.

At the Old Bailey, prosecuting counsel Mr. Bellercard opens the Crown's case against Nigel, laying out the Cornucopia share purchase, the doubled profit, and the mysterious 20,000-pound payment as clear evidence of guilt, and dwelling pointedly on the gulf between Nigel's working-class, Shepherd's Bush upbringing and the gentlemanly traditions of the City he has supposedly betrayed. Under Rumpole's cross-examination, witness Hugo Schillingford (present at the birthday party and busy juggling champagne glasses at the crucial moment) concedes he may simply not have heard Nigel mention that the Cornucopia shares were being bought for "a little old lady in Harrogate," and is pressed into admitting that there have been previous, unresolved suspicions of insider dealing inside Jayfett Jarroway's own corporate finance department, well before the Cornucopia affair. Rumpole also draws out of Nigel himself, mid-trial, a flash of recognition: the name "Mabel Globe" suddenly reminds him of "Nanny Prescott," the Jayfett family's childhood nurse, whose first name, it turns out, really was Mabel.

Sir Christopher Jayfett takes the stand and, under Rumpole's questioning, concedes readily (almost proudly) that he does not want his daughter Rosie marrying into the Timson family, that he regards Nigel as a "barrow boy" unworthy of her despite years of hard work, and that his earlier television remarks about City crime were indeed partly aimed at men like him; the judge, visibly uncomfortable at the direction of the cross-examination, repeatedly asks where Rumpole is going with it, and Rumpole insists he is going, simply, toward the truth. Rumpole then springs the trap: he produces a photograph confirming that "Nanny Prescott" and the long-vanished "Mabel Globe" are one and the same woman, and (having traced the anonymous 20,000-pound payment to an account at the Trident World Archipelago Bank in the Cayman Islands, a bank Sir Christopher is forced to admit he personally uses) accuses Sir Christopher directly of orchestrating the whole insider trade himself through his old nanny under her maiden name, pocketing most of the proceeds, framing Nigel by funnelling him the smaller sum, and doing it all specifically to cover up his own earlier insider dealing scandal at the firm by pinning the blame on a convenient, low-born scapegoat. Sir Christopher calls the suggestion "absolutely outrageous" and the judge adjourns the cross-examination overnight for Rumpole to gather supporting evidence, but the next court day brings news that Sir Christopher has simply fled the country, sending the Fraud Squad's case against Nigel collapsing for want of its chief witness. Rosie, rather than feeling vindicated for Nigel, blames him and the whole Timson clan for her father's disgrace and flight, and storms off calling him a "barrow boy" to his face.

Interwoven with the main case, Rumpole's own small comedies play out to deflating, ironic ends. At the Sheridan Club, a civil servant named Keith from the Lord Chancellor's office takes Rumpole aside to explain, as gently as such news can be delivered, that silk is simply out of the question for a man of Rumpole's age and type of practice (the "good old non-commissioned officers of the Bailey" are needed where they are), and that Hilda's unsolicited letter caused the Lord Chancellor some embarrassment on the Woolsack. Ballard, meanwhile, buys Rumpole a drink at the same club and lets slip, almost by accident, that he has just found the "missing" 50-pound cheque sitting forgotten in his own wallet all along, meaning Henry was never at fault, and must now face a different sort of disappointment: his dreamed-of escape to Australia, cabaret act and all, evaporates along with the scandal that was going to force it.

Back in chambers, Rumpole delivers the good news to Henry that he has been completely cleared, only to find Henry devastated rather than relieved, since clearing his name also strands him permanently in Bexleyheath, "married to a chair" as his wife's civic career leaves him more and more alone, with no pretext left for the new life in the Dandenong Mountains he had allowed himself to imagine. That evening at home, Rumpole breaks the silk question to Hilda gently by way of a half-truth: he reports that "Keith" spoke at length about her late father, C. H. Wystan, who was apparently on the point of being made a QC himself when he died, and that there is some unwritten rule against a family having two Queen's Counsel competing for the same honour, sparing Hilda the blunter truth that the Lord Chancellor's office considers Rumpole unsuitable for silk on his own merits. Hilda, moved and a little mollified, accepts this without further argument, and the episode closes with the two of them settling in front of the television, where a City-affairs bulletin reports that Sir Christopher Jayfett's sudden disappearance from the Old Bailey insider-dealing trial has rattled the London markets, news Rumpole receives with evident satisfaction and a contented, Macbeth-quoting assurance that, whatever else, Rumpole himself shall sleep well tonight.
